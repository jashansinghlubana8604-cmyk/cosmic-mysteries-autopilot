In 1671, the astronomer Giovanni Cassini discovered a new moon of Saturn. But something about it puzzled him. When it was on one side of Saturn, he could see it easily. When it swung around to the other side, it almost vanished.

> i: iapetus | v: saturn moons
Cassini made a brilliant guess. The moon, he suggested, always kept the same face towards Saturn, and one half of it must be dark, the other half bright. More than three hundred years later, spacecraft proved him right. [pause] One side of this moon is as dark as coal. The other is as bright as fresh snow.

> i: iapetus ridge | v: iapetus
And that was not even its strangest feature. In 2004, the spacecraft named after Cassini flew past and saw a ridge of mountains running almost perfectly along the moon's equator, rising up to around twenty kilometres high. [pause] As if someone had built a wall around its middle.

## Black And White
mood: mystery
> i: iapetus | v: iapetus
Iapetus is Saturn's third largest moon, about fifteen hundred kilometres across. It orbits far from Saturn, more than three and a half million kilometres away, taking seventy nine days to complete each orbit, and turning once in the same time, always showing the same face to the planet.

> i: iapetus cassini regio | v: iapetus
Its leading side, the half that faces forward as it travels around Saturn, is covered in a dark, reddish-brown material that reflects only a few percent of the sunlight that hits it. This dark region is called {{Cassini Regio|CASSINI REGIO}}. The trailing side is mostly bright ice, many times more reflective.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus rotation
The boundary between them is surprisingly sharp in places. Dark material fills crater floors, while the bright, icy walls of the same craters gleam. [pause] It looks like a moon that has been dipped in ink.

> i: iapetus | v: saturn moons
Inside, Iapetus seems to be mostly ice. Its density is only slightly higher than that of water. If you could find an ocean big enough, a moon made almost entirely of frozen water would very nearly float in it. [pause] Its dark coat may be only a thin skin over a world of ice.

## Dust From Another Moon
mood: mystery
> i: phoebe | v: saturn moons
Where does the dark material come from? The answer involves another moon, much further out, called {{Phoebe|PHOEBE}}. It orbits Saturn backwards and is dark itself, probably a captured object from the outer solar system.

> i: phoebe ring | v: saturn
In 2009, NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope discovered a gigantic, faint ring of dust around Saturn, far beyond the main rings, in Phoebe's orbit. Small impacts on Phoebe seem to chip off dark dust, which slowly spirals inward towards Saturn.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus
Iapetus orbits in the path of that inward-drifting dust. As it travels around Saturn, its leading side sweeps up the dark material, like the front of a car collecting insects on a highway. [pause] That explains why the dark side faces forward.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus rotation
But dust alone cannot explain the sharp contrast. Scientists think a second process makes it much stronger. Dark surfaces absorb more sunlight and warm up. On the darkened side, ice slowly evaporates and moves to the colder, brighter side, where it freezes again. The dark side gets darker, and the bright side brighter. [pause] A runaway process, painting the moon in black and white.

## The Great Ridge
mood: dread
> i: iapetus ridge | v: iapetus
When Cassini flew past Iapetus at the very end of 2004, it revealed something nobody had expected. A long, narrow ridge running along the equator, more than a thousand kilometres long, with peaks rising perhaps twenty kilometres high in places. That would be more than twice the height of Mount Everest.

> i: iapetus ridge | v: iapetus
The ridge runs so neatly along the equator that it gives Iapetus the shape of a walnut. No other moon in the solar system has anything quite like it. [pause] And scientists still do not agree on how it formed.

> i: iapetus ridge | v: iapetus
For a moon this small, the ridge is enormous. On a world about fifteen hundred kilometres wide, mountains twenty kilometres tall are like a ridge several kilometres high running around a small island. [pause] If the Earth had a ridge of the same proportion, it would tower far above the cruising height of airliners.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus
One idea is that Iapetus once spun much faster, and its spinning made the moon bulge at the equator. As its interior cooled and hardened, that bulge was frozen in place while the spin slowed down. Indeed, Iapetus is noticeably flattened, as if it remembers spinning once every sixteen hours or so. [pause] A fossil of its younger self.

> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
Another idea is even more dramatic. Perhaps Iapetus once had a ring of its own, or even a small moon, that broke apart and fell onto its equator, piling up into a line of mountains. [pause] A collapsed ring, frozen into a wall of rock and ice.

## A View Like No Other
mood: wonder
> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
Most of Saturn's large moons orbit in the plane of its rings. From their surfaces, the rings would look like a thin line slicing across the sky. But Iapetus's orbit is tilted, by about fifteen degrees.

> i: saturn from moon | v: saturn rings
So from Iapetus, you would see Saturn's rings from above or below, spread out in all their glory, like a giant jewelled disc hanging in a black sky. [pause] It may be the most spectacular view of Saturn anywhere in the solar system.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus
Arthur C. Clarke seems to have thought so. In his novel 2001: A Space Odyssey, the mysterious alien gateway, the Star Gate, is found on Iapetus. In the film, it was moved to Jupiter, because the special effects team found Saturn's rings too difficult to show convincingly. [pause] Clarke later said that when he saw Iapetus's strange markings, it seemed almost as if someone had put up a sign.

## Still A Puzzle
mood: mystery
> i: cassini spacecraft | v: cassini saturn
Cassini flew close to Iapetus only once, on the tenth of September 2007, passing about sixteen hundred kilometres above its surface. The images showed bright ice and dark material in incredible detail, along with enormous landslides on crater walls.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus
Those landslides are a mystery of their own. Some of them travelled remarkably far, flowing across crater floors for tens of kilometres. One study suggested that friction during the slide could heat the ice at its base, making it slippery, so the avalanches glide much further than rock slides do on Earth. [pause] On Iapetus, mountains do not just fall. They ski.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus rotation
Since Cassini's mission ended in 2017, nobody has been back. Many questions remain. Why is the ridge only partly complete? How old is it? How deep does the dark coating go? [pause] Some of them may not be answered for decades.

> i: iapetus | v: iapetus
A moon painted half black and half white by dust from a distant neighbour. A wall of mountains around its waist. A frozen bulge from a time when it spun fast. And a sky filled with the rings of Saturn. [pause] Cassini saw its two faces through a simple telescope over three hundred years ago. We still do not fully understand what he was looking at.
