## Not An Explosion
mood: mystery
> i: big bang | v: big bang
First, a common mistake. The Big Bang was not an explosion in space. It was not a bomb going off at one point, flinging material into an empty void. There was no outside for it to explode into.

> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
Instead, it was the expansion of space itself, happening everywhere at once. Every point in the universe was moving away from every other point. Imagine dots drawn on a balloon as it inflates. No dot is the centre. [pause] They all just get further apart.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
And the further back in time you go, the closer together everything was. Hotter. Denser. Until, about thirteen point eight billion years ago, the entire observable universe was squeezed into an incredibly hot, dense state.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
The name itself began as a joke. In 1949, the astronomer Fred Hoyle, who believed the universe had always existed, mocked the idea on BBC radio, calling it a big bang. [pause] The insult stuck. And the evidence turned out to be on the other side.

## The First Instant
mood: mystery
> i: big bang | v: big bang
The very first moment, the first ten million-trillion-trillion-trillionth of a second, is a mystery. Physicists call it the {{Planck era|PLANCK ERA}}. At that point, the universe was so hot and dense that our best theories of gravity and quantum physics both break down. We simply do not know what was happening.

> i: cosmic inflation | v: expanding universe
Then, according to the leading theory, something extraordinary. In a tiny fraction of a second, the universe ballooned in size by a factor so huge it is hard to write down. At least a hundred trillion trillion times. This is {{cosmic inflation|INFLATION}}. A region smaller than an atom became larger than a grapefruit, faster than the blink of an eye could even begin.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Inflation explains two puzzles at once. Why the universe looks almost exactly the same in every direction, and why space is so close to perfectly flat. And it did something else. Tiny quantum jitters, smaller than any atom, were stretched to enormous sizes. [pause] Those ripples became the seeds of every galaxy that exists today.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
When inflation ended, the energy that had driven it turned into a seething soup of particles and radiation. The universe was now unimaginably hot, around a trillion trillion degrees. [pause] And it began to cool.

## The Soup
mood: wonder
> i: quark gluon plasma | v: particle collision
For the first millionth of a second, the universe was filled with a blazing fluid of the smallest building blocks of matter. Quarks and gluons, moving freely, never sticking together. Physicists call it a {{quark-gluon plasma|QUARK-GLUON PLASMA}}.

> i: particle accelerator | v: particle accelerator
We have recreated this soup on Earth. At particle accelerators in New York and Geneva, physicists smash heavy atoms together at almost the speed of light, briefly reaching trillions of degrees. To their surprise, the plasma did not behave like a gas. It flowed like a nearly perfect liquid. [pause] For a tiny instant, they made a drop of the early universe.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
As the universe cooled past about a trillion degrees, quarks finally began to stick together in threes. They formed protons and neutrons, the particles at the heart of every atom. This happened when the universe was about one millionth of a second old.

> i: antimatter | v: particle collision
At the same time, matter and antimatter were annihilating each other in flashes of light. For every billion antimatter particles, there seems to have been one billion and one particles of matter. [pause] Almost everything was destroyed. That tiny leftover, one in a billion, became every star, every planet and every person.

## One Second To Three Minutes
mood: wonder
> i: neutrino | v: neutrino
At about one second, the universe was around ten billion degrees. Ghostly particles called neutrinos stopped interacting with everything else and began flying freely through space. They are still out there, all around us. A sea of neutrinos from the first second of time, passing through your body right now, too faint to detect.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
Protons and neutrons were now floating in a hot bath of radiation. Whenever they tried to stick together, the radiation smashed them apart again. The universe was too hot to build anything.

> i: nuclear fusion | v: star core
Then, at about {{three minutes|3 MINUTES}}, it had cooled to around a billion degrees, roughly sixty times hotter than the centre of our Sun. Cool enough, at last, for nuclei to survive. And the whole universe became a giant fusion reactor.

> i: big bang nucleosynthesis | v: nuclear fusion
In a frantic few minutes, protons and neutrons fused into deuterium, then helium, with a tiny sprinkle of lithium. Then the universe expanded and cooled too much, and fusion stopped. By about twenty minutes, it was all over. [pause] Roughly three quarters hydrogen. One quarter helium. And a trace of everything else.

> i: big bang nucleosynthesis | v: early universe
The leftover deuterium turned out to be a precious clue. How much of it survived depends very sensitively on how much ordinary matter the universe contains. When astronomers measured it in distant gas clouds, the answer was clear. [pause] Ordinary matter, the stuff of stars and people, makes up only about five percent of everything.

## The Proof
mood: wonder
> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
How do we know any of this? Because those numbers match what we see. When astronomers measure the oldest, most pristine gas clouds and stars in the universe, they find about three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium. [pause] Exactly what the first three minutes predicted.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
And there is more. About three hundred and eighty thousand years after the beginning, the universe cooled enough for atoms to form and light to travel freely for the first time. That light is still arriving from every direction in the sky. It is called the {{cosmic microwave background|COSMIC MICROWAVE BACKGROUND}}, and it matches the predictions of the Big Bang in extraordinary detail.

> i: book | v: library
In 1977, the physicist Steven Weinberg wrote a book about these moments, called The First Three Minutes. It became a classic, because it showed something incredible. That with physics tested in laboratories on Earth, we can reconstruct what happened in the first instants of time.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
So the next time you drink a glass of water, think about this. The hydrogen in it was forged in the first three minutes of the universe, in a fireball that filled all of space. [pause] You are not just living in the universe. You are made of its first moments.
