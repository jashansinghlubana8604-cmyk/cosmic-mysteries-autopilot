## The Guest Star
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky
Chinese astronomers kept careful records of the sky for centuries, because they believed changes in the heavens carried messages about events on Earth. Their records of the guest star of 1054 include its position and how long it remained visible.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Some scholars think it may also appear in Japanese and Arabic records. In the American southwest, at Chaco Canyon, there is a rock painting showing a crescent moon next to a bright star-like symbol, which some researchers have suggested might record the same event. [pause] That interpretation is uncertain, but appealing. People across the world may have looked up at the same new star.

> i: crab nebula | v: crab nebula
Strangely, there are almost no clear records of it from Europe. Historians are still not entirely sure why. Perhaps the records were lost. Perhaps no one wrote it down. [pause] Either way, the best eyewitness accounts come from the other side of the world.

> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
The connection between the records and the nebula was first suggested in the 1920s. Edwin Hubble, the astronomer who discovered that the universe is expanding, noted that the Crab Nebula sat right where the Chinese had recorded their guest star. [pause] It was one of the first times a cloud in space was linked to a specific event written down by human beings.

## The Nebula
mood: wonder
> i: crab nebula hubble | v: crab nebula
In 1731, the English astronomer John Bevis found a faint, fuzzy patch in Taurus. In 1758, the French comet hunter Charles Messier spotted it too, and at first thought it might be a comet. It did not move. Annoyed, he started a catalogue of fuzzy objects that were not comets, so that he would not be fooled again.

> i: crab nebula | v: crab nebula
That nebula became the first entry in his list: {{Messier 1|MESSIER 1}}. Today, the Messier catalogue is one of the most famous lists of beautiful objects in the sky. And it all began with this cloud.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
You can see it yourself. With a small telescope on a dark winter night, the Crab Nebula appears as a faint, oval smudge of grey light near the tip of Taurus's southern horn. [pause] Not much to look at. Until you remember what it is.

> i: crab nebula | v: nebula
In the 1840s, the Irish astronomer Lord Rosse observed it with his giant telescope and drew a sketch that looked a little like a crab, with legs sticking out. The name stuck. In the 1920s, astronomers measured the cloud's expansion and traced it backwards. [pause] It must have started expanding around nine hundred years earlier. The Crab Nebula was the remains of the guest star of 1054.

## The Spinning Heart
mood: mystery
> i: crab nebula pulsar | v: pulsar
For a long time, the Crab Nebula's glow was a puzzle. It shines with a strange, bluish light that is not produced by hot gas in the usual way. It comes from electrons spiralling at nearly the speed of light around magnetic fields. Something was continuously feeding it energy.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
In 1968, astronomers found the answer. At the centre of the nebula, there is a {{pulsar|PULSAR}}. A neutron star about twenty kilometres across, heavier than the Sun, spinning around thirty times every second, sweeping beams of radiation across space like a lighthouse.

> i: crab nebula | v: crab nebula
The pulsar is the collapsed core of the star that exploded in 1054. As it spins, its enormous magnetic field flings out a wind of energetic particles, lighting up the nebula around it. [pause] The dead star is still powering its own funeral cloud.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
And it is slowing down. Very slightly, its spin rate drops every day, as it loses energy into the nebula. That energy loss is about the same as the energy the nebula radiates. [pause] It was one of the first strong confirmations that pulsars are spinning neutron stars.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
The Crab pulsar also produces something rare: giant radio pulses, occasional bursts that are hundreds or even thousands of times stronger than its normal pulses, lasting only tiny fractions of a second. [pause] Astronomers study them for clues to the mysterious fast radio bursts that arrive from distant galaxies.

## A Cosmic Laboratory
mood: wonder
> i: crab nebula xray | v: chandra xray
The Crab Nebula is one of the brightest sources of X-rays and gamma rays in the sky. For decades, it was so steady that astronomers used it as a standard reference to calibrate their instruments. [pause] Then, in 2011, it surprised everyone.

> i: crab nebula | v: crab nebula
Gamma-ray satellites saw it suddenly flare to several times its usual brightness, for just a few days. The energy involved was enormous, and the region producing it had to be small. Scientists think magnetic fields in the nebula may snap and reconnect, accelerating particles to extreme energies. [pause] Even the most studied object in the sky still has secrets.

> i: crab nebula webb | v: crab nebula
In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope took a new image of the Crab Nebula in infrared light. It revealed the delicate filaments of gas and dust in remarkable detail, glowing in red and orange, wrapped around a ghostly cloud of white light from the pulsar's wind.

> i: crab nebula | v: crab nebula
Webb's measurements also helped astronomers study the elements forged in the explosion. They are trying to work out exactly what kind of star exploded, and how. [pause] Nearly a thousand years after the blast, we are still investigating the crime scene.

## Looking Back In Time
mood: wonder
> i: crab nebula | v: nebula
The Crab Nebula is about six and a half thousand light-years away. So the star actually exploded around six and a half thousand years before 1054. Its light had been travelling across space ever since, arriving on Earth in the summer of 1054, when someone in China happened to look up.

> i: supernova remnant | v: supernova remnant
It is one of the very few supernova remnants that we can link to a specific date in human history. That makes it priceless. We know exactly how old it is, so we can watch how a supernova remnant evolves over time.

> i: crab nebula webb | v: crab nebula
Next time you see a picture of the Crab Nebula, remember that it is a link between ancient astronomers and modern telescopes. Between a guest star in an imperial record and a spinning neutron star in a Webb image. [pause] A star died, people wrote it down, and nine and a half centuries later, we are still learning from it.
