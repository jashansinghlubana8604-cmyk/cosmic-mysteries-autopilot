On the night of the fifteenth of October 1991, in the desert of Utah, a detector designed to watch the sky for faint flashes of light recorded something it had never seen before.

> i: cosmic ray air shower | v: cosmic rays
High in the atmosphere, a single particle from space had slammed into the air. Not a meteor. Not a rock. A single subatomic particle, probably one proton, millions of times smaller than a grain of dust.

> i: cosmic rays | v: particle collision
But that one particle was carrying as much energy as a baseball thrown at around ninety kilometres an hour. Packed into something so small that tens of billions of them, side by side, would barely span the width of a hair. [pause] The scientists who analysed it were stunned.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
They gave it a name that stuck. The {{Oh-My-God particle|OH-MY-GOD PARTICLE}}. And more than thirty years later, nobody knows what could have launched it. [pause] Or where it came from.

## Rays From The Sky
mood: wonder
> i: hot air balloon | v: hot air balloon
The story starts in 1912. An Austrian physicist named Victor Hess carried instruments up in a hydrogen balloon, more than five kilometres high, to measure natural radiation in the air. Scientists assumed the radiation came from rocks in the ground, so it should fade with height.

> i: hot air balloon | v: clouds
It did the opposite. The higher Hess climbed, the stronger it got. He even flew during a near-total solar eclipse, and the radiation did not fade, so the Sun was not the source. Something was raining down on the Earth from space.

> i: cosmic rays | v: cosmic rays
They became known as {{cosmic rays|COSMIC RAYS}}, although they are not really rays at all. They are particles, mostly protons and atomic nuclei, racing through space at almost the speed of light. Hess won the Nobel Prize for discovering them. [pause] And right now, thousands of their offspring pass through your body every hour.

> i: cosmic ray air shower | v: particle collision
When a high-energy cosmic ray hits the top of the atmosphere, it smashes into an atom and sets off a cascade. One particle becomes many, which become many more. By the time this {{air shower|AIR SHOWER}} reaches the ground, it can contain billions of particles spread over kilometres.

## The Night In Utah
mood: mystery
> i: fly's eye detector | v: desert
The detector in Utah was called the Fly's Eye. It was made of dozens of mirrors pointing at different parts of the sky, like the many lenses of an insect's eye. On dark nights, it watched for the faint blue glow that air showers produce as they tear through the atmosphere.

> i: cosmic ray air shower | v: cosmic rays
On that October night, it caught a shower so big that the energy of the original particle had to be about three hundred million trillion electron volts. To put that in perspective, the Large Hadron Collider, the most powerful particle accelerator ever built, gives its protons about forty million times less.

> i: cosmic rays | v: particle collision
The Oh-My-God particle was moving incredibly close to the speed of light. So close that, if it raced a beam of light for a whole year, it would fall behind by only about a thousandth of the width of a human hair. [pause] Nothing we can build comes anywhere near it.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
And here is the puzzle. Particles with that much energy should not survive long journeys through the universe. Space is filled with the faint afterglow of the Big Bang. A particle that energetic should collide with that ancient light, losing energy as it goes. [pause] So it probably came from somewhere relatively close, within a few hundred million light-years. But when astronomers looked in its direction, there was no obvious source.

## The Amaterasu Particle
mood: mystery
> i: telescope array | v: desert
In 2021, another detector in Utah, called the Telescope Array, caught a second giant. It spreads more than five hundred detectors across about seven hundred square kilometres of desert. On the twenty seventh of May, many of them lit up at once.

> i: cosmic rays | v: cosmic rays
The particle that started the shower carried about two hundred and forty million trillion electron volts. Close to the Oh-My-God particle. When the result was published in 2023, the team named it after the Japanese sun goddess. The {{Amaterasu particle|AMATERASU PARTICLE}}.

> i: local void | v: cosmic web
Then they traced its direction back across the sky. It seemed to come from a region called the {{Local Void|LOCAL VOID}}. A vast, nearly empty patch of space next to our galaxy, with very few galaxies in it. [pause] An empty region of the universe had apparently fired the second most energetic particle ever recorded.

> i: magnetic field | v: magnetic field
That may not be the whole story. Magnetic fields in space can bend the path of charged particles, so the true source might lie in a different direction. Or the particle may have been heavier than a proton, and bent more. [pause] But the mystery only deepened.

## What Could Do This?
mood: dread
> i: black hole jet | v: black hole jet
What could accelerate a single particle to such absurd energies? Scientists have a list of suspects. The jets fired by supermassive black holes at the centres of active galaxies. The shock waves around exploding stars. Rapidly spinning, highly magnetised neutron stars.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
Some think gamma-ray bursts, the most powerful explosions in the universe, could do it. Others have suggested colliding galaxies, or the gigantic shocks formed as galaxy clusters merge. [pause] Every candidate is extreme. None has been confirmed.

> i: particle collision | v: particle collision
Some physicists wonder whether these particles could be clues to something entirely new. Perhaps the decay of some unknown heavy particle left over from the early universe, or a hint of physics beyond our current theories. Most think the answer will turn out to be astrophysical, a natural accelerator we simply have not identified yet. [pause] But until we find the source, the door stays open.

> i: pierre auger observatory | v: desert
The biggest detector on Earth, the {{Pierre Auger Observatory|PIERRE AUGER OBSERVATORY}} in Argentina, covers about three thousand square kilometres with around sixteen hundred water tanks. In 2017, it showed that the highest-energy cosmic rays mostly come from outside our galaxy, arriving slightly more from one broad direction in the sky. [pause] A clue, but not an address.

## Bullets From The Universe
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic rays | v: cosmic rays
Particles like these are incredibly rare. Above the energy of the Amaterasu particle, roughly one arrives per square kilometre of the Earth's surface per century. That is why the detectors have to be so enormous. You need to watch a huge area for years to catch even one.

> i: earth atmosphere | v: earth from space
Thankfully, our atmosphere protects us. A single particle carries a lot of energy for its size, but spread out into a shower of billions of fragments across kilometres, it is completely harmless. It is the astronauts, above the atmosphere, who have to worry about cosmic rays.

> i: astronaut | v: international space station
Astronauts on the Apollo missions reported seeing strange flashes of light with their eyes closed, especially in the dark. Scientists later concluded that these were cosmic rays passing straight through their eyeballs and brains, triggering the cells of the retina. [pause] They were literally seeing the universe's particles go through their heads.

> i: cosmic ray air shower | v: night sky
And yet these particles are messengers. Each one has travelled for millions of years, from some unknown cosmic engine more powerful than anything we understand, to end its journey in a flash of blue light over a desert. [pause] Somewhere out there is a machine that fires subatomic bullets with the punch of a baseball. We still do not know where it is. Or what it is.
