## The Highest Odds Ever
mood: dread
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
In June 2004, three astronomers at Kitt Peak Observatory in Arizona spotted a faint, fast-moving dot. They saw it for two nights, then lost it. In December, an observatory in Australia found it again. Now astronomers had enough to calculate its orbit.

> i: asteroid orbit | v: asteroid orbit
What they found was alarming. Its path crossed the Earth's orbit. And on the thirteenth of April 2029, the two might meet. With each new measurement, the odds of an impact went up. One in three hundred. One in sixty. Then, around Christmas 2004, about {{one in forty|1 IN 37}}.

> i: asteroid apophis | v: asteroid
No asteroid had ever been rated that high on the Torino scale, the system scientists use to rank impact threats. Apophis reached level four. The kind of number that makes planetary scientists cancel their holidays.

> i: telescope archive | v: telescope night
Then, two days after Christmas, someone found older images of Apophis, taken months before anyone knew it was there. With a longer stretch of observations, the orbit became sharper. [pause] And the impact in 2029 was ruled out. It would miss. But only just.

> i: asteroid artist impression | v: asteroid
Its discoverers gave it a fitting name. In Egyptian mythology, Apophis was a giant serpent of darkness who tried every night to swallow the Sun. Some of the team were also fans of a science fiction series with a villain of the same name. [pause] For a few days in 2004, the name seemed a little too appropriate.

## Thirty Two Thousand Kilometres
mood: mystery
> i: earth satellites | v: satellites orbit
So how close is just? On the thirteenth of April 2029, Apophis will pass around {{32,000 km|32,000 KM}} above the Earth's surface. That sounds like a lot. It is not.

> i: geostationary satellite | v: satellites orbit
The satellites that hover over one spot of the Earth, carrying television, communications and weather data, sit about thirty six thousand kilometres up. Apophis will pass inside that ring. Closer to us than they are.

> i: earth moon distance | v: earth moon
The Moon is about twelve times further away. If you shrank the Earth to the size of a basketball, Apophis would pass about two and a half metres from it. [pause] On the scale of the solar system, that is a bullet grazing your sleeve.

> i: asteroid earth flyby | v: asteroid flyby
And it will be visible. Over Europe, Africa and parts of Asia, it will appear as a point of light about as bright as the stars of the Plough, moving steadily across the sky. No telescope needed. It will be the first time in recorded history that an asteroid this large has been seen this close by the naked eye.

## What If It Did Hit
mood: dread
> i: asteroid impact | v: asteroid impact
Let's be clear: it will not. But it helps to know what we have escaped. An object the size of Apophis, moving at more than twelve kilometres per second, carries enormous energy.

> v: asteroid impact | i: impact crater
If it struck land, it would release energy estimated at around a thousand megatons. Many times more than the largest nuclear bomb ever tested. It would carve a crater several kilometres wide and devastate an area the size of a small country.

> v: ocean waves | i: ocean
If it struck the ocean, it could raise tsunamis that would threaten coastlines across a whole ocean basin. It would not end civilisation. It would not cause a mass extinction. [pause] But it would be the worst natural disaster in human history.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
That is why asteroids like Apophis are called city killers, or more accurately, region killers. And that is why, for years after the 2029 scare faded, scientists kept watching for later dates, 2036, then 2068. In 2021, after precise radar measurements, NASA announced that Apophis poses {{no risk|NO RISK}} for at least the next hundred years.

## The Earth Will Change It
mood: mystery
> i: asteroid apophis | v: asteroid rotation
Here is the part that excites scientists. Apophis will pass so close that the Earth's gravity will grab it and reshape its path around the Sun. Its orbit will be stretched, and it will leave on a different track from the one it arrived on.

> i: asteroid surface | v: asteroid surface
It may also change the asteroid itself. Apophis is thought to be a peanut-shaped pile of rock, tumbling as it goes. The Earth's tides could alter how it spins. Some scientists think it could even shake the surface, triggering tiny {{asteroid quakes|ASTEROID QUAKES}} and rockslides, revealing fresh rock underneath.

> i: asteroid earth | v: asteroid flyby
Nature is about to run an experiment we could never afford to do ourselves. A large asteroid, squeezed and shaken by a planet, in front of every telescope on Earth. [pause] And this happens with an asteroid this big only about once every few thousand years.

## The Welcome Party
mood: wonder
> i: osiris-rex spacecraft | v: osiris-rex
We will not just watch from the ground. NASA's {{OSIRIS-APEX|OSIRIS-APEX}} spacecraft, the same probe that brought back pieces of asteroid Bennu in 2023, has been sent on a new mission to meet Apophis.

> i: osiris-rex bennu | v: spacecraft asteroid
It is due to arrive shortly after the close approach, and to stay for about a year and a half, mapping the asteroid in detail and studying how the encounter changed it. It will even fire its thrusters close to the surface to blow away dust and see what lies beneath.

> i: spacecraft asteroid | v: spacecraft
The European Space Agency has also been preparing a mission of its own, designed to reach Apophis before the flyby and ride alongside it as it passes the Earth. If it flies, we would watch the encounter from the asteroid itself. [pause] A front-row seat to a planet's gravity at work.

> i: planetary defense dart | v: dart impact
And all of this matters for one reason. One day, we will find an asteroid that really is heading for us. When we do, we will need to know how these objects are built, how they respond to a push, and how fast we can reach them. In 2022, NASA's DART mission proved we can nudge an asteroid off course by crashing into it. [pause] Apophis is the next lesson.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
And we still have a lot of searching to do. Astronomers believe they have found nearly all the giant asteroids, the kilometre-sized ones that could threaten civilisation. But many of the smaller, city-sized ones are still out there, unseen. New survey telescopes on the ground and in space are being built to find them. [pause] The goal is simple: never be surprised.

> i: asteroid earth flyby | v: night sky
So on the evening of the thirteenth of April 2029, if you are in the right part of the world, look up. A faint point of light will slide between the stars, closer than our satellites, and then fade away into the dark. [pause] The god of chaos, passing by, and leaving us alone.
