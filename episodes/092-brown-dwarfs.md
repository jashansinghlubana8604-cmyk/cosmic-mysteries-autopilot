Imagine an object bigger than Jupiter, made of the same stuff as the Sun. It formed like a star, from a collapsing cloud of gas. It glowed when it was young. For a while, it even ran a weak kind of nuclear fusion in its core.

> i: brown dwarf artist impression | v: brown dwarf
But it never got heavy enough to become a true star. Its core never got hot enough to burn ordinary hydrogen. So it slowly cooled and dimmed, and kept cooling, for billions of years.

> i: luhman 16 | v: brown dwarf
Today, some of these objects are cooler than a cup of coffee. They have weather. Clouds of molten rock. Rain made of iron. Auroras with no star nearby to power them. [pause] And there may be billions of them in our galaxy, drifting in the dark.

> i: star field | v: star field
They are called brown dwarfs. The stars that failed. [pause] And for most of history, nobody knew they existed.

## Too Small To Burn
mood: mystery
> i: sun | v: sun
A true star, like the Sun, shines by fusing hydrogen into helium in its core. That takes enormous temperature and pressure, which needs a lot of mass. The smallest true stars are around eighty times the mass of Jupiter.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotation
Below about thirteen times the mass of Jupiter, there is no fusion at all. Objects like that are giant planets. And between thirteen and eighty, there is a strange middle ground. Objects heavy enough to briefly burn a rare form of hydrogen, called deuterium, but not heavy enough to keep going. [pause] {{Brown dwarfs|BROWN DWARFS}}.

> i: brown dwarf artist impression | v: brown dwarf
Here is something odd. Almost all brown dwarfs are roughly the same size as Jupiter, whether they weigh ten Jupiters or seventy. Pile more mass on, and gravity simply squeezes them tighter. [pause] They get heavier without getting bigger.

> i: brown dwarf | v: brown dwarf
They were first predicted in the early 1960s by the astronomer Shiv Kumar, who realised that the smallest collapsing clouds could not become proper stars. In 1975, the astronomer Jill Tarter gave them their name. Not because they are actually brown, but because no one could agree on a better colour for something between red and black.

## The First Discoveries
mood: wonder
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
For decades, brown dwarfs were too faint to find. They give off most of their light in the infrared, invisible to the eye and hard for older telescopes to detect. Then, in 1995, two discoveries came almost at once.

> i: pleiades | v: star cluster
In the Pleiades star cluster, a team in Spain found a young brown dwarf they called {{Teide 1|TEIDE 1}}. And around a nearby red dwarf star called Gliese 229, a team in the United States found a faint companion whose light showed the fingerprint of methane, the same gas found in the atmosphere of Jupiter. [pause] It was far too cold to be a star. A brown dwarf, at last.

> i: brown dwarf binary | v: binary star
That companion, Gliese 229B, held a surprise for nearly thirty years. It seemed too dim for its mass. In 2024, astronomers finally solved the puzzle. It is not one brown dwarf, but {{two|TWINS}}, orbiting each other every twelve days, so close together that they looked like a single object. [pause] The first brown dwarf ever found turned out to be a pair.

> i: star cluster | v: star cluster
Since then, Webb has peered into young star clusters and found free-floating objects weighing just a few times the mass of Jupiter, glowing faintly in the infrared, with no star at all. Nobody is sure whether they formed like tiny stars or were thrown out of planetary systems. [pause] The line between the smallest brown dwarfs and the biggest lost planets is getting very blurry.

## The Coldest Ones
mood: dread
> i: wise telescope | v: satellite orbit
In the 2010s, NASA's WISE space telescope surveyed the whole sky in infrared, perfect for spotting cool, faint brown dwarfs. It found thousands, including a new class colder than any known before, called Y dwarfs.

> i: brown dwarf | v: brown dwarf
The coldest of all is called {{WISE 0855|WISE 0855}}. It is only about seven light-years away, one of the closest neighbours of the Sun. And its temperature is estimated at somewhere around minus twenty five to minus fifty degrees. Colder than the North Pole in winter.

> i: brown dwarf artist impression | v: brown dwarf
An object made like a star, roughly the size of Jupiter, colder than an Arctic night, drifting just a few light-years from the Earth. In 2023, Webb studied its atmosphere and found water vapour, methane and ammonia. [pause] Some scientists suspect it may even have clouds of water ice.

> i: star field | v: star field
And here is a chilling thought. Brown dwarfs are so dim that there could be others near us that we still have not found. Some could be closer than the nearest known star. [pause] Dark, cold neighbours, invisible to our eyes.

## Weather On A Failed Star
mood: wonder
> i: luhman 16 | v: brown dwarf
In 2013, astronomers discovered the closest pair of brown dwarfs to the Sun, called {{Luhman 16|LUHMAN 16}}, only about six and a half light-years away. They were close enough to study their weather in detail.

> i: brown dwarf weather | v: storm clouds
As they spin, their brightness flickers, as patchy clouds rotate in and out of view. Scientists used these flickers to make rough maps of their atmospheres, showing bands and spots like those on Jupiter. The clouds are not made of water. On warmer brown dwarfs, they are thought to be made of {{molten rock and iron|IRON RAIN}}.

> i: brown dwarf artist impression | v: storm clouds
You could never stand on one. There is no surface, just atmosphere getting thicker and hotter the deeper you go. And the gravity is fierce. On a brown dwarf fifty times the mass of Jupiter, packed into the same size, the pull at the cloud tops would be dozens of times stronger than on Earth. [pause] Your own weight would crush you to the floor of the sky.

> i: brown dwarf aurora | v: aurora
And in 2025, Webb found something astonishing on a cold brown dwarf floating on its own, far from any star. Its atmosphere was glowing in a way that suggested an aurora, like the northern lights. On Earth, auroras are powered by the Sun. [pause] On this lonely brown dwarf, the power may come from its own magnetic field, or perhaps from an unseen moon.

## Where Planets End And Stars Begin
mood: wonder
> i: exoplanet | v: exoplanet
Brown dwarfs blur the line between planets and stars. Some planets are heavier than some brown dwarfs. Some brown dwarfs orbit stars like giant planets. Others float freely through space, like stars without light.

> i: exoplanet artist impression | v: exoplanet
Could a brown dwarf have a habitable planet? Some do have planets, and a world orbiting very close could be warm for a while. But a brown dwarf keeps cooling, so its warm zone keeps shrinking inwards. [pause] Any planet that began life comfortable would slowly freeze, as its failed sun faded around it.

> i: rogue planet | v: rogue planet
Astronomers are still debating how many there are. Some estimates suggest there could be one brown dwarf for every few stars in the galaxy, tens of billions in total. Most will never be seen with our eyes.

> i: brown dwarf | v: star field
And they will outlast almost everything. As they cool, they never die in an explosion. They simply fade, over trillions of years, becoming darker and colder. [pause] In the far future, when the last stars have burned out, the galaxy may be full of these silent, frozen embers. The failed stars that never really lived, and so can never really die.
