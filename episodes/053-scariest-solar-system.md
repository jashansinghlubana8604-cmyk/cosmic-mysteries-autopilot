The Earth is a strange place. It is the only world we know where you can step outside without a spacesuit. Where the air is breathable, the temperature is mild and the ground stays still beneath you.

> i: solar system | v: solar system
Everywhere else in the solar system is trying to kill you. Not slowly. Quickly. Some places would freeze you solid. Others would cook you, crush you, or fill your body with radiation in minutes.

> i: mercury messenger | v: mercury
We have sent robots to most of them. We know their temperatures, their pressures, their radiation, to remarkable precision. [pause] Which means we can say exactly how long you would last.

Here are eight of the most dangerous places in our solar system, ranked from deadly to unthinkable. [pause] And the most dangerous place of all is the one you see every day.

## The Cold Ones
mood: dread
> i: mars surface | v: mars surface
Number eight. The surface of {{Mars|MARS}}. It looks almost friendly in photographs, a red desert under a pale sky. But the air is about one percent as thick as the Earth's, and almost all of it is carbon dioxide.

> i: mars | v: mars
Without a suit, you would lose consciousness in about fifteen seconds. In air that thin, the water in your saliva and on your eyes would begin to boil away, even though the temperature is often far below freezing. The soil is laced with toxic chemicals called perchlorates. And with no magnetic field or thick air above you, the surface is bathed in radiation from space. [pause] Mars is the friendliest planet we know of. That should tell you something.

> v: mars dust storm | i: mars dust storm
And sometimes, the whole planet disappears. Every few years, dust storms on Mars grow and merge until they wrap the entire globe for months. In 2018, one of them blotted out the Sun over the Opportunity rover so completely that its solar panels could no longer charge. [pause] It never woke up again.

> i: moon shadowed crater | v: moon south pole
Number seven. The bottoms of the {{shadowed craters|SHADOWED CRATERS}} at the Moon's poles. Because the Moon barely tilts, some craters near its poles have not seen sunlight for billions of years. Darkness, total and permanent.

> i: lunar reconnaissance orbiter | v: moon south pole
In 2009, NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter measured temperatures in some of them of around minus two hundred and forty degrees. That is colder than the surface of Pluto. Some of the coldest places ever measured in the solar system are on our own Moon, a short trip from home. [pause] Anything that falls in there stays frozen, possibly forever, including water ice we may one day need.

## Fire And Ice
mood: dread
> i: mercury messenger | v: mercury
Number six. {{Mercury|MERCURY}}, the planet closest to the Sun. During its long day, the Sun looks around three times wider than it does from the Earth, and the ground reaches about four hundred and thirty degrees. Hot enough to melt lead.

> i: mercury surface | v: mercury
But Mercury has almost no air to hold the heat. When night falls, it plunges to around minus one hundred and eighty degrees. And a single day on Mercury, from one sunrise to the next, lasts about one hundred and seventy six Earth days. [pause] Months of scorching light. Then months of deep-freeze darkness.

> i: triton | v: triton
Number five. {{Triton|TRITON}}, the giant moon of Neptune. Its surface is around minus two hundred and thirty five degrees, among the coldest ever measured on any world. Nitrogen there is frozen solid, like snow and glaciers.

> i: triton geysers | v: triton
And yet, Voyager 2 saw dark plumes erupting kilometres into its thin sky. Geysers on a world so cold that the air you breathe would fall around you as frost. [pause] Above it, Neptune hangs in the sky, scoured by the fastest winds in the solar system.

## Invisible Death
mood: dread
> i: jupiter magnetosphere | v: jupiter aurora
Number four. The neighbourhood of {{Jupiter|JUPITER}}. Jupiter's magnetic field is the largest structure in the solar system after the Sun's. And it traps charged particles, accelerating them to incredible energies, creating radiation belts far stronger than the Earth's.

> i: europa | v: europa
On the surface of Europa, the radiation dose is estimated at several sieverts per day. A dose like that, all at once, is enough to kill a person. A day on Europa could deliver it. Spacecraft that pass through this region need thick armour around their electronics just to survive.

> i: europa clipper | v: europa clipper
NASA's Europa Clipper carries its most sensitive electronics inside a vault with walls of titanium and aluminium, and it will only swoop close to Europa briefly before retreating, again and again, to limit its dose. [pause] Even a robot built for this place can only visit in short, careful dashes.

> i: io | v: io volcano
Number three. {{Io|IO}}, the innermost of Jupiter's big moons. It sits even deeper in those radiation belts, so the dose there is far higher. You could get a lethal amount within hours.

> i: io volcano | v: lava
And if the radiation did not get you, the ground might. Io has around four hundred active volcanoes, lava lakes wider than countries and sulphur plumes shooting hundreds of kilometres into space. [pause] It is the only place where you could be killed by a volcano, invisible radiation and a sky of falling sulphur snow, all at once.

## The Furnace
mood: dread
> i: venus surface | v: venus
Number two. The surface of {{Venus|VENUS}}. It is about four hundred and sixty five degrees, day and night, pole to pole. Hotter than Mercury, even though Venus is almost twice as far from the Sun, because its thick carbon dioxide air traps the heat.

> i: venus clouds | v: venus clouds
The pressure at the ground is about ninety times the Earth's, like being nearly a kilometre deep in the ocean. The clouds above are made of sulphuric acid. Soviet landers that reached the surface were crushed and cooked within a couple of hours at most.

> i: venus | v: venus
You would be crushed, roasted and dissolved at the same time. [pause] And still, Venus is only number two.

## The Star
mood: dread
> i: sun solar dynamics observatory | v: sun
Number one. {{The Sun|THE SUN}}. The source of all life on Earth is also the most dangerous place in the solar system. Its visible surface is around five and a half thousand degrees. Its outer atmosphere, the corona, is somehow over a million degrees.

> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
It erupts with flares that release the energy of billions of nuclear bombs in minutes. It throws out clouds of plasma heavier than mountains. Its magnetic loops arch higher than whole Earths stacked on top of each other.

> i: parker solar probe | v: parker solar probe
And we have touched it. On the twenty fourth of December 2024, NASA's {{Parker Solar Probe|PARKER SOLAR PROBE}} passed about six million kilometres from the Sun's surface, closer than any human-made object ever. It was moving at around six hundred and ninety thousand kilometres per hour, the fastest object we have ever built.

> i: parker solar probe heat shield | v: parker solar probe
Its heat shield faced temperatures of around a thousand degrees, while the instruments behind it stayed close to room temperature. Out of radio contact during the pass, it survived, and called home a few days later. [pause] We sent a machine to the most dangerous place in the solar system, and it came back.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
Mars, the Moon, Mercury, Triton, Europa, Io, Venus and the Sun. Every one of them would kill you in minutes, or seconds. [pause] And then there is the Earth. A thin layer of air, a magnetic shield, liquid water and mild sunshine. The only safe place we have ever found. Look after it.
