## The Battered Moon
mood: wonder
> i: jupiter moons | v: jupiter moons
Callisto was discovered by Galileo in January 1610, along with Io, Europa and Ganymede. It is the outermost of the four, orbiting nearly two million kilometres from Jupiter. At about four thousand eight hundred kilometres across, it is almost exactly the size of the planet Mercury.

> i: callisto surface | v: callisto
When the Voyager spacecraft flew past in 1979, they revealed a surface absolutely saturated with craters. There is barely any room for more. Every new impact lands on top of older ones. [pause] There are no mountains made by volcanoes, no fresh lava plains, no signs of plate tectonics. Just craters, from horizon to horizon.

> i: valhalla crater | v: callisto
Its most dramatic feature is {{Valhalla|VALHALLA}}, a giant impact basin surrounded by rings of ridges and troughs spreading out across about three thousand eight hundred kilometres, like ripples frozen in a pond. It is one of the largest impact structures anywhere in the solar system. [pause] Something enormous struck Callisto billions of years ago, and the scar has never healed.

> i: callisto | v: callisto
Callisto's surface tells us how violent the early solar system was. Because nothing has erased its craters, it is like a history book whose pages have never been torn out. [pause] Reading it helps scientists date the surfaces of other worlds.

## A Strange Landscape
mood: mystery
> i: callisto galileo | v: callisto
When NASA's Galileo spacecraft studied Callisto up close in the late 1990s, it found something unexpected. At the smallest scales, many of the little craters were missing. The surface was blanketed in dark, fine material, with bright icy spires and knobs poking up through it.

> i: callisto surface | v: callisto
Scientists think the cause is slow erosion. As sunlight warms the dark surface, ice slowly evaporates and moves, leaving dark dust behind and collapsing crater rims. Over billions of years, this process has softened Callisto's smallest features, while leaving the giant craters intact.

> i: callisto | v: callisto
Callisto also has an incredibly thin atmosphere of carbon dioxide and traces of oxygen, far too thin to breathe. [pause] It is a frozen, dusty, silent world, where the most dramatic event in the last billion years may be a pebble falling from a crater wall.

> i: callisto interior | v: callisto
Callisto is also surprisingly light for its size. Its density suggests it is made of roughly equal amounts of rock and ice, mixed together. Unlike the Earth, with its iron core and rocky mantle, Callisto may never have fully separated into layers. [pause] A world that never quite finished forming its insides.

## The Hidden Ocean
mood: mystery
> i: galileo spacecraft | v: galileo spacecraft
And yet, inside, Callisto may not be so quiet. As the Galileo spacecraft flew past, its magnetometer picked up something surprising. Callisto was responding to Jupiter's changing magnetic field, generating a magnetic field of its own.

> i: callisto interior | v: callisto
The best explanation was a layer of electrically conducting material beneath the surface. Most likely, a {{salty ocean|SALTY OCEAN}}, buried perhaps a hundred kilometres or more beneath the ice. The same kind of signal had revealed oceans inside Europa and Ganymede.

> i: callisto interior | v: callisto
That was a shock. Callisto does not get squeezed and heated by Jupiter's tides as much as the inner moons do. Its interior may not even be fully separated into layers of rock and ice. Keeping an ocean liquid there might require something like ammonia, acting as antifreeze. [pause] The most lifeless-looking moon in the system may have a sea inside it.

> i: deep ocean | v: deep sea
Could anything live there? Callisto's ocean may be sandwiched between thick layers of ice, cut off from rock and from much of the chemical energy life might need. Most scientists think Europa and Enceladus are better places to look. [pause] But we once thought Callisto could not have an ocean at all. It has surprised us before.

## The Best Place For A Base
mood: wonder
> i: jupiter magnetosphere | v: jupiter aurora
Jupiter is surrounded by some of the most intense radiation in the solar system, trapped in its giant magnetic field. Near Io and Europa, the radiation is strong enough to kill an unprotected human within a day. That makes them terrible places to visit in person.

> i: callisto | v: callisto
Callisto orbits much further out, beyond the worst of the radiation belts. The dose at its surface is far lower, low enough that astronauts could stay there with modest shielding. In 2003, a NASA study imagined sending humans to Callisto, perhaps around the middle of this century, to build a base.

> i: moon surface | v: moon surface
Gravity there is about an eighth of the Earth's, similar to our Moon. Astronauts could bound across the surface in giant leaps. Ice for drinking water, for oxygen and for rocket fuel would be everywhere beneath their feet. [pause] Everything a base needs, except warmth.

> i: jupiter from moon | v: jupiter rotation
From there, they could control robots exploring Europa's ocean, make fuel from Callisto's ice, and look up at a sky dominated by Jupiter, appearing several times wider than the full Moon looks from Earth. [pause] Callisto could be humanity's gateway to the outer solar system.

## Coming Back
mood: wonder
> i: juice spacecraft | v: juice spacecraft
The European Space Agency's JUICE spacecraft, on its way to Jupiter, is planned to make many close flybys of Callisto in the early 2030s, mapping its surface and probing its interior with radar and magnetic measurements. It will help confirm whether the ocean is really there.

> i: jupiter moons | v: jupiter moons
There is a reason Callisto is so different from its neighbours. Io, Europa and Ganymede are locked in an orbital rhythm that keeps their orbits slightly stretched, so Jupiter's tides constantly knead and heat them. Callisto is outside that rhythm. Its orbit stays calm, and it receives far less tidal heat. [pause] It is the moon that was left out of the dance.

> i: callisto | v: callisto
NASA's Europa Clipper will also pass Callisto during its tour. Between them, the two spacecraft may finally tell us what lies beneath this battered, ancient crust.

> i: callisto galileo | v: jupiter moons
A moon that has been struck more times than almost anything else in the solar system, and simply absorbed the blows. A frozen surface slowly crumbling into dust. And perhaps, deep below, a dark ocean. [pause] Callisto is not boring. It is patient. And it has been waiting four billion years for someone to notice.
