The Earth is about thirteen thousand kilometres wide. That feels enormous. But the Sun is more than a hundred times wider. And our whole solar system, out to the orbit of Neptune, is more than three hundred thousand times wider than the Sun.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
Our galaxy, the Milky Way, is so big that light takes around a hundred thousand years to cross it. It contains hundreds of billions of stars. Most people would say that is as big as anything gets.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
It is not even close. Galaxies are not scattered randomly. They are arranged into groups, clusters, superclusters, and vast sheets and filaments, separated by enormous empty voids. A {{cosmic web|COSMIC WEB}}, stretching across the whole universe.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
And some of its strands are so large, billions of light-years across, that astronomers are not sure they should be able to exist at all. [pause] These are the biggest things in the universe.

## The Ladder Of Size
mood: wonder
> i: local group | v: galaxy
Our galaxy belongs to a small gang of more than fifty galaxies called the {{Local Group|LOCAL GROUP}}, about ten million light-years across. The biggest members are the Milky Way and Andromeda. The rest are mostly small dwarf galaxies.

> i: virgo cluster | v: galaxy cluster
The Local Group sits on the outskirts of something much bigger, the Virgo Cluster, a swarm of more than a thousand galaxies about fifty million light-years away. And the Virgo Cluster is part of something bigger still.

> i: laniakea supercluster | v: cosmic web
In 2014, astronomers mapped the motions of thousands of galaxies and found that we belong to a gigantic {{supercluster|SUPERCLUSTER}} they named Laniakea, a Hawaiian word meaning immeasurable heaven. It contains around a hundred thousand galaxies and stretches around five hundred million light-years across.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Every galaxy in Laniakea is flowing towards a central region, like rivers draining into a valley. [pause] And Laniakea is still not the biggest structure out there. Not even close.

## The Great Walls
mood: mystery
> i: galaxy survey map | v: cosmic web
When astronomers began mapping the positions of galaxies in three dimensions, they found long sheets of galaxies, called walls, stretched between empty voids. In 2003, the Sloan Digital Sky Survey revealed one so long it was named the {{Sloan Great Wall|SLOAN GREAT WALL}}, more than a billion light-years from end to end.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
In 2025, a team led by Hans Böhringer announced an even larger one, much closer to home, within about a billion light-years of us. They named it Quipu, after the knotted strings the Inca used to keep records, because its shape looked like a long main cord with branches. It stretches around one point four billion light-years and contains dozens of galaxy clusters. [pause] One of the largest structures ever found in the nearby universe.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
And further out, there is something astronomers have spotted using gamma-ray bursts, the most powerful explosions in the universe. In one region of the sky, these bursts seem to cluster together, hinting at a vast concentration of galaxies called the Hercules-Corona Borealis Great Wall. Some estimates put it at around {{ten billion light-years|10 BILLION LIGHT-YEARS}} long. [pause] That would be about a tenth of the whole observable universe. But its existence is still debated.

## The Arc And The Ring
mood: mystery
> i: quasar | v: quasar
In 2021, a PhD student at the University of Central Lancashire named Alexia Lopez was studying the faint light of distant quasars, which passes through gas around galaxies on its way to us. She noticed a pattern in the absorption, a long, curved line of galaxies stretching across the sky.

> i: giant arc | v: cosmic web
It became known as the {{Giant Arc|THE GIANT ARC}}. About three point three billion light-years long, roughly a fifteenth of the diameter of the observable universe, and about nine billion light-years away.

> i: big ring | v: cosmic web
Then, in 2024, Lopez and her colleagues found another structure, close to the Giant Arc in the sky, at the same distance. A nearly perfect ring of galaxies, about one point three billion light-years across. They called it {{the Big Ring|THE BIG RING}}. If you could see it, it would appear about fifteen times the width of the full Moon in our sky.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Two gigantic structures, side by side, at the same distance from us. Lopez said that neither one is easy to explain with our current understanding of the universe. [pause] And their shapes are almost too regular.

> i: quasar | v: quasar
They are not the first rule-breakers. In 2013, astronomers reported a cluster of seventy three quasars, the blazing cores of distant galaxies, grouped together in a structure about four billion light-years long. It was called the Huge Large Quasar Group. [pause] Other researchers argued it might be a chance arrangement. The argument has never fully been settled.

## Breaking The Rules
mood: dread
> i: early universe | v: early universe
Why does size matter? Because one of the most important assumptions in all of cosmology is called the {{cosmological principle|COSMOLOGICAL PRINCIPLE}}. It says that, on the largest scales, the universe looks roughly the same everywhere and in every direction. No special places. No giant patterns.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Calculations suggest that, if this principle holds, there should be a limit to how big structures can grow in the time the universe has existed. That limit is around a billion light-years or so. Beyond it, things should start to look smooth and even, like the surface of a calm sea seen from a plane.

> i: big ring | v: cosmic web
The Giant Arc, the Big Ring and the Hercules-Corona Borealis Great Wall all seem to go beyond that limit. If they are real, they could mean our standard model of the universe is missing something. [pause] Perhaps something about how structures form. Perhaps something about the early universe itself.

> i: galaxy survey map | v: cosmic web
Many astronomers are cautious. Spotting patterns in a sky full of galaxies is tricky, and our brains, and our statistics, can find shapes in random noise. New, larger surveys will test whether these giants are real. [pause] But the debate goes to the heart of what kind of universe we live in.

## Our Place In The Web
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Computer simulations of the universe, starting from the tiny ripples seen in the oldest light, produce something beautiful. A web of glowing filaments, threaded with galaxies, wrapped around enormous voids. It looks almost like a living thing, like the network of neurons in a brain.

> i: dark matter | v: dark matter
That web is mostly made of dark matter, invisible but heavy, pulling ordinary matter along its strands. Galaxies form where the strands meet. We, and everything we have ever seen, are like dew drops on the threads of a gigantic spider's web.

> i: cosmic void | v: cosmic web
And between the threads lie the voids. Enormous bubbles of almost nothing, some of them hundreds of millions of light-years across, containing only a handful of galaxies where we would expect thousands. Most of the volume of the universe is empty. [pause] The giants and the voids are two sides of the same pattern, written into the universe in its first moments.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
So here is our address. A small rocky planet, around an ordinary star, in one arm of the Milky Way, in the Local Group, on the edge of the Virgo Cluster, inside the Laniakea supercluster, in one strand of a cosmic web billions of light-years across. [pause] We are very small. And the structures we have found are so big they may be teaching us that we still do not understand the shape of the universe.
