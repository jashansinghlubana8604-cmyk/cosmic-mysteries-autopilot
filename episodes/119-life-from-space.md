About four billion years ago, the young Earth was a violent place. Volcanoes everywhere. Oceans forming. And a constant rain of rocks falling from space, asteroids and comets, smashing into the surface.

> i: meteorite | v: meteor shower
For a long time, scientists thought of those impacts as purely destructive. But now we know that many of those space rocks carried something precious. Water. Carbon. And the chemical building blocks of life.

> i: bennu sample | v: asteroid
In 2023, a NASA spacecraft returned a sample from an asteroid called Bennu. When scientists studied it, they found amino acids, the components of proteins, and all five of the bases used to build DNA and RNA. [pause] The recipe for life, sealed inside a rock from space.

> i: asteroid bennu | v: asteroid
So did life on Earth begin here, using ingredients delivered from space? Or did life itself arrive, already alive, from somewhere else? [pause] It is one of the oldest and boldest ideas in science. And it is called {{panspermia|PANSPERMIA}}.

[title]

## An Ancient Idea
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky
The idea goes back more than two thousand years. The Greek philosopher Anaxagoras suggested that the seeds of life were scattered throughout the universe. The word panspermia means seeds everywhere.

> i: physics laboratory | v: physics laboratory
In 1903, the Swedish Nobel Prize-winning chemist Svante Arrhenius proposed that tiny spores could drift between planets, pushed along by the pressure of starlight. In the 1970s, the astronomers Fred Hoyle and Chandra Wickramasinghe argued that life might be common in comets.

> i: dna helix | v: dna helix
Even Francis Crick, co-discoverer of the structure of DNA, explored a more radical version with Leslie Orgel in 1973, called directed panspermia. They speculated that an advanced civilisation might have deliberately seeded the Earth with life. [pause] He presented it as a provocative possibility, not as something he could prove.

## The Meteorite That Fell In Australia
mood: wonder
> i: meteorite | v: meteor
On the twenty eighth of September 1969, a bright fireball streaked across the sky over the town of {{Murchison|MURCHISON}}, in Australia. It broke apart and scattered fragments over a wide area. People picked up more than a hundred kilograms of dark, crumbly stone that smelled faintly of methylated spirits.

> i: meteorite | v: physics laboratory
It was a rare type of meteorite rich in carbon, almost unchanged since the birth of the solar system. And it fell just months after the Apollo 11 Moon landing, when laboratories had been prepared to analyse lunar samples for organic chemicals without contaminating them. [pause] The timing was perfect.

> i: amino acids | v: dna helix
Scientists found more than seventy different amino acids inside it, many of them never seen in living things on Earth, which showed they were not contamination. Later studies found nucleobases, the building blocks of genetic material, and even ribose, the sugar at the heart of RNA. [pause] A rock from space was full of the chemistry of life.

## Fresh From An Asteroid
mood: mystery
> i: osiris-rex | v: osiris-rex
Meteorites have a problem. Once they land on Earth, they can be contaminated by our own biology. So scientists went to the source. NASA's {{OSIRIS-REx|OSIRIS-REx}} spacecraft travelled to the asteroid Bennu, briefly touched its surface in 2020, collected rocks and dust, and returned them to Earth in a sealed capsule in September 2023.

> i: bennu sample | v: lunar sample
The sample, about a hundred and twenty grams, was handled with extreme care in clean laboratories. In 2025, scientists reported what they found. Fourteen of the twenty amino acids that life on Earth uses to build proteins. All five nucleobases used in DNA and RNA. Large amounts of ammonia. [pause] Later studies reported finding sugars, including ribose, as well.

> i: bennu sample | v: asteroid
They also found salts and minerals that form when salty water evaporates, suggesting that the parent body of Bennu once had pockets of liquid brine where complex chemistry could happen. [pause] A frozen record of the chemistry that came before life.

> i: ryugu | v: asteroid
Japan's Hayabusa2 mission brought back samples from another asteroid, Ryugu, in 2020. In those, scientists also found amino acids and the nucleobase uracil. Asteroids, it seems, are natural chemistry labs, cooking up the ingredients of life all across the solar system.

## Could Life Itself Travel?
mood: mystery
> i: mars meteorite | v: meteor
Ingredients are one thing. Living organisms are another. Could actual microbes survive a journey through space? We know that rocks do travel between planets. More than three hundred meteorites found on Earth came from Mars, blasted off its surface by impacts.

> i: tardigrade | v: microscope
Some living things are astonishingly tough. Tardigrades, tiny animals less than a millimetre long, have survived exposure to the vacuum and radiation of space. A hardy bacterium called {{Deinococcus radiodurans|DEINOCOCCUS RADIODURANS}} survived for three years outside the International Space Station, in clumps where the outer cells shielded the inner ones.

> i: international space station | v: international space station
Inside a large rock, shielded from radiation, some microbes might survive the millions of years it could take to travel between planets, and even the fiery entry into a new atmosphere. [pause] It has never been shown to happen. But it may not be impossible.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
Some scientists have even wondered whether life could have started on Mars, which may have been habitable early on, and later travelled to Earth inside a meteorite. If that were true, we might all be Martians. [pause] It is speculation. But it is taken seriously enough to be studied.

## Where Life Began
mood: wonder
> i: early earth | v: early earth
Most scientists think life probably began on Earth itself. But the discoveries from meteorites and asteroids show that its ingredients were not rare. They were made in space, carried on asteroids and comets, and delivered to young planets in huge amounts.

> i: amino acids | v: dna helix
There is one more intriguing clue. Molecules like amino acids come in left-handed and right-handed versions, mirror images of each other. Life on Earth uses almost only left-handed amino acids. Some meteorites contain a slight excess of left-handed ones too. [pause] Could space have given life its preference, before life even existed?

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
If the building blocks of life fall from the sky onto every young rocky planet, then life may not need a miracle to begin. It may only need the right place, enough time, and water. [pause] The atoms in your body were made in stars. Some of the molecules that started life may have been made in asteroids. In every sense, we are children of space.
