You have probably heard that most of the universe is made of mysterious dark matter and dark energy. Ordinary matter, the stuff of atoms, of stars, planets and people, is only about five percent of everything.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
But here is something less well known. For decades, astronomers could not even find all of that five percent. When they added up all the stars, all the galaxies, all the gas they could see, roughly half of the ordinary matter that should exist was simply missing.

> i: intergalactic gas | v: cosmic web
Not dark matter. Normal matter. Hydrogen, helium, the same atoms that make up your body. Calculations of the early universe said exactly how much there should be. And today, half of it seemed to have vanished. [pause] It was called the missing baryon problem.

> i: fast radio burst | v: radio telescope night
It took decades and many clever tricks to solve. And in the end, part of the answer came from some of the strangest signals in the sky, flashes of radio waves from distant galaxies. [pause] They counted the missing atoms, one by one.

## How Much Should There Be?
mood: mystery
> i: big bang | v: big bang
Ordinary matter is made of protons and neutrons, particles that physicists call {{baryons|BARYONS}}. The name comes from the Greek word for heavy. Almost all the mass of every atom, including every atom in your body, is in its baryons. How many baryons the universe contains was set in the first few minutes after the Big Bang.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Two completely independent measurements agree on the amount. One comes from the light elements forged in those first minutes, like deuterium. The other comes from the pattern of tiny ripples in the cosmic microwave background, the oldest light in the universe. Both say ordinary matter makes up about five percent of the total.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
And when astronomers look at the very distant universe, billions of years ago, they can account for it. Back then, most of the ordinary matter was in cool clouds of hydrogen gas between galaxies, which absorb light from distant quasars and leave clear fingerprints. [pause] The problem was the universe near us, today.

## The Accounting Problem
mood: mystery
> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
In the nearby universe, astronomers counted everything they could. The stars in galaxies. The cold gas between stars. The hot gas glowing in X-rays inside galaxy clusters. Even the dust.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
Stars, it turned out, contain only a small fraction of the ordinary matter in the universe. Most of it was supposed to be gas. But even after adding up all the gas they could detect, around half of the expected matter was unaccounted for.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Computer simulations suggested where it might be hiding. As the universe evolved, gas fell along the strands of the cosmic web, the vast network of filaments that connects galaxies. As it fell, it was heated by shocks to between about a hundred thousand and ten million degrees. [pause] Too hot to be seen as cool gas. Too thin and too cool to glow brightly in X-rays.

> i: intergalactic gas | v: cosmic web
Astronomers called it the {{warm-hot intergalactic medium|WARM-HOT GAS}}. It was predicted to be there, stretched across millions of light-years. But it was almost invisible. [pause] Finding it would be like trying to see a faint mist spread across an entire continent.

## Catching The Mist
mood: wonder
> i: quasar | v: quasar
One approach was to use bright, distant objects as backlights. When light from a quasar passes through hot gas on its way to us, specific elements in the gas can absorb particular wavelengths, leaving tiny dips in the spectrum. In 2018, a team reported X-ray absorption by highly charged oxygen in gas along one such line of sight, consistent with the missing material.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Another approach used the oldest light in the universe. When the cosmic microwave background passes through hot gas, its photons get a tiny energy boost from fast-moving electrons. By stacking together observations of many thousands of filaments between pairs of galaxies, teams in 2019 detected that faint distortion.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Each method found evidence of the hidden gas. But each had limitations, and there was still uncertainty about exactly how much matter was there. [pause] What astronomers really needed was a way to count every electron along a path through the universe.

> i: chandra xray | v: chandra xray
Part of the reason the search took so long is that our X-ray telescopes are not quite sensitive enough to see this faint gas directly across the sky. The European Space Agency is planning a much larger X-ray observatory for the late 2030s, designed partly to map this hidden gas in detail. [pause] Until then, astronomers have had to be clever.

## Flashes That Weigh The Universe
mood: wonder
> i: fast radio burst | v: radio telescope night
That is where {{fast radio bursts|FAST RADIO BURSTS}} come in. These are brief flashes of radio waves, lasting just thousandths of a second, from galaxies far across the universe. Their cause is still debated, but many may come from magnetars.

> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope
As a burst travels through space, it passes through every electron along its path. Free electrons slow down lower radio frequencies slightly more than higher ones. So the low notes of the burst arrive a fraction of a second after the high notes. [pause] The size of that delay tells you how many electrons the burst passed through.

> i: askap | v: radio telescope
In 2020, a team led by astronomer Jean-Pierre Macquart used Australian radio telescopes to pinpoint several bursts to their host galaxies, so they knew how far each signal had travelled. When they compared the delays with the distances, the numbers matched the full amount of ordinary matter predicted by cosmology. [pause] The missing baryons were there.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
In 2025, another team used dozens of precisely located bursts to map where it is. They found that around three quarters of the ordinary matter in the universe sits in the thin gas between galaxies, and much of the rest in hot halos around them. [pause] Only a small fraction is in stars, planets and people.

## What It Means
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Solving the missing baryon problem was a triumph for cosmology. It confirmed that our picture of the early universe, built from the first few minutes and the oldest light, correctly predicts what is around us today.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
It also changes how we think about galaxies. They are not the main repository of matter. They are bright islands in a vast, dim ocean of gas. And powerful winds from supernovae and black holes seem to push large amounts of gas out of galaxies into that ocean.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
And some of it falls back in. The gas surrounding a galaxy acts like a reservoir, slowly cooling and raining back down to feed new generations of stars. Galaxies breathe out and breathe in, over hundreds of millions of years. [pause] The missing matter turns out to be part of how galaxies live.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
So the next time you think of the universe as stars and galaxies, remember that most of the ordinary matter is somewhere else entirely, in a faint, hot mist stretched between them, almost invisible. [pause] It was missing for decades. And it was found by counting the electrons that a flash of radio waves passed on its way to us.
