In January 1992, a young astronomer stood up at a scientific meeting in the United States and announced something that people had been hoping to hear for centuries. He had found planets orbiting another star.

> i: arecibo telescope | v: radio telescope
It was the first confirmed discovery of planets beyond our solar system. But they were not orbiting a star like the Sun. [pause] They were circling the collapsed core of a star that had exploded.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
A spinning ball of crushed matter, about the size of a city, firing beams of radiation into space hundreds of times every second. Around it, at least three worlds, bathed not in sunlight but in a storm of particles.

> i: pulsar planets | v: exoplanet
Nobody expected the first alien planets to be found around a dead star. Nobody could explain how they survived. [pause] And more than thirty years later, they remain some of the strangest worlds we know.

## The Cosmic Clock
mood: wonder
> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
When a massive star runs out of fuel, its core collapses in less than a second, and its outer layers are blasted away in a supernova. What remains can be a {{neutron star|NEUTRON STAR}}, more massive than the Sun but only about twenty kilometres across.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar beam
Many neutron stars spin rapidly and sweep beams of radio waves across the sky. If a beam passes over the Earth, we see a regular pulse, like the flash of a lighthouse. These are called {{pulsars|PULSARS}}, and they were first discovered by Jocelyn Bell Burnell in 1967.

> i: millisecond pulsar | v: pulsar
Some pulsars spin astonishingly fast, hundreds of times per second. They are among the most precise clocks in nature, rivalling atomic clocks on Earth. [pause] And that precision is what made the discovery possible.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope
If something tugs on a pulsar, making it move slightly towards us and then away, its pulses arrive a tiny bit early, then a tiny bit late. By timing the pulses to within millionths of a second, astronomers can detect even very small wobbles. [pause] Including the pull of a planet.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
The effect is astonishingly small. A planet the size of the Earth makes a pulsar sway back and forth at only a few millimetres per second, slower than a snail. Over a distance of thousands of light-years, astronomers can still measure it. [pause] No other method of the time could have found planets so small.

## A Retraction And A Discovery
mood: mystery
> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope dish
The story begins with a mistake. In 1991, a team in Britain led by Andrew Lyne announced a planet orbiting a pulsar. It made headlines around the world. [pause] But a few months later, Lyne realised the signal was an error caused by not correctly accounting for the Earth's own orbit.

> i: arecibo telescope | v: arecibo telescope
At the same meeting in January 1992, he stood up and told everyone that his planet did not exist. The audience gave him a standing ovation for his honesty. [pause] And then, the next speaker was Aleksander Wolszczan.

> i: arecibo telescope | v: radio telescope
Working with the giant {{Arecibo|ARECIBO}} radio telescope in Puerto Rico, Wolszczan had been studying a pulsar now called PSR B1257+12, about two thousand three hundred light-years away. It spins about one hundred and sixty times every second. With his colleague Dale Frail, he had found patterns in its timing that could only be explained by two planets orbiting it.

> i: pulsar planets | v: exoplanet orbit
This time, the result held up. Later, a third, smaller planet was found. Then the researchers did something no one had done before: they showed that the two larger planets were tugging on each other, exactly as theory predicted. [pause] It was proof beyond doubt. The first {{exoplanets|FIRST EXOPLANETS}} had been found.

## Three Haunted Worlds
mood: dread
> i: pulsar planet artist | v: exoplanet
In 2015, the public was invited to name them. The pulsar became {{Lich|LICH}}, an undead creature from fantasy stories. Its planets were named {{Draugr|DRAUGR}}, {{Poltergeist|POLTERGEIST}} and {{Phobetor|PHOBETOR}}, after creatures of myth and the god of nightmares. [pause] The names fit.

> i: exoplanet orbit | v: exoplanet orbit
The system is also strangely tidy. All three planets seem to orbit close to the same plane, like the planets around our Sun, and some studies suggested there may even be a thin belt of asteroids or dust further out. [pause] A whole little solar system, rebuilt around a corpse.

> i: pulsar planets | v: exoplanet orbit
Draugr, the innermost, is one of the smallest planets ever found, only about twice the mass of our Moon. It orbits in just twenty five days. Poltergeist and Phobetor are each about four times the mass of the Earth, circling in about two and three months.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
They receive almost no visible light. Instead, the pulsar blasts them with X-rays, high-energy particles and a powerful wind of charged matter. [pause] Any atmosphere would be constantly battered.

> i: aurora | v: aurora
Some scientists have imagined that, if they have magnetic fields, these planets could be wrapped in brilliant auroras, glowing in skies with no sun. [pause] Life as we know it seems very unlikely there. But no one has seen these worlds directly. Everything we know comes from the ticking of a dead star.

## How Did They Survive?
mood: mystery
> i: supernova explosion | v: supernova explosion
The great puzzle is how planets could exist there at all. A supernova is one of the most violent events in the universe. Any planets orbiting the original star would likely have been destroyed or flung away into space.

> i: dust disk | v: protoplanetary disk
So most astronomers think these are {{second-generation planets|SECOND GENERATION}}. After the explosion, a disc of debris may have formed around the pulsar, and new planets assembled from it, the way planets form around young stars.

> i: binary star | v: binary star
That debris might have come from a companion star. Fast-spinning pulsars like this one are thought to be spun up by stealing matter from a partner. In some cases, the pulsar may destroy its companion completely, leaving a disc of its remains. [pause] The planets might be built from the wreckage of a star.

> i: millisecond pulsar | v: pulsar
And these worlds are rare. Astronomers have timed hundreds of fast pulsars, and only a handful show signs of planets. A survey published in 2022 estimated that fewer than one in a hundred have them. [pause] We found the rarest kind of planet first.

> i: arecibo telescope | v: arecibo telescope
The telescope that found them is gone too. In December 2020, after cables snapped, the instrument platform of the Arecibo telescope crashed into its dish. [pause] But its timing records of the dead star and its worlds remain one of its great legacies.

## Even Stranger Worlds
mood: epic
> i: globular cluster | v: globular cluster
Other pulsar worlds have since turned up. In the ancient star cluster Messier 4, a pulsar is orbited by a white dwarf and a planet about two and a half times the mass of Jupiter. It is nicknamed the {{Methuselah planet|METHUSELAH}}, because it is thought to be around twelve billion years old.

> i: diamond planet | v: exoplanet
In 2011, astronomers found an object orbiting another pulsar every two hours and ten minutes. It has about the mass of Jupiter, but must be very small and dense. It may be the leftover core of a star that the pulsar stripped away, rich in carbon, perhaps crushed into crystal. [pause] The press called it the {{diamond planet|DIAMOND PLANET}}.

> i: exoplanet | v: exoplanet
Three years after Wolszczan's discovery, in 1995, astronomers in Switzerland found the first planet orbiting a normal star like the Sun. That discovery later won a Nobel Prize, and opened the flood. [pause] Today more than six thousand exoplanets are known.

> i: arecibo telescope | v: radio telescope
But the very first ones came from a dead star, discovered by listening to its ticking with a radio telescope. [pause] Before we found a single world like ours, we found ghosts circling a stellar corpse. It was the universe's first hint that planets form almost anywhere, even in the most unlikely places.
