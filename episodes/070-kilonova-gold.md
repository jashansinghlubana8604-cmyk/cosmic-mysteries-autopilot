> i: neutron star merger | v: neutron star merger
For decades, scientists argued about where gold could possibly come from. Normal stars cannot make it. Even most exploding stars seem to struggle. [pause] It needed something more extreme. A collision between two of the densest objects in the universe.

> i: kilonova | v: kilonova

## Stars Cannot Make Gold
mood: mystery
> i: sun | v: sun
Stars are element factories. Our Sun turns hydrogen into helium. Bigger stars go further, building carbon, oxygen, neon, silicon, all the way to iron. Every atom of oxygen you breathe and carbon in your cells was forged inside a star.

> i: star core | v: star interior
But iron is where ordinary fusion stops. Making elements heavier than iron does not release energy. It costs energy. So stars cannot simply keep fusing their way up to gold, platinum or uranium.

> i: neutron | v: particle collision
To build those, you need a different trick. You need to bombard atoms with a flood of {{neutrons|NEUTRONS}}, so many and so fast that nuclei swallow them before they have time to decay. Physicists call it the rapid neutron-capture process, or the r-process.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
That requires an environment absurdly rich in neutrons. For a long time, the main suspect was supernovae, exploding stars. But detailed models struggled to make enough gold that way. [pause] Something else had to be responsible.

## Two Dead Stars
mood: dread
> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
Enter neutron stars. The collapsed cores of giant stars, each heavier than the Sun but only about twenty kilometres across. Made almost entirely of neutrons. A teaspoon would weigh about a billion tonnes.

> i: binary neutron star | v: neutron star merger
Sometimes two of them orbit each other. As they circle, they ripple space and time, sending out gravitational waves that carry away energy. Over millions of years, they spiral closer and closer.

> i: neutron star merger | v: neutron star merger
In their final seconds, they whirl around each other hundreds of times a second, then smash together. In the collision, a huge spray of neutron-rich matter is flung out into space. [pause] The perfect conditions for the r-process. In seconds, that debris is transformed into heavy elements.

> i: kilonova | v: kilonova
The radioactive new elements then decay and heat the expanding cloud, which glows for days. Scientists predicted this glow and called it a {{kilonova|KILONOVA}}, because it should shine about a thousand times brighter than an ordinary nova. [pause] But nobody had ever clearly seen one. Until 2017.

## The Day Space Shook
mood: wonder
> i: ligo | v: ligo
At 12:41 universal time on the seventeenth of August 2017, the two LIGO detectors in the United States and the Virgo detector in Italy felt a signal. A ripple in space itself, rising in pitch for around a hundred seconds. Two neutron stars, spiralling together and colliding.

> i: ligo | v: ligo
To catch it, LIGO measures the length of two four-kilometre-long laser arms with almost unbelievable precision. A passing gravitational wave stretches one arm and squeezes the other by less than a ten-thousandth of the width of a proton. [pause] The detectors felt two dead stars collide, from one hundred and thirty million light-years away, as a tremble smaller than an atomic nucleus.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
Just one point seven seconds after the collision signal, NASA's Fermi satellite detected a short burst of gamma rays from the same part of the sky. For the first time, a collision had been both felt and seen. The event was named {{GW170817|GW170817}}.

> i: ngc 4993 | v: galaxy zoom
The gravitational-wave detectors narrowed down where to look. About eleven hours later, a telescope in Chile spotted a new point of light in a galaxy called NGC 4993, about one hundred and thirty million light-years away. Around seventy observatories around the world, and in space, turned to watch it.

> i: kilonova | v: kilonova
It was a kilonova. And it behaved exactly as predicted. It started hot and blue, then faded and reddened over days, as heavy elements in the cloud blocked the bluer light. [pause] The signature of freshly made gold, platinum and other heavy metals.

## How Much Gold?
mood: wonder
> i: gold | v: gold
By studying the kilonova's light, scientists estimated how much heavy material the collision made. The answer was staggering. Many times the mass of the Earth in heavy elements. Some estimates suggested {{several Earths' worth of gold|EARTHS OF GOLD}} alone.

> i: kilonova | v: neutron star merger
Later, astronomers identified a specific element in the debris: strontium. And in 2023, Webb studied another neutron star collision and spotted the fingerprint of tellurium, an element heavier than iron, in the glow. [pause] We were watching the periodic table being written.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
The event also settled a deep question about physics. The gravitational waves and the gamma rays had raced each other across a hundred and thirty million light-years and arrived less than two seconds apart. [pause] Gravity travels at the speed of light, to an accuracy better than one part in a million billion.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
And what was left behind? The merged object was probably too heavy to stay a neutron star for long, and likely collapsed into a black hole. Radio telescopes tracked the aftermath for years, and found a narrow jet of material that had punched out of the debris at nearly the speed of light. [pause] One collision, and the whole story of a black hole's birth, written across the sky.

## Stardust On Your Finger
mood: wonder
> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
Neutron star collisions are probably not the only source. Some scientists think certain rare, rapidly spinning collapsing stars, and even giant flares from magnetars, may also forge heavy elements. But collisions like GW170817 are now thought to be a major one.

> i: human body | v: dna helix
And it is not only gold. The same process makes platinum, silver, iodine and uranium. Your body needs iodine to make thyroid hormones that control how you grow and how you burn energy. [pause] Some of the chemistry keeping you alive right now was forged in the crash of two dead stars.

> i: solar system formation | v: solar system formation
Long before the Sun formed, collisions like this one happened in our galaxy, seeding the clouds of gas with gold, platinum, iodine and uranium. About four and a half billion years ago, one of those enriched clouds collapsed to form our solar system.

> i: earth formation | v: early earth
When the Earth was young and molten, most of its gold sank deep into the iron core, out of reach. The gold we mine today may largely have arrived later, delivered by meteorites that pounded the surface after the crust had formed.

> i: gold | v: gold
So the gold in a wedding ring has taken an extraordinary journey. Forged in the collision of two dead stars. Scattered across the galaxy. Swept into the cloud that became our Sun. Delivered to the Earth by falling rocks. Dug from the ground. [pause] And placed on a finger, as a promise. It is, quite literally, made of stardust. Of the most violent kind.
