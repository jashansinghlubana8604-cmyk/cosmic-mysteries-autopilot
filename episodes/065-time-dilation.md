## The Moving Clock
mood: mystery
> i: albert einstein | v: clock
In 1905, a twenty six year old patent clerk named Albert Einstein published an idea that broke common sense. The speed of light is the same for everyone, no matter how fast they are moving. To make that work, something else had to give. [pause] Time itself.

> i: clock | v: clock
Einstein showed that a moving clock ticks more slowly than one at rest, as seen by the person standing still. At everyday speeds, the difference is unimaginably small. But as you approach the speed of light, it grows without limit. This is {{time dilation|TIME DILATION}}.

> i: spacecraft flight | v: spacecraft flight
Imagine twins. One stays on Earth. The other flies to a distant star and back at ninety nine and a half percent of the speed of light. For the traveller, the trip takes about four years. When they land, they find their twin has aged around forty. [pause] Neither clock was broken. They simply lived through different amounts of time.

> i: cosmic rays | v: cosmic rays
We see this every day without noticing. Cosmic rays hitting the upper atmosphere create particles called muons, which should decay long before they reach the ground. But they move so fast that, from our point of view, their internal clocks slow down. [pause] So they survive the journey, and rain through your body right now.

## The Heavy Clock
mood: wonder
> i: spacetime curvature | v: spacetime curvature
Ten years later, Einstein found a second way to slow time. Gravity. In general relativity, mass bends space and time together. The deeper you are in a gravitational field, the more slowly your clock runs compared with one further out.

> i: atomic clock | v: physics laboratory
In 1971, two physicists, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating, put four atomic clocks on commercial passenger flights and flew them around the world, eastward and westward, then compared them with clocks left on the ground. The clocks disagreed by tiny amounts, billionths of a second. [pause] Exactly as Einstein's theory predicted.

> i: atomic clock | v: physics laboratory
Today's best atomic clocks are so precise that physicists can measure the difference in time between two clocks placed just one millimetre apart in height. Time really does run faster for your head than your feet. [pause] Over a lifetime, your head ages by roughly a hundred billionths of a second more.

> i: gps satellite | v: satellites orbit
And this is not just a curiosity. GPS satellites orbit high above the Earth, where gravity is weaker, so their clocks run faster, by about forty five millionths of a second a day. But they are also moving fast, which slows them by about seven. Overall, they gain around thirty eight millionths of a second every day. [pause] Without correcting for that, GPS positions would drift by around ten kilometres a day.

## Space Travellers
mood: wonder
> i: international space station | v: international space station
Astronauts aboard the International Space Station are moving at about twenty eight thousand kilometres an hour. Their speed slows their clocks more than the weaker gravity speeds them up. So they age slightly more slowly than everyone on the ground.

> i: astronaut | v: international space station
The effect is small. After six months in orbit, an astronaut has aged a few thousandths of a second less than if they had stayed home. The Russian cosmonaut {{Sergei Krikalev|SERGEI KRIKALEV}}, who spent more than eight hundred days in space across his career, is estimated to be around two hundredths of a second younger than he would otherwise be.

> i: astronaut | v: spacewalk
He has been called the greatest time traveller in history. [pause] He travelled two hundredths of a second into the future. And that is the most anyone has ever managed.

## The Edge Of A Black Hole
mood: dread
> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
Now take gravity to its limit. Near a black hole, space and time are bent so severely that clocks slow dramatically. And at the event horizon itself, the boundary of no return, something truly strange happens.

> i: black hole falling | v: black hole
If you watched a friend fall towards a black hole from far away, you would see their clock slow down, and their image grow redder and dimmer as they approached the edge. They would seem to freeze, hovering just above the horizon, fading away. [pause] You would never see them cross.

> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
But for your friend, nothing would seem unusual about their own clock. Their watch would tick normally. They would cross the horizon in what felt like an ordinary moment. Two people. Two completely different stories of the same event. [pause] Both correct.

> i: sagittarius a star orbits | v: stars orbiting black hole
We have measured this effect near a real black hole. In 2018, astronomers watched the star S2 swing close to Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the centre of our galaxy. As it passed, its light was stretched redder by the black hole's gravity, by exactly the amount relativity predicts. [pause] Einstein, again.

## Miller's Planet
mood: mystery
> i: black hole interstellar | v: black hole accretion disk
So could Interstellar's planet really exist? In the film, Miller's planet orbits a giant black hole called Gargantua, where one hour on the surface equals seven years far away. The physicist Kip Thorne, who advised the film, worked out what it would take.

> i: black hole spinning | v: black hole
The black hole would have to be enormous, around a hundred million times the mass of the Sun, and spinning almost as fast as is physically possible. The planet would have to orbit just outside the horizon, in a region where a spinning black hole allows stable orbits much closer than a still one. [pause] Under those extreme conditions, the numbers can work.

> i: exoplanet | v: exoplanet
Whether a planet could actually survive there, with the tides, the radiation and the glowing disc of gas, is much less certain. But the time dilation itself is real. [pause] Spend an hour there, and you could come home to find your children older than you.

> i: clock space | v: time lapse
We think of time as a steady river, flowing at the same speed for everyone. It is not. It flows faster on mountains than in valleys. Slower for travellers than for those who stay home. [pause] And at the edge of a black hole, it almost stops. Time is not the stage the universe is played on. It is part of the play.
