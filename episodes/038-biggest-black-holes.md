## The One At Home
mood: mystery
> i: milky way center | v: milky way center
Our starting point is right here, at the centre of the Milky Way, about twenty six thousand light-years away. A black hole called {{Sagittarius A*|SAGITTARIUS A*}}. It weighs about four million times as much as the Sun.

> i: sagittarius a star orbits | v: stars orbiting black hole
For decades, astronomers watched stars whipping around an empty point in the galactic centre at thousands of kilometres per second. One of them, a star called S2, completes an orbit every sixteen years. Only something incredibly massive and incredibly small could hold it. In 2020, that work won a Nobel Prize.

> i: sagittarius a black hole image | v: event horizon telescope
In 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope revealed its first image: a glowing ring of hot gas around a dark shadow. Its event horizon is about twenty four million kilometres across. If you dropped it into our solar system, it would fit comfortably inside the orbit of Mercury.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
Four million Suns, squeezed inside the orbit of the smallest planet. That sounds enormous. [pause] But in the world of supermassive black holes, Sagittarius A* is a lightweight. Almost a baby.

## The One We Photographed
mood: dread
> i: m87 galaxy | v: m87 jet
Fifty five million light-years away sits a giant elliptical galaxy called {{M87|M87}}. At its centre is a black hole about six and a half billion times the mass of the Sun. More than fifteen hundred times heavier than ours.

> i: m87 black hole | v: event horizon telescope
In April 2019, it became the first black hole ever photographed, by a network of radio telescopes spread across the whole Earth, working together as one planet-sized eye. The image showed a fiery orange ring around a perfect darkness.

> i: m87 jet | v: m87 jet
M87's black hole also fires a jet of particles out of the galaxy at nearly the speed of light. That jet is about five thousand light-years long. Longer than the distance from here to many of the stars you can see at night.

> i: solar system | v: solar system
And its size. The event horizon of M87's black hole is larger than the orbits of all the planets. Put it where the Sun is, and its edge would reach far beyond Neptune, far beyond Pluto. [pause] Voyager 1, after nearly fifty years of flying, would still be inside it.

## The Monsters
mood: dread
> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Go to the hearts of the biggest galaxies, at the centres of giant galaxy clusters, and the black holes get heavier still. In the Coma Cluster, the galaxy NGC 4889 holds a black hole estimated at around {{twenty billion Suns|20 BILLION SUNS}}.

> i: abell 85 | v: elliptical galaxy
In the cluster Abell 85, the galaxy Holmberg 15A has a strangely faint, swollen core, as if its middle has been scooped out. Astronomers think a giant black hole has been flinging stars away from the centre. When they weighed it in 2019, they found about forty billion times the mass of the Sun.

> i: cosmic horseshoe | v: gravitational lensing
And in 2025, a team studying a beautiful ring of distorted light called the {{Cosmic Horseshoe|COSMIC HORSESHOE}} found a black hole of around thirty six billion Suns. What makes that one special is how it was measured. Not guessed from how brightly it shines, but weighed directly, by how it bends light and moves stars.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
Forty billion Suns. Its event horizon would stretch across about two hundred and forty billion kilometres, many times wider than our whole planetary system. [pause] A darkness you could fly through for days at the speed of light.

> i: black hole | v: black hole falling
And here is the strangest thing about these giants. The bigger a black hole is, the gentler its edge. Near a small black hole, the difference in gravity between your head and your feet would tear you apart long before you reached the horizon. Near one of these monsters, you could drift across the point of no return without feeling anything at all. [pause] No warning. No pain. Just a door that has quietly closed behind you.

## The Uncertain Kings
mood: mystery
> i: quasar | v: quasar
Then there are black holes so far away that we cannot weigh them directly. We can only see the light pouring from the gas falling into them, and estimate their mass from how fast that gas moves. These estimates come with big error bars.

> i: ton 618 | v: quasar
The most famous is {{TON 618|TON 618}}, a quasar more than ten billion light-years away, blazing with the light of over a hundred trillion Suns. Some estimates put its black hole at around sixty six billion solar masses, although others come out lower.

> i: phoenix cluster | v: galaxy cluster
And in the Phoenix Cluster, about five point eight billion light-years away, some studies have suggested the central black hole, Phoenix A, could be as much as a hundred billion Suns. That number is based on indirect methods and is hotly debated. [pause] If it is right, it would be the heaviest black hole known.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole accretion disk
These figures are not certain. But even the cautious ones say the same thing. There are black holes out there weighing tens of billions of Suns. [pause] And that raises a terrifying question. How did they get so big?

## How Big Can They Get?
mood: mystery
> i: early universe | v: early universe
The universe is only about thirteen point eight billion years old. To build a black hole of tens of billions of Suns, you need to feed it relentlessly for a very long time. And Webb keeps finding giant black holes that existed only a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.

> i: uhz1 | v: chandra xray
One of them, spotted using Webb and the Chandra X-ray Observatory, seems to have been around ten million times the Sun's mass just four hundred and seventy million years after the beginning. [pause] Too big, too early, for the usual story to work.

> i: black hole formation | v: black hole
One idea is that some black holes were born big, from vast clouds of gas that collapsed directly, skipping the stage of being a star. Another is that they ate far faster than we thought possible. Nobody knows yet which is right.

> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
And there may be a ceiling. As a black hole grows, the disc of gas around it gets so large that its outer parts cool, clump together and form stars, instead of falling in. Calculations suggest a limit of around {{fifty billion Suns|~50 BILLION}} for most black holes, perhaps a few times more for those spinning at incredible speed.

> i: supermassive black hole | v: star field
Which means the biggest black holes we see may be sitting right at the edge of what nature allows. Enormous, dark and almost finished growing. [pause] The largest objects ever to collapse under their own weight, hiding at the heart of galaxies, waiting for whatever comes too close.
