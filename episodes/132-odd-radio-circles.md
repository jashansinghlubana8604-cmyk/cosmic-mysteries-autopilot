In 2019, astronomers in Australia were testing a new radio telescope, mapping the sky in radio waves. Among millions of faint radio sources, they found something they had never seen before. A perfect, faint ring.

> i: odd radio circle | v: radio telescope night
Then they found another. And another. Ghostly circles of radio light, with sharp outer edges and, in some cases, a galaxy sitting right in the middle. They did not appear in visible light, or in infrared, or in X-rays. Only in radio.

When the team worked out how big they were, the answer was staggering. Some of these rings are around a million light-years across. About ten times the width of our entire galaxy.

> i: odd radio circle | v: radio telescope
Nothing in astronomy predicted them. The discoverers gave them a deliberately simple name: {{odd radio circles|ODD RADIO CIRCLES}}. [pause] And years later, astronomers are still arguing about what makes them.

## A New Kind Of Object
mood: mystery
> i: askap telescope | v: radio telescope
The discovery came from {{ASKAP|ASKAP}}, the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder, a set of thirty six radio dishes in the remote outback of Western Australia. It was beginning a huge survey called EMU, the Evolutionary Map of the Universe, intended to chart tens of millions of radio sources.

> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
In the early test images, a member of the team spotted the first faint ring. The astronomer Ray Norris led the investigation. At first, the team considered every possible ordinary explanation. A fault in the telescope. A supernova remnant in our own galaxy. A ring of star formation in a distant galaxy. Lensed light bent by gravity.

> i: odd radio circle | v: radio telescope
None of them fitted. Supernova remnants in our own galaxy can form radio rings, like the remains of Cassiopeia A, but those also glow in X-rays and appear in other kinds of light. Planetary nebulae, the shells around dying Sun-like stars, form beautiful rings too, but they shine in visible light. The rings were too big, too faint, too round, and visible only in radio waves. When their findings were published in 2021, the team made the cautious but exciting claim. [pause] These were probably a new type of astronomical object.

> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
Radio telescopes see a different universe from the one our eyes see. Much of the radio sky is lit by electrons racing through magnetic fields at nearly the speed of light, a glow called synchrotron radiation. It reveals energetic events, shocks and magnetic fields that can be completely invisible to optical telescopes. [pause] The odd radio circles exist only in that hidden universe.

## How Big, How Far
mood: wonder
> i: odd radio circle meerkat | v: galaxy zoom
Some of the circles have an elliptical galaxy right at their centres. Assuming the galaxy and the ring belong together, astronomers can work out the distance, roughly a billion light-years or more away, and from that, the true size of the ring.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
The answer, for the best-studied examples, is roughly a million light-years across. Our Milky Way is around a hundred thousand light-years wide. [pause] You could line up about ten Milky Ways across one of these circles.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
And they are extraordinarily faint. Their radio signals are so weak that the total energy collected from one of them by a telescope on Earth, over a whole year, would be far less than the energy of a single falling snowflake. [pause] Finding them required both a sensitive telescope and a very sharp-eyed scientist.

> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope
In 2022, the South African MeerKAT telescope, one of the most sensitive radio telescopes in the world, produced a much sharper image of the first circle. It revealed faint inner rings and filaments inside the main ring, like an onion of radio light. [pause] It also showed that the radio emission comes from electrons spiralling around magnetic fields at almost the speed of light.

## What Could Make Them?
mood: mystery
> i: galaxy collision | v: galaxy merger
One leading idea is a gigantic shock wave. When two galaxies merge, their central black holes can also merge, releasing huge amounts of energy. That energy could drive a shock wave outward into the thin gas around the galaxy, lighting up a spherical shell of radio emission. Seen from far away, a shell looks like a ring.

> i: black hole jet | v: black hole jet
Another idea involves the jets fired by supermassive black holes. If we happened to be looking straight down one of these jets, end on, its lobe of radio-emitting gas might look like a circle. [pause] But that would require a lot of lucky alignments.

> i: starburst galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
A third idea points to galaxies that once formed stars at a furious rate. In a burst of star formation, countless supernovae and massive stars can blast out powerful winds. In 2023 and 2024, a team found that the galaxy at the centre of one circle had once driven an enormous outflow of gas. They proposed that, when such a wind slams into the surrounding gas, it creates a {{shock|A SHOCK WAVE}}, a ring visible in radio. [pause] Different circles might even have different causes.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Astronomers already knew of other strange radio structures. In collisions between whole clusters of galaxies, giant shock waves light up as curved arcs called radio relics, millions of light-years long. Odd radio circles might be a smaller, rounder cousin of the same physics. [pause] Shocks, it seems, are one of the universe's favourite ways to make something glow.

## Only A Handful
mood: wonder
> i: odd radio circle | v: radio telescope
So far, only a handful of odd radio circles have been confirmed. Some sit around a single galaxy. Some do not seem to have any galaxy at their centre at all. One pair of circles even appears to overlap.

> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope night
Citizen scientists are helping to find more. Volunteers looking through radio survey images have spotted candidates that professional astronomers then confirmed. In 2025, a team reported one of the most distant examples yet, a double ring found with the help of amateurs.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope
Part of the reason they went unnoticed for so long is that they are extremely faint. Earlier radio telescopes simply were not sensitive enough. [pause] It is a reminder that, every time we build a better instrument, the universe shows us something new.

## Why It Matters
mood: mystery
> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
If odd radio circles really are shock waves from galaxy mergers or galactic winds, they would give astronomers a new way to study how galaxies interact with the gas around them, and how much energy they inject into their surroundings. Shocks like these may also be natural particle accelerators, boosting electrons and other particles to enormous energies, perhaps contributing to the cosmic rays that rain down on the Earth. That is a key part of understanding how galaxies grow and evolve.

> i: square kilometre array | v: radio telescope
Much bigger radio telescopes are being built. The Square Kilometre Array, with dishes in South Africa and antennas in Australia, will be far more sensitive than anything before it. It could reveal hundreds or thousands of these circles, enough to finally work out what they are.

> i: odd radio circle | v: radio telescope night
For now, odd radio circles remain one of the strangest new discoveries in astronomy. Enormous, faint rings, a million light-years wide, invisible to every telescope except those listening in radio waves. [pause] The universe keeps reminding us that some of its biggest structures can hide in plain sight, until we learn how to listen.
