Twenty six thousand light-years from here, at the centre of our galaxy, there is a monster. A black hole about four million times heavier than the Sun. Everything in the Milky Way, including us, orbits around the region where it sits.

And yet, compared with the black holes at the centres of other galaxies, it is strangely quiet. Almost starving. [pause] But it was not always this calm. Around two hundred years ago, it seems to have flared up dramatically.

## Hidden Behind The Dust
mood: mystery
> i: milky way center | v: milky way time-lapse
On a dark night in the middle of the year, the Milky Way blazes brightest towards the constellation Sagittarius. That is the direction of our galaxy's centre. But you cannot see the centre itself. It is hidden behind enormous clouds of dust.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
In 1974, astronomers using radio telescopes found a compact, bright source of radio waves right at the galaxy's heart. It was given a name: Sagittarius A star. The star in the name was not a star. It meant something exciting, an important new object. Nobody could yet say what it was.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope
It was found by two young astronomers, Bruce Balick and Robert Brown, using radio dishes in West Virginia. Brown later explained the asterisk: in physics, an excited state of an atom is often marked with a star, and this source was exciting. [pause] Half a century later, we know just how right he was.

> i: infrared telescope | v: telescope dome
Infrared light can pass through dust more easily than visible light. So from the 1990s, two teams, one led by {{Reinhard Genzel|REINHARD GENZEL}} in Germany and one led by {{Andrea Ghez|ANDREA GHEZ}} in the United States, began watching the galactic centre in infrared, with some of the largest telescopes on Earth. They wanted to watch the stars move.

## The Stars That Proved It
mood: wonder
> i: stars orbiting black hole | v: stars orbiting black hole
Year after year, the teams tracked dozens of stars in the crowded heart of the galaxy. They had to correct for the blurring of the Earth's atmosphere, using techniques such as adaptive optics, which adjusts the telescope's mirror hundreds of times a second.

> i: sagittarius a star orbits | v: stars orbiting black hole
Slowly, the stars traced out orbits. And they were all circling the same invisible point. One star, called {{S2|S2}}, completes an orbit every sixteen years. At its closest, it passes about one hundred and twenty times the Earth-Sun distance from the centre, moving at over seven thousand kilometres per second.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
From those orbits, the teams could weigh what lay at the centre. About four million times the mass of the Sun, packed into a region smaller than our solar system. Nothing but a black hole could be that heavy and that small. [pause] In 2020, Genzel and Ghez shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

> i: sagittarius a star | v: stars orbiting black hole
And S2 kept giving. In 2018, as it swung close, an instrument called GRAVITY measured how the black hole's gravity stretched its light, exactly as Einstein predicted. In 2020, the team showed that S2's orbit slowly rotates in the way relativity says it should. [pause] A star, used as a probe, confirming Einstein next to a black hole.

## The Picture
mood: wonder
> i: sagittarius a black hole image | v: event horizon telescope
In May 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope, a network of radio dishes spread across the Earth, revealed the first image of Sagittarius A*. A glowing ring of hot gas around a dark shadow, about the size you would expect for a black hole of four million Suns.

> i: black hole shadow | v: black hole
From the Earth, that ring looks incredibly small, about the size a doughnut would appear if it were sitting on the surface of the Moon. The dark centre is not the black hole itself, but its shadow: a region where light that comes too close is captured or bent away. [pause] Around it, light can even circle the black hole before escaping towards us.

> i: sagittarius a black hole image | v: black hole
It was harder to capture than the first black hole image, of M87. Because Sagittarius A* is smaller, the gas around it orbits in minutes, changing shape while the telescopes are still collecting data. [pause] In 2024, the team released an image showing the polarisation of the light, revealing strong, organised magnetic fields spiralling around the edge.

## A Starving Giant
mood: mystery
> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
For a black hole of its size, Sagittarius A* is remarkably dim. It swallows only a tiny trickle of gas. If it were a person, it would be eating a grain of rice every few million years. Other supermassive black holes, in active galaxies, are feasting and blazing brighter than all their stars combined.

> i: black hole flare | v: black hole
But it does flicker. Several times a day, it flares in X-rays and infrared, as clumps of hot gas near the event horizon light up, perhaps when magnetic fields snap and reconnect. In 2019, astronomers saw it flare to around seventy five times its normal infrared brightness. [pause] Something had just fallen in, or been torn apart.

> i: gas cloud black hole | v: black hole
A few years earlier, astronomers had watched an object called G2 approach the black hole, expecting it to be shredded in a spectacular feast. It passed by mostly intact. It may have been a star wrapped in a cocoon of gas, too dense to rip apart. [pause] The monster did not even notice.

## The Echo Of An Outburst
mood: dread
> i: molecular cloud | v: nebula
And here is the most dramatic part. Around the galactic centre, there are giant clouds of gas and dust, light-years away from the black hole. Some of them glow in X-rays, even though they have no source of X-rays of their own.

> i: chandra xray | v: chandra xray
They are reflecting light. X-rays that left Sagittarius A* in the past are only now reaching these clouds, bouncing off them, and heading to us. A {{light echo|LIGHT ECHO}}. Studies of these echoes, including results published in 2024, suggest that around two hundred years ago, the black hole flared to as much as a million times its current brightness, for perhaps a year.

> i: fermi bubbles | v: milky way galaxy
And there are signs of something bigger, much further in the past. Two gigantic bubbles of hot gas, called the Fermi bubbles, stretch tens of thousands of light-years above and below the plane of our galaxy. Many astronomers think they were blown out by a powerful outburst from the black hole a few million years ago.

> i: milky way center | v: milky way center
So our black hole is not dead. It is resting. It wakes up, flares, feeds and goes quiet again, over timescales from hours to millions of years. [pause] If a large cloud of gas or a star wandered close enough, it could flare again. The monster at the centre of our galaxy is sleeping. But it sleeps lightly.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
Could a full awakening hurt us? At twenty six thousand light-years away, even a dramatic flare would pose no danger to the Earth. Its light would simply add a new glow to the heart of the Milky Way. [pause] We live at a safe distance from the monster. Close enough to study it. Far enough not to fear it.
