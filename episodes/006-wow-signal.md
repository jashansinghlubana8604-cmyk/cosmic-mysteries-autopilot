## A Word In Red Ink
mood: mystery
> c: Big Ear radio telescope | i: radio telescope array
The telescope was called Big Ear. It did not look like the giant dishes you see in films. It was a flat field of aluminium, about the size of three football pitches, lying on the ground near Delaware, Ohio, with two great reflectors standing at either end.

> v: star trails time-lapse | i: star trails
Big Ear could not steer. It simply stared upward, and let the rotation of the Earth drag the sky across its view. Any single point in space would drift through its beam in about seventy-two seconds.

> radio telescope array | i: radio telescope
By 1977 it had been given a strange new job. Ohio State University had turned it into one of the first full-time searches for extraterrestrial intelligence, the project known as {{SETI}}. Volunteers kept it running for free, night after night, for years.

> v: radio telescope antenna | i: radio telescope array night
Its results came out on paper. Every twelve seconds, a computer printed a column of characters, one for each radio channel. A blank meant nothing. A one meant a faint flicker. The numbers climbed to nine, and after nine, the computer switched to letters. The further down the alphabet, the stronger the signal.

> c: wow signal | i: radio waves
A few days after the night of August the fifteenth, a volunteer astronomer named Jerry Ehman sat at his kitchen table, working through a stack of these printouts. Page after page of ones and twos. The quiet noise of an empty sky.

> v: pulsar | i: pulsar

## Why It Was Impossible To Ignore
mood: wonder
> v: hydrogen gas cloud | hydrogen gas nebula
To understand why astronomers still argue about this signal, you need to know about one very special frequency. [pause] Hydrogen is the most common element in the universe. When cold hydrogen gas floats in space, it gives off a faint radio whisper at exactly {{1,420 megahertz|1420 MHz}}.

> i: hydrogen map milky way | v: galaxy gas simulation
Every radio astronomer on Earth knows this frequency. In 1959, two physicists, Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison, published a famous paper with a bold suggestion. If alien civilisations wanted to be found, they would likely broadcast near the hydrogen line, because any species that studies the sky would know about it.

> radio telescope night | radio dishes stars
Even more striking, that part of the spectrum is protected by international agreement. Transmitters on Earth are not allowed to use it, precisely so that astronomers can listen in silence. So when the Wow! signal appeared, it was sitting in one of the quietest places in the radio sky.

> v: earth rotation | i: earth from space night
Then there is its shape. A star, a galaxy, or a transmitter fixed somewhere in space would rise and fall in a very particular way as the Earth turned it through Big Ear's beam. A smooth climb, a peak, a smooth decline, lasting about seventy-two seconds.

> v: observatory time-lapse | i: star trails observatory
That is exactly what the Wow! signal did. It was not a random burst of static, and it did not behave like a passing aircraft or a satellite. It behaved like something {{out among the stars|DEEP SPACE}}.

> v: radio telescope dish | i: radio telescope
It was also narrow. Natural radio sources usually smear their energy across a wide range of frequencies. The Wow! signal was packed into a single channel, just ten kilohertz wide. That is the kind of signal engineers build on purpose.

## The Horn That Heard Nothing

Big Ear listened through two receivers, called feed horns, pointed at slightly different parts of the sky. As the Earth turned, any source should have passed through the first horn, and then through the second, about three minutes later.

## The Explanations
mood: wonder
> v: comet tail | i: comet
So what was it? The easy answers fall apart quickly.

> v: earth from space | i: earth night from space
A satellite? None were known to be in that position, and few would be broadcasting in that protected band. A reflection of a signal from Earth, bouncing off space debris? Possible, but it would be unlikely to produce such a clean, star-like shape.

> v: comet nucleus | i: comet nucleus
In 2017, one researcher suggested two comets, which were near that part of the sky in 1977 and carry clouds of hydrogen. It made headlines around the world. But most radio astronomers rejected it. Comets do not shine brightly at that frequency, and they would not explain a signal this strong.

> v: sun-like star | i: sun-like star
In 2020, an amateur astronomer searched the star catalogues and found a single Sun-like star inside the region, about {{1,800 light years|1,800 LY}} away. No one knows if it has planets. It is a name on a list, and nothing more.

> v: magnetar flare | i: magnetar artist impression
Then, in 2024, came the most compelling natural explanation so far. A team led by Abel Méndez, who studies habitable worlds, proposed that the signal came from a cold cloud of hydrogen gas. A sudden burst of energy from a powerful star, like a magnetar flare, could make that cloud briefly flare up at the hydrogen frequency, like a neon sign switched on for a moment.

> v: hydrogen gas cloud | i: dark nebula
They even found weaker signals with a similar character in old data from the Arecibo telescope. If they are right, the Wow! signal was not a message at all. It was the rarest possible kind of natural flash, caught by luck, on the one night someone was watching.

> v: radio telescope | i: radio telescope array
And yet, no one has ever seen a cloud do this. A recent reanalysis of the original Big Ear records found that the signal was even more powerful than first believed. The natural explanation is promising. [pause] It is not proven.
