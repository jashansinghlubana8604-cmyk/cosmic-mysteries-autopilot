When the James Webb Space Telescope began staring into the deep universe in 2022, astronomers expected to see young galaxies. Baby galaxies, faint and messy, still putting themselves together a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.

> i: little red dots webb | v: deep field zoom
They found those. But they also found something else. Tiny, intensely red points of light, scattered through the deep images. Far too compact to be ordinary galaxies. Far too red. And far too many of them.

> i: early universe galaxy | v: early universe
At first, some astronomers thought they might be galaxies so packed with stars that they should not exist yet, so early in cosmic history. Others thought they were black holes. Neither explanation quite fit. [pause] They became known simply as the {{little red dots|LITTLE RED DOTS}}.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
And as Webb looked closer, a stranger idea emerged. Some of them might be a kind of object no one had ever seen before. [pause] A black hole wrapped inside something like a star.

## Dots That Should Not Be There
mood: mystery
> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
Webb sees in infrared light, which lets it catch light from the most distant galaxies. That light has been stretched by the expansion of the universe, from visible and ultraviolet into infrared, during its journey of more than twelve billion years.

> i: little red dots webb | v: deep field zoom
In the first deep surveys, astronomers noticed compact red objects that seemed to appear mostly between about six hundred million and one and a half billion years after the Big Bang. Then, in later cosmic history, they seemed to almost vanish. [pause] A population that came and went.

> i: webb deep field | v: webb deep field zoom
They turned up in survey after survey, with names like JADES, CEERS and UNCOVER, each staring at small patches of sky for many hours. Hundreds have now been catalogued. And by some estimates, in that early era, they were far more common than the bright quasars astronomers already knew about. [pause] Whatever they are, they were not rare. They were a normal part of the young universe.

> i: webb deep field | v: hubble deep field
Some of them looked so bright and so red that, if they were made only of stars, they would contain as much mass as our Milky Way, packed into a region a tiny fraction of its size. In 2023, one team called some of these candidates {{universe breakers|UNIVERSE BREAKERS}}. [pause] Our models of the early universe could not build galaxies that big, that fast.

> i: early universe galaxy | v: early universe
But the colours were odd. Red in some parts of the spectrum, but strangely blue in others, as if two different kinds of light were mixed together. Something unusual was going on inside them.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
And they were tiny. Our Milky Way is around a hundred thousand light-years across. Many of the little red dots appear to be just a few hundred light-years wide, or even smaller, too small for Webb to resolve at all. [pause] Imagine the light of a whole galaxy pouring out of a region you could cross in a few centuries at the speed of light.

## The Black Hole Clue
mood: mystery
> i: quasar | v: quasar
When astronomers split the light of the red dots into spectra, many showed a telltale sign: broad emission lines from hydrogen gas, smeared out because the gas was swirling at thousands of kilometres per second. On Earth's telescopes, that is the classic signature of gas orbiting a growing black hole.

> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
So perhaps the little red dots are not giant star-packed galaxies. Perhaps they are young {{supermassive black holes|SUPERMASSIVE BLACK HOLES}}, feeding furiously, buried in so much gas and dust that their light is reddened.

> i: chandra xray | v: chandra xray
But that raised a new problem. Feeding black holes normally blaze in X-rays. When astronomers pointed X-ray telescopes like Chandra at the red dots, most of them were almost silent. [pause] If these were black holes, they were strangely quiet ones.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
And some of the black hole masses implied by the data seemed very large compared with the stars around them, as if the black holes had grown first, and their galaxies later. [pause] That was the opposite of what astronomers expected.

## The Cliff
mood: mystery
> i: little red dots webb | v: deep field zoom
In 2025, a team studying one particularly bright red dot, nicknamed {{The Cliff|THE CLIFF}}, found something remarkable in its spectrum. A sharp jump in brightness at a particular wavelength, much too strong to be explained by old stars. Its light had left it when the universe was less than two billion years old.

> i: dense gas | v: nebula
Their best explanation was dense gas. An enormous cocoon of hydrogen, thick and hot, surrounding a central black hole and absorbing much of its light, then glowing on its own. So much gas that the whole object might look and behave, from the outside, more like a single enormous star than a galaxy.

> i: black hole star | v: star
Some astronomers have started calling this idea a {{black hole star|BLACK HOLE STAR}}. Not a true star, powered by fusion, but a black hole buried inside a giant ball of gas, with the energy of the falling material making the ball glow. [pause] Theoretical physicists had imagined objects like this decades ago, under the name quasi-stars. Nobody expected to see anything like them.

> i: star interior | v: star interior
What would the inside of such an object be like? Near the centre, a black hole perhaps millions of times the Sun's mass, swallowing gas. Around it, a vast, churning envelope of hydrogen, hotter and denser than anything in a normal galaxy, absorbing the black hole's fierce radiation and re-emitting it as a soft red glow. [pause] A monster hiding inside a cloak of its own making.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
If this picture is right, it would solve several puzzles at once. It explains why they are so compact. Why their colours are so strange. And perhaps why they are so quiet in X-rays, with the dense gas smothering that radiation. [pause] But it is still being tested.

## Seeds Of Giants
mood: wonder
> i: supermassive black hole | v: black hole
One of the deepest mysteries in astronomy is how supermassive black holes, millions or billions of times the Sun's mass, grew so big so fast. Webb keeps finding them in the young universe, earlier than our theories comfortably allow.

> i: early universe galaxy | v: early universe
The little red dots might be a missing stage in that story. A brief, hidden phase when young black holes grew rapidly inside thick cocoons of gas, before blasting their cocoons away and emerging as the quasars and galaxy cores we see later. [pause] They might be the baby pictures of the giants.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
Astronomers have also begun finding a few rare objects with similar properties much closer to us, in the more recent universe. If they are the same kind of thing, they could be studied in far more detail, like finding a living fossil of an extinct species.

> i: webb deep field | v: webb deep field zoom
Right now, nobody is certain what the little red dots really are. Galaxies, black holes, black hole stars, or something not yet imagined. Webb found them by accident, in its very first deep images. [pause] And they may turn out to be one of its most important discoveries. Tiny red points, holding the secret of how the universe built its greatest monsters.
