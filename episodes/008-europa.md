## The Moon Of Cracks
mood: wonder
> v: jupiter | i: jupiter
In January 1610, Galileo Galilei pointed his small telescope at Jupiter and saw four points of light moving alongside it. They were moons, the first ever found orbiting another planet. One of them was Europa.

> i: europa voyager | v: europa
For more than three centuries, it remained a point of light. Then, in 1979, the Voyager spacecraft flew past and revealed its face. It was astonishingly smooth, almost without craters, covered instead in a web of long dark lines crossing the entire moon.

> v: europa surface | i: europa cracks
A surface with so few craters must be young. Something keeps erasing the scars of old impacts, resurfacing the moon over and over. And those cracks looked like the fractures in sea ice on Earth, where the ice breaks and shifts over moving water.

> i: europa chaos terrain | v: europa chaos terrain
Some regions, called chaos terrain, look like a jumble of broken ice blocks that have drifted, tilted and frozen back in place, as if the surface had melted from below and refrozen. Scientists began to suspect that beneath the ice, there might be water.

> v: jupiter magnetosphere | i: jupiter radiation belts
Europa orbits inside Jupiter's radiation belts, a region where charged particles are accelerated to tremendous energies by the planet's colossal magnetic field. Standing on its surface, unprotected, you would receive a deadly dose of radiation in about a day. Any spacecraft that goes there has to be built like a vault, and even then, it can only stay for a short while on each pass.

## The Ocean
mood: wonder
> v: galileo spacecraft | i: galileo spacecraft jupiter
In the 1990s, NASA's Galileo spacecraft gave the answer. As it passed Europa, its instruments detected that Jupiter's enormous magnetic field was being disturbed, as if something inside the moon was conducting electricity.

> i: europa interior | v: europa interior
The best explanation was a global layer of salty, liquid water beneath the ice. A real ocean, wrapped around the entire moon, perhaps sixty to a hundred and fifty kilometres deep, under an ice shell around fifteen to twenty five kilometres thick.

> v: europa ocean | i: ocean under ice
Europa is smaller than our Moon. Yet its ocean may hold {{twice as much water|2x EARTH'S OCEANS}} as all of Earth's seas combined.

> i: jupiter europa tidal | v: jupiter moons
How can water stay liquid so far from the Sun? The answer is Jupiter. As Europa orbits, the giant planet's gravity squeezes and stretches it, flexing the moon's interior. That constant kneading creates heat, the way a paperclip warms up when you bend it back and forth. This is called {{tidal heating|TIDAL HEATING}}.

## Plumes, Salt And Carbon
mood: mystery
> v: europa plumes | i: europa plume
In 2012, and again in 2016, the Hubble Space Telescope caught what appeared to be plumes of water vapour erupting from Europa's surface, rising perhaps up to two hundred kilometres into space. The detections were faint and are still debated. But if real, Europa's ocean may be spraying into space.

> i: enceladus plumes | v: enceladus geysers
We know it can happen. On Saturn's moon Enceladus, the Cassini spacecraft flew directly through plumes of water from an underground ocean, and tasted salt, silica and organic molecules.

> v: europa surface colour | i: europa tara regio
Then the telescopes looked closer at Europa's colours. In a region called Tara Regio, the yellowish surface turned out to contain sodium chloride, ordinary table salt, likely brought up from the ocean below.

> i: webb europa | v: webb telescope
In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope found carbon dioxide in the same region. The way it is spread across the surface suggests that the carbon came from the ocean itself. [pause] Carbon is one of the essential ingredients for life as we know it.

> i: europa surface | v: europa ice
That same radiation may be a gift. As it strikes the ice, it breaks water molecules apart, creating oxygen and other reactive chemicals on the surface. If the ice slowly churns, carrying that material down into the ocean, it could deliver a steady supply of chemical energy into the water below. Some scientists have estimated that Europa's ocean could hold more oxygen than many had expected, enough, in principle, to support simple life.

## Spikes Of Ice
mood: wonder
> i: europa surface ice | v: europa surface flyover
Not everything about Europa is hidden. Some of the strangest features may be standing on its surface, right now.

> v: ice spikes | i: penitentes ice
On Earth, in high deserts such as the Andes, sunlight sculpts snowfields into tall blades of ice called penitentes. In 2018, scientists calculated that the same process could happen on Europa's equator, over millions of years. If so, the surface may be covered in jagged ice spikes {{up to fifteen metres tall|15 M ICE SPIKES}}.

> i: europa | v: europa rotation
That would make landing there extraordinarily difficult. Any future lander would have to come down among blades of ice taller than a house, on a world where no one has ever seen the ground up close.

> v: europa ice shell | i: europa cracks
And the ice shell may not even be locked to the moon beneath it. Some evidence suggests the shell drifts slowly, rotating at a slightly different rate from the interior, floating on its hidden ocean like a crust on a pot of soup.

> v: europa jupiter orbit | i: jupiter moons
Europa circles Jupiter every three and a half days, locked in a gravitational rhythm with its neighbours Io and Ganymede. Every time Io goes around four times, Europa goes around twice and Ganymede once. That steady rhythm keeps Europa's orbit slightly stretched, and that is what keeps the tides, and the heat, going for billions of years.

## Is Anything Alive Down There?
mood: mystery
> v: deep sea hydrothermal vent | i: hydrothermal vent
This is the question that drives everything. Life on Earth needs three things: liquid water, the right chemical ingredients, and a source of energy. Europa may have all three.

> i: europa ocean floor | v: ocean floor
Its ocean sits on a rocky seafloor. If that seafloor has volcanic vents, like the ones at the bottom of Earth's oceans, they could pour out heat and chemical energy. On Earth, such vents host entire ecosystems in total darkness, living without any sunlight at all.

> v: europa clipper | i: europa clipper
To find out, NASA launched the Europa Clipper spacecraft in October 2024. It is the largest planetary spacecraft NASA has ever built. It is expected to arrive at Jupiter in 2030 and fly past Europa dozens of times, mapping its ice, measuring its ocean, and perhaps flying through a plume.

> i: juice spacecraft | v: juice jupiter
Europe's JUICE mission, launched in 2023, is heading to Jupiter's moons too. For the first time, two spacecraft will study these hidden oceans at the same time.

> v: europa | i: europa jupiter
Europa will not answer us easily. Its ocean is buried under kilometres of ice, in the deadliest radiation in the solar system. [pause] But somewhere beneath that frozen surface, in the dark, there may be the most important discovery in human history waiting to be made.
