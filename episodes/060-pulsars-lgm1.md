In the summer of 1967, in a field outside Cambridge, a young research student was checking kilometres of paper. Every day, a radio telescope she had helped build scribbled its measurements onto a moving roll of chart paper. More than thirty metres of it, every single day.

> i: radio telescope | v: chart recorder
Among the wiggles, she noticed something odd. A small patch of what she called scruff. A tiny disturbance that did not look like interference, and did not look like anything else she had seen. It took up about a centimetre, out of hundreds of metres of paper.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
When she finally recorded it at high speed, she saw it clearly. A pulse. Then another. And another. Exactly one point three three seven seconds apart. Regular as a clock. Coming from the same spot among the stars, night after night.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Nothing in nature was known to do that. Her team gave the source a half-joking name. {{LGM-1|LGM-1}}. [pause] Little Green Men.

## Four Acres Of Wire
mood: wonder
> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope
The student's name was {{Jocelyn Bell|JOCELYN BELL}}. She was twenty four, working for her PhD under the astronomer Antony Hewish. Her first two years at Cambridge had been spent building the telescope itself, with a small team of students and helpers.

> i: radio telescope array | v: field
It did not look like a telescope at all. It was a field of more than two thousand wooden poles, strung with wire, covering about four and a half acres. Sheep grazed among them. She later said she had swung a sledgehammer to drive many of the posts into the ground.

> i: quasar | v: quasar
The telescope was built to study quasars, the distant, brilliant cores of galaxies. As their radio waves pass through the wind of particles from the Sun, they twinkle, the way stars twinkle in our atmosphere. Measuring that twinkle could reveal how small they were.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
Bell's job was to analyse the output, by hand, by eye, along those endless rolls of paper. The telescope scanned the same strip of sky every day. [pause] And that is how she noticed that the scruff kept coming back, at the same position among the stars, rather than at the same time of day.

## The Clock In The Sky
mood: mystery
> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
At the end of November 1967, Bell installed a faster recorder and caught the signal in detail. It was a string of sharp pulses, separated by one point three three seven three seconds. Precise to better than a millionth of a second.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
Hewish was at first convinced it had to be human interference. A faulty car engine, a radar, a satellite. They checked. The source moved with the stars, not with the Earth. It was coming from far beyond our solar system. [pause] But nothing natural was known to keep time like that.

> i: star field | v: star field
So the team had to seriously consider a possibility that nobody wanted to say out loud. That this might be a signal made by someone. Hewish and his colleagues even discussed what they would do if it were. Who would they tell? How would they announce it?

> i: night sky | v: night sky
They checked whether the source was moving the way a planet orbiting a star would. If it were a transmitter on an alien world, its signal should shift slightly as the planet went around its sun. [pause] It did not. Whatever it was, it was not sitting on a planet.

## LGM-2
mood: wonder
> i: radio telescope night | v: chart recorder
Just before Christmas, Bell went back through her charts, late at night, and found another patch of scruff, from a completely different part of the sky. She recorded it again in the early hours. It pulsed too, about once every one and a quarter seconds.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
That changed everything. It was very unlikely that two separate groups of little green men, on opposite sides of the sky, would both be signalling us at almost the same frequency, at the same time. [pause] This had to be a new kind of natural object. Something nobody had ever seen.

> i: pulsar | v: neutron star
In February 1968, the team published their discovery. The objects became known as {{pulsars|PULSARS}}. Within months, other astronomers proposed the answer: rapidly spinning neutron stars, the collapsed cores of dead stars, sweeping beams of radio waves across space like a lighthouse.

> i: crab nebula pulsar | v: crab nebula
The proof came later that year, when astronomers found a pulsar in the heart of the Crab Nebula, the remains of a star that exploded in the year 1054. It was spinning about thirty times a second. [pause] Pulsars were the ghosts of supernovae.

## The Nobel Prize
mood: mystery
> i: nobel prize | v: physics laboratory
In 1974, the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for pioneering work in radio astronomy. One half went to Antony Hewish, for the discovery of pulsars. The other went to Martin Ryle. {{Jocelyn Bell|JOCELYN BELL BURNELL}} was not included.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope
Many people saw it as a glaring injustice. The astronomer Fred Hoyle publicly protested. Bell Burnell herself was strikingly gracious about it, saying that prizes usually went to senior scientists who led the work. [pause] But the story became one of the most famous examples of how discoveries can be overlooked.

> i: jocelyn bell burnell | v: telescope dome
She went on to a distinguished career in astronomy. In 2018, she received the Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics, worth three million dollars. She gave all of it away, to help students from under-represented groups train as physicists. [pause] The student who almost got written out of the story made sure others would not be.

## Pulsars Everywhere
mood: wonder
> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
Today, astronomers know of thousands of pulsars. Some spin once every few seconds. Some spin hundreds of times every second, more precisely than many of the best atomic clocks on Earth.

> i: pioneer plaque | v: voyager golden record
Their regularity made them perfect cosmic landmarks. The plaques on the Pioneer spacecraft and the covers of the Voyager golden records carry a map showing the Sun's position relative to fourteen pulsars, each labelled with its timing. [pause] If anyone ever finds them, the pulsars will tell them where we came from.

> i: binary pulsar | v: neutron star merger
In 1974, astronomers found a pulsar orbiting another neutron star. As the pair slowly spiralled closer, losing energy exactly as Einstein's theory predicted, they provided the first evidence of gravitational waves. That discovery won a Nobel Prize too.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
And that very first pulsar, CP 1919, became famous in an unexpected way. A chart of its pulses, stacked one above another like a mountain range, was printed in an astronomy encyclopaedia. In 1979, the band Joy Division used it as the cover of their album {{Unknown Pleasures|UNKNOWN PLEASURES}}. [pause] Millions of people have worn it on T-shirts, without knowing they are wearing a dead star's heartbeat.

> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope night
It began with a centimetre of scruff on a roll of paper, noticed by a student who refused to ignore it. [pause] We went looking for little green men. We found the spinning hearts of dead stars instead. And in some ways, that is even more astonishing.
