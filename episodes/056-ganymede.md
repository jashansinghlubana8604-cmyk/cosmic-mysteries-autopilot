## The Biggest Moon
mood: wonder
> i: jupiter moons | v: jupiter moons
In January 1610, Galileo Galilei turned his telescope on Jupiter and saw four small stars beside it, shifting from night to night. They were moons. It was the first time anyone had seen objects orbiting another world, and it helped prove that not everything circles the Earth.

> i: ganymede | v: ganymede rotation
The largest of the four is {{Ganymede|GANYMEDE}}. It is about five thousand two hundred and seventy kilometres across. Mercury is about four thousand eight hundred and eighty. The biggest moon in the solar system is bigger than the smallest planet.

> i: ganymede surface | v: ganymede
But Ganymede is lighter than Mercury, because it is not made mostly of rock and iron. Roughly half of it is water, frozen into ice near the surface. Its surface temperature hovers around minus one hundred and sixty degrees, colder than anywhere on Earth.

> i: ganymede galileo | v: ganymede
Its face is a patchwork. Ancient, dark regions, covered in craters from billions of years of impacts. And brighter, younger bands, criss-crossed with long grooves and ridges, where the icy crust seems to have been pulled apart and refrozen. [pause] Something has been reshaping this world from within.

## A Moon With A Magnet
mood: mystery
> i: galileo spacecraft | v: galileo spacecraft
In 1996, NASA's Galileo spacecraft flew past Ganymede and detected something no one expected. The moon had its own {{magnetic field|MAGNETIC FIELD}}. Ganymede is the only moon in the solar system known to generate one.

> i: earth magnetic field | v: earth magnetic field
On the Earth, our magnetic field is generated by liquid iron churning in the core. Ganymede must have something similar, a molten iron core, still hot and still moving after four and a half billion years. A tiny moon-sized version of our planet's heart.

> i: ganymede aurora | v: aurora
And where there is a magnetic field, there are auroras. Charged particles from Jupiter's huge magnetic bubble rain down onto Ganymede's poles, making two glowing ribbons of light. [pause] Northern and southern lights, on a frozen moon.

> i: jupiter magnetosphere | v: jupiter magnetosphere
Ganymede's field carves out a small magnetic bubble of its own, nested inside Jupiter's gigantic one. A bubble within a bubble. It even shields parts of the moon's surface, near its equator, from some of the harsh radiation that pours down elsewhere. [pause] It is a world with its own invisible armour.

## The Hidden Ocean
mood: mystery
> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
In 2015, astronomers used the Hubble Space Telescope to watch those auroras very carefully. As Jupiter spins, its magnetic field sweeps past Ganymede, and Ganymede's auroras should rock back and forth by a certain amount.

> i: ganymede aurora | v: aurora
But they rocked much less than expected. Something inside Ganymede was fighting back against Jupiter's field, damping the motion. The best explanation was a huge layer of {{salty water|SALT WATER}} beneath the ice, conducting electricity and creating a magnetic field of its own.

> i: ganymede interior | v: ganymede interior
The ocean is thought to lie under perhaps a hundred and fifty kilometres of ice, and could itself be around a hundred kilometres deep. That is more than ten times deeper than the deepest trench on Earth. By some estimates, it holds more water than every ocean on our planet combined.

> i: ganymede interior | v: ganymede interior
But Ganymede's ocean may be trapped between layers of ice. Below it, the pressure is so great that water could freeze into exotic, dense forms of ice, sealing the ocean off from the rocky floor. [pause] And without contact between water and rock, the chemistry that may have started life on Earth might never get going. Some models suggest the ocean could still touch rock in places. Nobody knows yet.

## The Juno Flyby
mood: wonder
> i: juno spacecraft | v: juno spacecraft
On the seventh of June 2021, NASA's Juno spacecraft swept past Ganymede at just over a thousand kilometres, the closest any spacecraft had come in more than twenty years. It took the sharpest images ever made of large parts of its surface.

> i: ganymede juno | v: ganymede
Its instruments also found hints of minerals on the surface, including salts and organic compounds, possibly brought up from the ocean below at some point in the past. If so, the surface of Ganymede may hold traces of what that hidden sea is made of.

> i: ganymede surface | v: ganymede
Juno's radio instrument also picked up radio emissions as it flew through Ganymede's magnetic field. NASA turned them into a short audio track, shifted into a range human ears can hear. [pause] A strange, swirling chorus from a moon five hundred million kilometres away.

> i: ganymede | v: ganymede rotation
And the scars on Ganymede tell their own story. In 2024, a study of its ancient grooves suggested they all centre on one point, as if a giant asteroid, perhaps three hundred kilometres wide, smashed into it about four billion years ago. [pause] An impact so huge it may have shifted the moon's whole orientation.

## The First Moon Orbiter
mood: wonder
> i: juice spacecraft | v: juice spacecraft
On the fourteenth of April 2023, the European Space Agency launched a spacecraft called {{JUICE|JUICE}}, the JUpiter ICy moons Explorer. It is taking the long road, swinging past the Earth, the Moon and Venus to gain speed. In August 2024, it flew past the Moon and then the Earth within about a day, the first double flyby of its kind ever attempted. It worked perfectly.

> i: juice spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
It is due to reach Jupiter in 2031. It will study Europa and Callisto, and then, in the mid 2030s, it will do something no spacecraft has ever done. It will slow down and go into orbit around Ganymede. [pause] The first spacecraft to orbit any moon other than our own.

> i: ganymede | v: ganymede
From orbit, JUICE will map Ganymede's surface, measure its magnetic field, probe the thickness of its ice with radar and work out how deep its ocean goes. It will help answer the biggest question of all. Could this ocean ever have supported life?

> i: jupiter moons | v: jupiter moons
A moon bigger than a planet. A molten iron heart. Auroras over a frozen surface. And a hidden ocean, deeper than any on Earth, sealed in the dark. [pause] Ganymede has been waiting for four and a half billion years. In a few years, someone will finally come to stay.
