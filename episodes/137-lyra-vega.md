For centuries, travellers have found their way at night using a single star. Polaris, the North Star. Stand outside in the northern hemisphere, find it, and you are facing north. It hardly moves, while every other star in the sky wheels slowly around it.

> i: night sky star trails | v: star trails
It seems eternal, the one fixed point in a turning sky. Sailors have trusted it. Poets have written about it. Escaped slaves in America followed it to freedom.

> i: vega | v: night sky
But it is not eternal. Five thousand years ago, when the pyramids of Egypt were being built, Polaris was not the North Star. And about twelve thousand years from now, the bright star Vega will be close to taking its place. [pause] The North Star is changing, slowly, all the time.

> i: earth rotation | v: earth rotation
The reason is that the Earth is wobbling, like a spinning top that is slowly tilting around. [pause] And that wobble changes our sky, our calendars, and even our climate.

## The Spinning Top
mood: wonder
> i: earth rotation | v: earth rotation
The Earth spins on an axis, an imaginary line through the North and South Poles. That axis is tilted by about twenty three and a half degrees, which gives us the seasons. And it points in a particular direction in space.

> i: polaris | v: night sky
Right now, the northern end of the axis points almost exactly at Polaris. As the Earth spins, every star appears to circle around that point. Long-exposure photographs of the night sky show star trails curving around Polaris like rings on a target.

> i: big dipper | v: night sky
Finding it is easy. Locate the Plough, also called the Big Dipper, and follow the two stars at the end of its bowl upward, about five times the distance between them. You arrive at a moderately bright star standing on its own. [pause] That is Polaris. Not the brightest star in the sky, despite what many people think, but the most useful.

> i: earth tilt | v: earth rotation
But the axis is not fixed. The Earth is not a perfect sphere; it bulges slightly at the equator. The gravity of the Sun and the Moon tugs on that bulge, trying to straighten the Earth's tilt. Instead of straightening, the axis slowly swings around in a circle, like a spinning top wobbling as it spins.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
This motion is called {{precession|PRECESSION}}. It is incredibly slow. One full circle takes about twenty six thousand years. [pause] But over the lifetime of a civilisation, it changes the sky.

## The Pole Stars Of History
mood: wonder
> i: draco constellation | v: night sky
Around five thousand years ago, the Earth's axis pointed close to a faint star called {{Thuban|THUBAN}}, in the constellation Draco, the dragon. For the people of ancient Egypt, Thuban was the star around which the heavens turned.

> i: pyramids | v: desert
Some researchers have suggested that narrow shafts in the Great Pyramid may have been aligned with Thuban and other stars of the time. That idea is debated. But the ancient Egyptians certainly paid close attention to the northern sky, which they associated with stars that never set. [pause] They called them the imperishable ones.

> i: polaris | v: star trails
Polaris has only been close to the pole for a relatively short time, and it is still getting closer. It will be nearest to the true north celestial pole around the year twenty one hundred. After that, the pole will slowly move away from it again.

> i: vega | v: night sky
Thousands of years from now, the pole will drift towards the constellation Cepheus and then towards Lyra. Around the year fourteen thousand, the brilliant star {{Vega|VEGA}} will be the brightest star near the pole, though not as close to it as Polaris is now. [pause] Our distant descendants may navigate by a different star entirely.

## Discovered Two Thousand Years Ago
mood: mystery
> i: ancient astronomy | v: night sky
Precession was discovered in the second century BC by the Greek astronomer {{Hipparchus|HIPPARCHUS}}. He compared his own careful measurements of star positions with records made about a century and a half earlier, and noticed that all the stars seemed to have shifted slightly in the same way.

> i: zodiac constellations | v: night sky
He worked out that the points where the Sun crosses the celestial equator each spring and autumn were slowly moving backwards through the constellations. This is called the precession of the equinoxes. [pause] It was one of the greatest achievements of ancient astronomy.

> i: ancient astronomy | v: night sky
Hipparchus also compiled one of the first great star catalogues, recording the positions of hundreds of stars, and he is often credited with an early version of the system astronomers still use to describe how bright stars look. [pause] Working with simple instruments and the naked eye, he detected a motion that takes twenty six thousand years to complete.

> i: zodiac constellations | v: night sky
It also means that the signs of the zodiac used in astrology no longer match the constellations. When the system was set up over two thousand years ago, the Sun was in the constellation Aries at the spring equinox. Today, because of precession, it is in Pisces. [pause] If you check the sky on your birthday, the Sun is probably in a different constellation from your star sign.

## The Wobble And The Ice Ages
mood: wonder
> i: ice age | v: glacier
Precession is not just about stars. It also affects the Earth's climate. As the axis wobbles, it changes which hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun when the Earth is closest to it in its orbit.

> i: earth orbit | v: earth orbit
In the early twentieth century, the Serbian scientist Milutin Milankovitch calculated how precession, together with slow changes in the Earth's tilt and the shape of its orbit, alters the amount of sunlight reaching different parts of the planet over tens of thousands of years. The tilt itself rocks between about twenty two and twenty four and a half degrees over roughly forty one thousand years, and the orbit stretches and relaxes over about a hundred thousand years.

> i: glacier | v: ice
Those cycles, now called {{Milankovitch cycles|MILANKOVITCH CYCLES}}, help set the rhythm of the ice ages. Records from ocean sediments and ice cores show the Earth's climate swinging in time with them. [pause] The same wobble that changes the North Star helps decide when glaciers advance and retreat.

## A Sky In Motion
mood: wonder
> i: night sky star trails | v: star trails
The southern hemisphere does not currently have a bright pole star. The south celestial pole sits in a dim patch of sky near a faint star called Sigma Octantis, barely visible to the naked eye. Southern navigators have traditionally used the Southern Cross to find south. In thousands of years, precession will bring brighter stars closer to the southern pole too.

> i: polaris | v: star
And Polaris itself is more interesting than it looks. It is a giant star, about four hundred and thirty light-years away, and it is not alone; it is part of a triple star system. It also pulses slightly in brightness, a type of star called a Cepheid, the same kind astronomers use to measure distances across the universe. [pause] The light you see from it tonight left it around the time Galileo first pointed a telescope at the sky.

> i: polaris | v: star trails
So the next time you find Polaris, remember that it is not fixed forever. It is just today's guide. [pause] The sky that looks so permanent is slowly turning, on a clock with a twenty six thousand year hand. Every generation sees it from a slightly different angle. And thousands of years from now, someone will look up, find Vega near the pole, and call it the North Star.
