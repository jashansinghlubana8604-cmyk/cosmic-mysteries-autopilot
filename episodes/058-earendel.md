In 2022, astronomers announced that they had seen a single star whose light had been travelling for almost thirteen billion years. Not a galaxy. Not a cluster. One star, shining when the universe was less than a tenth of its current age.

> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
This should be impossible. At that distance, even whole galaxies are faint smudges. An individual star should be far too dim to see, by thousands of times, with any telescope ever built.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: gravitational lensing
And yet, there it was. A tiny point of light sitting on a stretched, glowing arc of a galaxy. Magnified by something enormous, something that bent its light and focused it towards us like a giant lens. [pause] A lens made of gravity.

> i: earendel | v: deep field zoom
Its discoverers named it {{Earendel|EARENDEL}}, an old English word meaning morning star, or rising light. [pause] It is the most distant single star humanity has ever seen.

## A Lens Made Of Gravity
mood: wonder
> i: albert einstein | v: spacetime curvature
In 1915, Einstein's theory of general relativity predicted that mass bends space. And because light travels through space, light passing close to a heavy object should bend too. In 1919, astronomers measured exactly that, as starlight bent around the Sun during a total eclipse. The British astronomer Arthur Eddington led expeditions to Africa and Brazil to photograph the stars near the darkened Sun. When the results were announced, newspapers around the world declared that Newton's view of gravity had been overthrown, and Einstein became the most famous scientist on Earth.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Now imagine something far heavier than the Sun. A {{galaxy cluster|GALAXY CLUSTER}}, a swarm of hundreds or thousands of galaxies, together with enormous amounts of dark matter, weighing as much as a million billion Suns. Its gravity bends the light of anything behind it.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: gravitational lensing
Light from a distant galaxy behind the cluster is bent around it and focused, like light through a magnifying glass. The background galaxy appears brighter and bigger, but also stretched and smeared into arcs, sometimes into rings. [pause] The universe has built its own telescopes, and they are millions of light-years wide.

> i: gravitational lensing arcs | v: galaxy cluster
Astronomers use these natural lenses all the time to study galaxies that would otherwise be too faint to see. But they almost never expected to see a single star through one. [pause] Until a team looked very closely at one particular arc.

## The Sunrise Arc
mood: mystery
> i: earendel sunrise arc | v: gravitational lensing
In a Hubble program designed to study galaxy clusters, astronomers imaged a cluster called {{WHL0137-08|WHL0137-08}}. Behind it, the light of a very distant galaxy had been stretched into a long, thin, curved line. They nicknamed it the Sunrise Arc.

> i: earendel | v: deep field zoom
In 2022, a team led by astronomer {{Brian Welch|BRIAN WELCH}} noticed a tiny, intensely bright spot sitting right in the middle of the arc. It did not look like a cluster of stars. It looked like a single point. And it sat exactly where the lens should be magnifying the most.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: gravitational lensing
Along certain lines behind a cluster, called caustics, the magnification can become extreme. A star sitting almost exactly on one of those lines can be brightened thousands of times. [pause] By the team's estimates, Earendel's light was being magnified by a factor of at least a thousand.

> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
How could they be sure it was a star and not a small galaxy or a bright clump? Its light was squeezed into a region far too small for a galaxy, a few light-years across at most. And it stayed bright in Hubble images taken over three and a half years. A chance alignment with a passing star in the cluster would have flared and faded quickly. [pause] Earendel just kept shining.

> i: earendel | v: earendel
The star's light left it about twelve point nine billion years ago, when the universe was only around nine hundred million years old. And because space has been expanding all that time, the place where Earendel shone is now roughly twenty eight billion light-years away. [pause] It may have died billions of years before the Earth was born.

## What Kind Of Star?
mood: mystery
> i: blue giant star | v: star
To be visible at all, even with that magnification, Earendel had to be extraordinary. Early estimates suggested it was at least fifty times the mass of our Sun, and millions of times brighter.

> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope looked at it too, in infrared light. Webb's data suggested Earendel is a massive, hot star, more than twice as hot as our Sun, and possibly accompanied by a cooler companion star.

> i: star cluster | v: star cluster
Later studies raised another possibility: that what we see might not be a single star at all, but a small, tight cluster of stars, also magnified enormously. Telling the difference at that distance is incredibly hard. [pause] Whatever it is, it is one of the most distant individual objects we have ever resolved.

> i: first stars | v: early universe
There is a tantalising hope. The very first stars in the universe, called Population Three, were made only of hydrogen and helium, the elements left from the Big Bang. No one has ever seen one directly. A star as early as Earendel is not that old, but it is a step closer. [pause] Lenses like this may one day let us see a true first-generation star.

## Breaking Records
mood: wonder
> i: icarus star | v: gravitational lensing
Before Earendel, the record belonged to a star nicknamed {{Icarus|ICARUS}}, found in 2018. Its light had travelled for about nine billion years, magnified by another galaxy cluster. Earendel smashed that record by billions of years.

> i: lensed star | v: gravitational lensing
Since then, astronomers have found more magnified stars in the distant universe, flickering as the lens shifts and as individual stars inside the cluster move across the light path. Webb has even caught dozens of individual stars in a single lensed galaxy far away.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
It is a new kind of astronomy. Not just galaxies as blurry wholes, but individual stars inside them, across billions of years of cosmic history. [pause] We are beginning to see the universe one star at a time.

## Light From The Morning
mood: wonder
> i: earendel | v: earendel
Think about the journey of the light from Earendel. It left the star before the Sun existed, before the Earth formed, before the Milky Way had settled into its spiral shape. It crossed almost thirteen billion years of expanding space.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Just before the end of its journey, it passed by a vast cluster of galaxies, whose gravity bent it ever so slightly towards a small blue planet. And on that planet, a telescope happened to be pointed in exactly that direction.

> i: earendel sunrise arc | v: gravitational lensing
The name Earendel also echoes a character in the stories of J. R. R. Tolkien, who sails across the sky carrying a shining jewel, becoming a star of hope. [pause] It is a fitting name for a light that has travelled so far, against such odds, to reach us.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
The star itself is almost certainly gone. Massive stars live fast and die young, in a few million years. Earendel exploded or collapsed long, long ago. [pause] But its light is still arriving. A morning star from the dawn of time, still shining, long after the star has died.
