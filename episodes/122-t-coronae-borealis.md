In the constellation Corona Borealis, the northern crown, there is a star you cannot normally see. It is far too faint for the naked eye, about a thousand times too dim. Most nights, nothing marks its place.

> i: nova | v: supernova explosion
But roughly once every eighty years, it suddenly flares up. In just a few hours, it brightens around a thousand times, becoming as bright as the North Star. For about a week, a new star shines in the crown. Then it fades away again.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
It last did this in 1946. Before that, in 1866. And in the mid-2020s, astronomers noticed it behaving exactly the way it did shortly before its last eruption. [pause] They believe it is due to flare again, very soon.

> i: white dwarf red giant | v: binary star
Its name is {{T Coronae Borealis|T CORONAE BOREALIS}}. Its nickname is the Blaze Star. [pause] And if you have not already seen it, you may be one of the people who gets to.

## A Star Eating Its Neighbour
mood: wonder
> i: white dwarf red giant | v: binary star
T Coronae Borealis is not one star but two. One is a {{red giant|RED GIANT}}, a bloated, cool star near the end of its life. The other is a {{white dwarf|WHITE DWARF}}, the dense, Earth-sized core left behind by a star that has already died. They orbit each other every two hundred and twenty eight days.

> i: accretion disk | v: accretion disk
The white dwarf's gravity is enormous for its size. It pulls hydrogen gas off the outer layers of the red giant. That gas spirals in and settles onto the white dwarf's surface, layer by layer, year after year.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
As the layer builds up, it gets hotter and denser at its base. Eventually, after decades, it reaches a tipping point. The hydrogen ignites in a runaway nuclear explosion across the whole surface of the white dwarf. [pause] That explosion is a {{nova|NOVA}}.

> i: nova | v: nova
A nova is not a supernova. The white dwarf is not destroyed. It blasts the outer layer of gas into space, glows brilliantly for a while, and then settles down. And the process begins again. The red giant keeps feeding it. [pause] The clock resets.

> i: nova | v: nova
Each explosion throws off a shell of gas weighing roughly as much as a small planet, perhaps similar to the mass of the Earth or less, flung out at thousands of kilometres per second. On the scale of stars, it is a tiny amount. [pause] But it is enough to make a star a thousand times brighter for a week.

## The Eruptions Of The Past
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky
On the twelfth of May 1866, an Irish astronomer named John Birmingham noticed a bright star in Corona Borealis that he had never seen before. It was T Coronae Borealis, in eruption. It was one of the first novas ever studied with a spectroscope, which revealed the glowing gases it was blasting out.

> i: corona borealis | v: night sky
Eighty years later, on the ninth of February 1946, it erupted again, nearly as brightly. By then, astronomers were watching it, and they recorded something important. In the year or so before the eruption, the star dimmed slightly, then flared. [pause] A pre-eruption dip.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
In March 2023, astronomers noticed T Coronae Borealis beginning to dim in the same way. Based on the timing in 1946, some predicted an eruption in 2024 or 2025. When this video was written, it had not yet happened, and the predictions had moved further out. [pause] Novas do not keep perfect schedules.

## What You Will See
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky
When it erupts, it will happen fast. Within hours, T Coronae Borealis will rise from invisible to about second magnitude, roughly the brightness of Polaris, the North Star. It will appear as a new star in the small, semicircular constellation of the northern crown, between the bright stars Arcturus and Vega.

> i: corona borealis | v: night sky
It will not look like an explosion. It will look like an ordinary star that was not there before. That is exactly why ancient astronomers called these events novas, from the Latin for new. [pause] For a few nights, the crown will have an extra jewel.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
It will not last long. After peaking, it will fade over about a week, dropping back below what the naked eye can see. If you want to see it, you will need to look quickly, as soon as the news breaks. Astronomers across the world, professional and amateur, will be watching.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
You will not need a telescope. A modern phone camera on a steady surface, taking a few seconds' exposure, can easily capture a star that bright. Compare it with an older photo of the same patch of sky, and the new star will stand out immediately. [pause] Millions of people could record it from their back gardens.

## Is It Dangerous?
mood: mystery
> i: star field | v: star field
T Coronae Borealis is about three thousand light-years away. A nova is far less powerful than a supernova, and at that distance, it poses no danger at all to the Earth. [pause] It will be a show, not a threat.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
But in the very distant future, it could become something more dramatic. Each eruption blasts off most, but perhaps not all, of the material the white dwarf has gained. If the white dwarf slowly grows heavier over many cycles, it could one day approach the maximum mass a white dwarf can have.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
If it reaches that limit, it could explode as a type of supernova, completely destroying itself. That is far in the future, if it happens at all. Some astronomers think it may be losing more than it gains. [pause] For now, the Blaze Star is simply a cosmic clock, ticking towards its next flash.

> i: nova | v: star field
It is not the only star that does this. Astronomers know of about ten recurrent novas in our galaxy, stars that have been seen to erupt more than once. One called RS Ophiuchi erupted in 2006 and again in 2021. Another, U Scorpii, flares roughly every ten years. [pause] T Coronae Borealis is special because, when it erupts, it is one of the brightest and closest of them all.

## Once In A Lifetime
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky
Most people only get one chance to see T Coronae Borealis erupt, if they get one at all. Its eruptions are spaced about as far apart as a human lifetime. Those who saw it in 1946 were children or young adults. Very few of them are still alive today.

> i: corona borealis | v: night sky
So find the northern crown. On spring and summer evenings in the northern hemisphere, look for the bright orange star Arcturus, then trace eastward to a small arc of seven stars. That is Corona Borealis. Learn its shape. [pause] Then, if one night you see an extra star there, you will know exactly what you are looking at.

> i: nova | v: star field
A white dwarf, feeding on its dying partner, finally reaching its breaking point, and lighting up the sky for a few nights, three thousand years after the light began its journey. [pause] A star, being born for a week, in a crown in the sky.
