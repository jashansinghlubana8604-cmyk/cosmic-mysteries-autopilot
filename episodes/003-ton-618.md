## The Faint Blue Star
mood: wonder
> i: observatory night | v: observatory time-lapse
In the 1950s, astronomers at the Tonantzintla Observatory in Mexico were cataloguing faint blue stars in the northern sky. One entry, number 618, looked like nothing special. A pale dot in the constellation Canes Venatici, the Hunting Dogs.

> v: radio telescope | i: radio telescope milky way
Years later, radio astronomers found that this dot was also sending out radio waves. When its light was split into a spectrum, it revealed something astonishing. The light was stretched so far toward red that the object had to be billions of light years away.

> i: quasar | v: quasar jets
That meant TON 618 was not a faint star at all. It was a {{quasar|QUASAR}}, one of the brightest kinds of object in the universe, so far away that its light left it around ten billion years ago, before the Sun or the Earth existed.

> v: accretion disc | i: accretion disc quasar
A quasar is what happens when a supermassive black hole is fed. Gas spirals inward, forming a disc that heats up to enormous temperatures through friction, glowing brighter than anything else. The black hole itself is invisible. The light comes from everything falling toward it.

> i: quasar artist impression | v: quasar
TON 618 shines with the light of around a hundred and forty trillion Suns. Even across ten billion light years, it can still be seen today through a large amateur telescope.

## Weighing A Monster
mood: mystery
> v: black hole simulation | i: black hole
How do you weigh something that does not let light escape? You watch what moves around it.

> v: gas orbiting black hole | i: gas clouds black hole
In TON 618, clouds of gas swirl around the black hole at tremendous speeds. Their light reveals how fast they are moving. The faster the gas, the stronger the gravity holding it, and the more massive the black hole must be.

> i: supermassive black hole artist | v: supermassive black hole
In 2019, astronomers used this method to estimate the mass of TON 618 at around sixty six billion times the mass of the Sun. The measurement is uncertain, and other methods give somewhat lower numbers. But by any estimate, it is one of the most massive black holes ever found.

> v: milky way centre zoom | i: sagittarius a star
For comparison, the black hole at the heart of our own galaxy, Sagittarius A star, weighs about four million Suns. TON 618 is roughly {{fifteen thousand times heavier|15,000x}}.

> i: m87 black hole event horizon telescope | v: m87 black hole
Even the famous black hole in the galaxy M87, the first ever photographed, weighs six and a half billion Suns, and it is a giant by any standard. TON 618 is ten times heavier still. If M87's black hole is a mountain, TON 618 is a mountain range.

## Bigger Than A Solar System
mood: wonder
> v: solar system | i: solar system planets
Let us try to picture its size. The event horizon, the point of no return, of a black hole with sixty six billion solar masses would be nearly four hundred billion kilometres across.

> i: pluto | v: pluto new horizons
It would be more than thirty times wider than the entire orbit of Pluto. Light, the fastest thing in the universe, would take more than two weeks to cross from one side to the other.

> v: black hole accretion disc | i: black hole disc
And the glowing disc of gas around it would be even larger, stretching across a region of space perhaps hundreds of times bigger than the black hole itself.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy rotation
If TON 618 were placed where our Sun is, there would be no Earth, no Mars, no planets at all. Everything out far beyond Neptune would already have fallen past the edge. [pause] And yet, compared to its galaxy, it would still be tiny.

> v: falling into black hole | i: black hole artist impression
Strangely, this monster would be one of the gentlest black holes to fall into. Because it is so enormous, the difference in gravity between your head and your feet near its edge would be tiny. You could cross its event horizon without feeling a thing. You would simply never come back out, and the journey from the edge to the centre could take you many hours.

## How Did It Grow So Big?
mood: mystery
> v: early universe | i: early universe galaxies
This is where TON 618 becomes a genuine puzzle. We see it as it was about ten billion years ago, when the universe was less than four billion years old. That is not much time to build a black hole this large.

> i: black hole feeding | v: black hole feeding
Black holes cannot grow as fast as they like. As gas falls in, it heats up and shines, and the pressure of that light pushes against the incoming gas. This sets a speed limit on growth, called the {{Eddington limit|EDDINGTON LIMIT}}. To reach sixty six billion Suns that quickly, TON 618 would have to have been feeding close to that limit for most of its life.

> v: galaxy merger | i: galaxy collision
Perhaps it started from an unusually massive seed, a huge cloud of gas that collapsed directly into a black hole in the early universe. Perhaps it grew through mergers, swallowing other black holes as galaxies collided. Perhaps it sometimes fed faster than the limit allows. Astronomers are still debating which is true.

> i: quasar | v: quasar jets
Some theorists have even calculated that there may be a natural upper limit to how big a feeding black hole can get, around fifty billion Suns, beyond which its gas disc would break apart into stars instead of falling in. If the highest estimates are right, TON 618 is pressing right up against that limit.

## The Other Giants
mood: wonder
> v: galaxy cluster | i: galaxy cluster
TON 618 is not alone. In the centres of the largest galaxy clusters sit other monsters. One, in a cluster called Phoenix, may weigh as much as a hundred billion Suns, though that estimate is controversial. Another, found in 2023 by watching how it bends the light of a distant galaxy, weighs more than thirty billion. And in 2025, astronomers weighed one in a galaxy known as the Cosmic Horseshoe at around thirty six billion Suns, one of the most massive ever measured.

> i: gravitational lens | v: gravitational lensing
These are sometimes called ultramassive black holes. Each one sits at the heart of a giant galaxy, shaping how its stars form, and sometimes shutting star formation down entirely with its outflows.

> v: zoom deep field | i: hubble ultra deep field
TON 618 itself has probably stopped shining as brightly by now. The light we see left it ten billion years ago. Today, the black hole is likely still there, larger than ever, quiet and dark in the centre of a vast old galaxy. [pause] And it will outlast almost everything.

> v: quasar | i: quasar artist impression
In a way, TON 618 is a fossil. The era of the brightest quasars peaked around ten billion years ago, when galaxies were young and full of gas. Since then, most of the giant black holes have gone quiet, starved of fuel. When we look at TON 618, we are looking back at the most violent chapter in the history of the universe.

> i: black hole | v: black hole simulation
Black holes slowly evaporate, but a black hole this large would take so long to vanish that the number has more than ninety digits. Long after the last stars have gone out, TON 618 will still be there, one of the last great monuments of a universe that once burned with light.
