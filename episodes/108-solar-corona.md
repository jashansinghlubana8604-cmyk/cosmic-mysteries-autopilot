Imagine standing next to a campfire. The closer you get, the hotter it feels. Step back, and it cools. That is how heat works. The further you go from the source, the colder it gets.

> i: solar corona eclipse | v: total solar eclipse
The Sun breaks that rule. Its visible surface is about five and a half thousand degrees. But its outer atmosphere, a wispy halo called the corona, stretching millions of kilometres into space, is more than a million degrees. Some parts are far hotter.

> i: total solar eclipse | v: total solar eclipse
It is as if you stepped away from a fire and the air started burning hotter and hotter. For more than eighty years, scientists have struggled to explain it. [pause] It is called the coronal heating problem.

> i: parker solar probe | v: parker solar probe
To solve it, we have sent a spacecraft closer to the Sun than anything ever built. It has flown through the corona itself. [pause] And it is finally giving us answers.

## The Crown Of The Sun
mood: wonder
> i: total solar eclipse | v: total solar eclipse
Most of the time, you cannot see the corona. The bright surface of the Sun drowns it out. But during a total solar eclipse, when the Moon perfectly blocks the Sun's disc, the sky darkens and a ghostly, pearly-white glow appears around the black Moon.

> i: solar corona eclipse | v: solar corona
Streamers stretch out for millions of kilometres. Delicate loops and plumes trace invisible magnetic fields. For centuries, people who saw it called it a crown, which is what the Latin word {{corona|CORONA}} means.

> i: total solar eclipse | v: total solar eclipse
In 1869, astronomers studying the corona during an eclipse split its light into a spectrum and found a bright green line that did not match any known element. They thought they had discovered a new one, and called it {{coronium|CORONIUM}}.

> i: sun corona sdo | v: solar corona
It took about seventy years to solve the mystery. Around 1940, physicists Walter Grotrian and Bengt Edlén showed that the green line came from iron, but iron that had lost thirteen of its electrons. Stripping that many electrons requires an extraordinary temperature. [pause] The corona had to be over a million degrees.

## The Impossible Heat
mood: mystery
> i: sun interior | v: sun interior
The Sun's energy is made deep in its core, at around fifteen million degrees. It works its way outward over many thousands of years, until it reaches the visible surface, the photosphere, and escapes as light. The temperature drops steadily on the way out. [pause] Everything makes sense, until the corona.

> i: sun corona sdo | v: solar corona
Just above the surface, in a thin layer, the temperature begins to rise. Then, in a narrow region, it shoots up dramatically, from thousands of degrees to more than a million, over a distance smaller than the Earth. Something is pumping energy into the corona, far more than simple heat flowing outward could ever supply.

> i: solar magnetic field | v: solar flare
Almost all scientists agree that the answer involves the Sun's magnetic field. The Sun is a churning ball of electrically charged gas, and its motion twists and tangles magnetic fields everywhere. The question is exactly how those fields deliver their energy to the corona. [pause] There are two leading ideas.

> i: solar corona | v: solar corona
There is one thing that makes the puzzle a little less impossible. The corona's gas is incredibly thin, billions of times thinner than the air you breathe. So although each particle is moving with the energy of a million degrees, there are so few of them that the total heat is small. Like the sparks from a sparkler, which are extremely hot but barely warm your hand. [pause] Still, something has to keep feeding that heat, constantly.

## Waves Or Explosions?
mood: mystery
> i: solar magnetic field | v: solar magnetic field
The first idea is waves. As the Sun's surface boils and churns, it shakes the magnetic field lines rooted in it, sending waves rippling upward, like flicking the end of a rope. These magnetic waves, called {{Alfvén waves|ALFVÉN WAVES}}, could carry energy up into the corona and deposit it there as heat.

> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
The second idea is explosions. In 1988, the physicist Eugene Parker proposed that the corona is heated by countless tiny explosions, which he called {{nanoflares|NANOFLARES}}. As magnetic field lines twist and cross, they snap and reconnect, releasing bursts of energy. Each is a miniature version of a giant solar flare. [pause] Millions of them, every second, could keep the corona boiling.

> i: solar orbiter | v: spacecraft
In 2020, the European Space Agency's Solar Orbiter spacecraft sent back its first close-up images of the Sun and revealed something new: tiny flashes all over the surface, which scientists nicknamed {{campfires|CAMPFIRES}}. They were far smaller than ordinary flares, but there were many of them. [pause] They might be part of the nanoflare story.

> i: inouye solar telescope | v: telescope dome
Also in 2020, the giant Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope in Hawaii released the most detailed images ever taken of the Sun's surface, showing a pattern of bubbling cells, each roughly the size of a country, with bright magnetic lanes between them. [pause] The engine of the corona, seen in unprecedented detail.

## Touching The Sun
mood: wonder
> i: parker solar probe | v: parker solar probe
In 2018, NASA launched the {{Parker Solar Probe|PARKER SOLAR PROBE}}, named after Eugene Parker himself. It was the first NASA mission ever named after a living person. Parker watched the launch.

> i: parker solar probe | v: parker solar probe
Using repeated flybys of Venus to shrink its orbit, the probe has dived closer and closer to the Sun. In 2021, it crossed into the corona itself, the first spacecraft ever to fly through the Sun's atmosphere. [pause] Humanity had, in a sense, touched the Sun.

> i: parker solar probe heat shield | v: parker solar probe
Behind its heat shield, the instruments measured magnetic fields and particles in a region no spacecraft had ever visited. They found sudden S-shaped kinks in the magnetic field, called switchbacks, which seem to start low in the Sun's atmosphere. They may help heat the corona and power the solar wind.

> i: parker solar probe | v: sun
On the twenty fourth of December 2024, Parker made its closest approach, passing about six million kilometres from the Sun's surface at almost seven hundred thousand kilometres per hour, the fastest object humans have ever built. It survived, and kept sending back data.

## Why It Matters
mood: wonder
> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
The corona is not just a curiosity. It is the source of the solar wind, the stream of particles that flows past the Earth, and of the huge eruptions that can trigger solar storms, knock out satellites and threaten power grids.

> i: sun | v: sun
The Sun's activity rises and falls in a cycle of roughly eleven years. Around 2024 and 2025, it reached the peak of its latest cycle, with more sunspots, more flares and more eruptions from the corona. That peak brought the brilliant auroras seen across much of the world in May 2024.

> i: aurora | v: aurora
Understanding how the corona is heated means understanding the space weather that affects our technology. It may also help us understand the stars that host other planets, and whether their winds and flares make those planets habitable.

> i: total solar eclipse | v: total solar eclipse
The mystery is not completely solved. The answer may turn out to be a mix of waves and tiny explosions, working together. [pause] But for the first time, we have a spacecraft inside the Sun's crown, measuring the fire directly. The next time you see a total eclipse, and that ghostly halo appears around the black Moon, you will be looking at one of the greatest puzzles in the solar system.
