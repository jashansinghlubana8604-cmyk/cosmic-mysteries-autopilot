On the twenty first of May 2019, three detectors on Earth, two in the United States and one in Italy, trembled together for about a tenth of a second. The signal was tiny, a ripple in space itself, smaller than the width of a proton. It had been travelling towards us for around seven billion years.

One of them sat right inside a range of masses where, according to our best understanding of how stars live and die, black holes should not be able to form at all. Physicists call it the {{mass gap|MASS GAP}}.

## Listening To Space
mood: wonder
> i: ligo | v: gravitational waves
When two black holes spiral into each other, they shake the fabric of space and time, sending out ripples called gravitational waves. Albert Einstein predicted them in 1916, but he doubted they could ever be measured.

> i: ligo detector | v: laser
Detecting them took giant instruments called {{LIGO|LIGO}}, in Washington and Louisiana, and later {{Virgo|VIRGO}}, near Pisa in Italy. Each one fires laser beams down tunnels several kilometres long, measuring changes in their length to a tiny fraction of the width of a proton. In 2015, LIGO caught its first signal, from two black holes merging over a billion light-years away. [pause] A new way of seeing the universe was born.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
The shape of each signal reveals the masses of the two black holes. Heavier pairs merge at lower frequencies, producing a shorter, deeper signal. Most early detections came from black holes ten to forty times the mass of the Sun. [pause] GW190521 was different. It was so heavy that the detectors only caught its final few cycles, a brief deep thump instead of a long rising chirp.

## The Heaviest Collision
mood: epic
> i: binary black hole | v: black hole merger
When the analysis was published in September 2020, the numbers were startling. The two black holes were about {{eighty five|85 SUNS}} and sixty six times the mass of the Sun. They merged into a single black hole of about {{one hundred and forty|142 SUNS}}.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
The missing mass, about eight times the mass of the Sun, was converted directly into gravitational waves, released in a fraction of a second. For that brief moment, the collision was emitting more power than all the stars in the visible universe shining together.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
The final black hole was the first clear detection of a so-called {{intermediate-mass black hole|INTERMEDIATE-MASS}}, heavier than a hundred Suns but far lighter than the giants at the centres of galaxies. Astronomers had long searched for this missing middle class of black holes. [pause] Now one had announced itself through the shaking of space.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
And it was far away. The signal had left its source when the universe was about half its present age, long before the Earth existed. [pause] It is one of the most distant black hole collisions ever detected.

## The Forbidden Gap
mood: dread
> i: massive star | v: star explosion
The problem was the heavier of the two original black holes. Black holes like these are thought to form when massive stars run out of fuel and collapse. But theory says that the very largest stars cannot leave black holes of certain sizes behind.

> i: star core | v: star
Inside a giant star, more than about a hundred times the mass of the Sun, the core becomes so hot that its high-energy light starts turning into pairs of particles: electrons and their antimatter twins, positrons. That robs the core of the pressure holding it up. It suddenly shrinks, heats further, and ignites in a runaway thermonuclear explosion.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
This is called a {{pair-instability supernova|PAIR-INSTABILITY}}. For the heaviest stars, it blows the entire star apart, leaving nothing behind. Slightly smaller stars survive, but shed huge amounts of mass in violent pulses before collapsing. [pause] Either way, there should be no black holes from single stars between roughly sixty five and a hundred and thirty times the mass of the Sun.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
That forbidden range is the mass gap. And the eighty five solar mass black hole in GW190521 sat right in the middle of it. [pause] Something had made a black hole that stars, on their own, should not be able to make.

## How Did It Get There?
mood: mystery
> i: globular cluster | v: star cluster
One explanation is that the black holes were not made directly by stars. They might be the products of earlier collisions. In a crowded star cluster, smaller black holes could merge, and the merged black hole could then find another partner and merge again. [pause] Black holes built up step by step, like a family tree.

> i: galaxy core | v: black hole accretion disk
Another idea places the collision near a supermassive black hole. Smaller black holes could be trapped in the vast disc of gas swirling around it, growing heavier as they swallow gas and merging with each other again and again.

> i: quasar | v: black hole accretion disk
Intriguingly, about a month after GW190521, a telescope in California recorded a flare from a bright active galaxy, a {{quasar|QUASAR}}, in roughly the same region of the sky. Some astronomers suggested that the newly merged black hole had been kicked through the gas disc around that galaxy's central giant, lighting it up. [pause] Others argue the match could be coincidence. The connection remains unproven.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
Other explanations are more exotic. Perhaps the black holes formed in the very early universe, directly from dense regions of matter, without any stars at all. Some researchers even tested whether the signal could have come from colliding objects made of hypothetical particles, rather than black holes. [pause] The signal was so short that several interpretations fit it.

> i: physics laboratory | v: star
Others suspect the theory of the gap itself may need adjusting. Its exact edges depend on nuclear reactions inside stars that are hard to measure in laboratories, and on how massive stars lose material over their lives. [pause] The gap might be narrower than we thought, or start at a higher mass.

## Heavier Still
mood: epic
> i: ligo | v: gravitational waves
GW190521 was not the last surprise. In November 2023, the detectors caught a signal now called GW231123, announced in 2025. Two black holes, each estimated at more than a hundred times the mass of the Sun, merged into one of around {{two hundred and twenty five|225 SUNS}}, the heaviest merger detected so far.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
Both black holes also appeared to be spinning extremely fast, close to the limit allowed by Einstein's theory. That is another clue that they may have formed from earlier mergers, because collisions tend to leave behind spinning black holes. [pause] Again, black holes where single stars should not put them.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
Each new detection adds to a growing catalogue of hundreds of collisions. Together they are revealing the hidden population of black holes across the universe, how they pair up, how they grow and where they live. [pause] And future detectors, more sensitive and even placed in space, should hear far more of them.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
A tenth of a second of trembling, from seven billion years ago. A collision between objects that should not exist. [pause] GW190521 showed that the universe can build black holes in ways we are still trying to understand. And it reminded us that every time we find a new way to listen, the cosmos has something unexpected to say.
