In the constellation Libra, about two hundred light-years from Earth, there is a faint star you can see with a pair of binoculars. It has no name, only a catalogue number: {{HD 140283|HD 140283}}.

> i: old star | v: star
For years, it looked like a perfectly ordinary star. Then astronomers measured its age. [pause] The answer was around sixteen billion years.

> i: big bang | v: big bang
There was a problem. The universe itself is only about thirteen point eight billion years old. A star cannot be older than the universe it lives in.

> i: methuselah star | v: star twinkling
The press named it the {{Methuselah star|METHUSELAH STAR}}, after the oldest man in the Bible. [pause] And solving its paradox would tell us something important about how we measure time in the cosmos.

## A Visitor From The Halo
mood: wonder
> i: milky way | v: milky way
Most of the stars near the Sun circle the centre of our galaxy together, in a flat disc. But HD 140283 is not one of them. It is racing through our neighbourhood at around {{1.3 million kilometres per hour|1.3 MILLION KM/H}}, on a steep, looping orbit.

> i: milky way halo | v: milky way
It belongs to the galactic {{halo|HALO}}, a vast, sparse cloud of very old stars surrounding the disc of the Milky Way. Halo stars are survivors from our galaxy's earliest days, and some may have come from small galaxies that the Milky Way swallowed long ago. [pause] HD 140283 is only passing through. In a few million years, it will be gone from our part of the sky.

> i: star field | v: star field
That speed is why it caught astronomers' attention more than a century ago. Stars racing past us much faster than their neighbours are often halo stars on unusual orbits. [pause] By sorting stars by their speed and chemistry, astronomers have learned to pick out these ancient wanderers among the crowd.

> i: star spectrum | v: star
What makes it special is its chemistry. When the universe began, it contained only hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium. Every heavier element, like the carbon, oxygen and iron in your body, was made later, inside stars, and spread through space when they died.

> i: old star | v: star
So the first generations of stars were almost pure hydrogen and helium. Later stars formed from gas enriched by earlier ones, and contain more heavy elements. [pause] HD 140283 has only around {{one two hundred and fiftieth|1/250}} as much iron as the Sun. It must have formed very early, before many stars had lived and died.

## Measuring A Star's Age
mood: mystery
> i: star | v: star twinkling
How do you measure the age of a star? You cannot see its birth certificate. Instead, astronomers rely on our understanding of how stars change as they burn their fuel.

> i: subgiant star | v: red giant star
HD 140283 is a {{subgiant|SUBGIANT}}. It has used up the hydrogen in its core and is beginning to swell. That stage is very useful for dating, because the star's brightness at this point depends strongly on its age. [pause] Measure exactly how bright it really is, and you can work out how long it has been shining.

> i: star field | v: star field
But that requires knowing its distance precisely. A star that looks faint could be dim and close, or bright and far away. [pause] Any error in distance becomes an error in brightness, and so an error in age.

> i: star | v: star
There is another complication. A star's colour and brightness also depend on what it is made of. A star with fewer heavy elements burns slightly differently, so its age can only be calculated if its chemistry is measured precisely too. [pause] Every number in the calculation carries its own small uncertainty.

> i: hipparcos satellite | v: satellite
In 2000, using distance measurements from the European Hipparcos satellite, astronomers estimated its age at around sixteen billion years. The uncertainty was large, about two billion years either way. [pause] But even at the lower end, it was uncomfortably close to the age of the universe.

## The Paradox Deepens
mood: dread
> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
In 2013, a team led by Howard Bond used the Hubble Space Telescope to measure the star's distance more precisely. They watched it shift slightly against the background as the Earth went around the Sun, a method called parallax.

> i: old star | v: star
With better distance, they calculated an age of about {{14.5 billion years|14.5 BILLION YEARS}}, give or take eight hundred million years. Still older than the universe, though now the two numbers overlapped within the uncertainties. [pause] It could be as young as about thirteen point seven billion years.

> i: big bang | v: early universe
Even so, that would mean the star formed almost immediately after the Big Bang, within a few hundred million years at most. That was hard to believe for a star already containing some heavy elements made by earlier stars. [pause] Something about the measurement, or the models, had to give.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The age of the universe is measured in a completely different way, from the afterglow of the Big Bang and the expansion of space. The Planck satellite pinned it at around {{13.8 billion years|13.8 BILLION}}. [pause] Two independent clocks, and they seemed to disagree.

## It Happened Before
mood: mystery
> i: globular cluster | v: globular cluster
Astronomers had faced this kind of crisis before. In the early 1990s, the oldest star clusters in our galaxy, called {{globular clusters|GLOBULAR CLUSTERS}}, seemed to be around fifteen to eighteen billion years old. At the time, measurements of the expanding universe suggested it was only ten to fifteen billion.

> i: hipparcos satellite | v: satellite orbiting earth
Two developments resolved it. First, the Hipparcos satellite showed that the clusters were farther away than thought, which made their stars brighter and therefore younger.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Second, in 1998, studies of distant exploding stars revealed that the expansion of the universe is speeding up, driven by what we now call dark energy. That made the universe older than astronomers had calculated. [pause] The two clocks moved towards each other, and the crisis disappeared.

> i: star field | v: star field
So when the Methuselah star appeared, most astronomers suspected a similar answer: a small error hidden somewhere in the measurements or in the physics of the star. [pause] The universe was probably fine.

## The Answer
mood: wonder
> i: old star | v: star
Several things could make the star seem older than it is. The models depend on details like how much oxygen the star contains, how heavy elements sink slowly inside it over billions of years, and how heat moves through its outer layers. Small changes in any of them shift the age by hundreds of millions of years.

> i: gaia satellite | v: gaia satellite
The European Gaia satellite has since measured the star's distance with even greater precision. In 2021, researchers combined Gaia's measurements with improved models of the star and found an age of roughly {{twelve billion years|12 BILLION YEARS}}. [pause] Ancient, but comfortably younger than the universe.

> i: milky way | v: milky way
So the Methuselah star was not breaking the laws of physics. It is simply one of the oldest stars near us, born when the universe was only a couple of billion years old, perhaps before the Milky Way had even settled into its familiar shape.

> i: old star | v: star twinkling
Astronomers have since found other ancient stars with even less iron, such as one called SMSS J0313-6708, which contains less than one ten millionth as much iron as the Sun. [pause] It may be a direct descendant of the very first stars.

> i: star field | v: star field
These stars are living fossils. They carry the chemistry of the young universe in their atmospheres and hold the record of how the first heavy elements were made. [pause] And HD 140283 left us with an important lesson: when one of nature's clocks seems to break the rules, the answer is usually that we have not yet learned to read it properly.
