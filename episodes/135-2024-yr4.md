In the last days of 2024, a telescope in Chile spotted a small asteroid that had just passed the Earth. It was about the size of a large building. Astronomers calculated its orbit, as they do for thousands of new asteroids every year.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
But this one was different. As more observations came in, the chance that it would hit the Earth in December 2032 kept rising. One percent. Two percent. By February 2025, it reached about {{3 percent|3.1%}}. [pause] The highest ever calculated for an asteroid of its size.

> i: asteroid impact | v: asteroid impact
It was nicknamed a city killer. Not big enough to end civilisation, but big enough to devastate a city if it struck one. Space agencies around the world began preparing contingency plans.

> i: moon | v: moon
Then, just days later, the danger to Earth vanished. But a new possibility appeared. [pause] The asteroid might hit the Moon instead.

## Found After It Passed
mood: mystery
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
On the twenty seventh of December 2024, a telescope belonging to the ATLAS survey, a network designed to give warning of asteroids on a collision course, spotted a new object. It had already made a close pass by the Earth a couple of days earlier. It was given the name {{2024 YR4|2024 YR4}}.

> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
ATLAS stands for the Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System. Its telescopes, spread across Hawaii, Chile and South Africa, scan the whole visible sky every night, looking for anything moving that should not be there. [pause] It was built for exactly this kind of discovery.

> i: asteroid | v: asteroid rotation
At first, its size was uncertain, perhaps forty to ninety metres across, based on how bright it looked. Its orbit took it around the Sun every four years, crossing the Earth's path. And when astronomers projected that orbit forward, they found a possible close encounter on the twenty second of December 2032.

> i: earth orbit | v: earth orbit
With only a few weeks of observations, the uncertainty in its future position was large, a long, stretched region of space. The Earth sat inside that region. That meant there was a small chance of impact. [pause] And as is common, early calculations rise before they fall.

## The Odds Climb
mood: dread
> i: asteroid earth flyby | v: asteroid flyby
Why would the odds rise before falling? Imagine the uncertainty as a long cloud of possible positions. At first, the Earth covers a tiny fraction of it. As new measurements arrive, the cloud shrinks. If the Earth still lies inside the shrinking cloud, it covers a bigger and bigger fraction, and the probability goes up.

> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
By February 2025, the impact probability reached about three percent, putting the asteroid at level three on the {{Torino scale|TORINO SCALE}}, the system used to rate impact threats. For the first time, international groups set up to coordinate planetary defence formally started following an asteroid this closely.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
Astronomers even mapped the possible impact zone, a narrow band stretching across the Earth through the eastern Pacific, northern South America, the Atlantic, Africa, the Arabian Sea and South Asia. Somewhere along that line, if the asteroid hit, it would come down. [pause] Millions of people lived within it.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
Scientists stressed that there was no reason to panic. A ninety seven percent chance of a miss is still very good odds. [pause] And they expected that, with more data, the cloud would most likely shrink until the Earth fell outside it.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
That is exactly what happened. Within days, new observations pushed the Earth out of the uncertainty region. The impact probability for the Earth dropped to effectively zero. [pause] The system had worked exactly as designed.

## Webb Takes A Look
mood: wonder
> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
In March 2025, the James Webb Space Telescope observed the asteroid. Its heat measurements gave a much better estimate of its size: about sixty metres across, roughly the height of a fifteen-storey building. Other observations suggested it spins quickly, turning once in roughly twenty minutes, and is shaped somewhat like a flattened disc rather than a ball.

> i: tunguska | v: forest
An object that size, hitting the Earth, could release energy of several megatons, hundreds of times the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. It would explode in the atmosphere or strike the ground, devastating an area tens of kilometres across. Something similar happened in 1908, over Tunguska in Siberia, flattening around two thousand square kilometres of forest.

> i: asteroid | v: asteroid
And as observations continued to refine its path, the odds of the asteroid hitting the Moon in December 2032 rose, to around {{four percent|~4%}}. [pause] Small. But real.

## If It Hits The Moon
mood: dread
> i: moon | v: moon
If 2024 YR4 did hit the Moon, it would strike at around fifteen kilometres per second, releasing energy equivalent to a large nuclear explosion. It would blast out a new crater perhaps around a kilometre wide.

> i: moon impact | v: moon surface
The flash of the impact could be visible from the Earth, depending on where on the Moon it struck. It would be the largest impact on the Moon ever witnessed by human beings. [pause] Scientists would study it for decades.

> i: moon surface | v: moon surface
The Moon is hit all the time, mostly by tiny rocks. Telescopes on Earth regularly catch brief flashes of light on its dark side, as small meteoroids strike and vaporise. With no atmosphere to protect it, every impact reaches the surface. [pause] But an object the size of 2024 YR4 would be in a completely different league.

> i: satellites orbit | v: satellites orbit
There is a possible side effect. Some studies suggested that a fraction of the material thrown off the Moon could escape and drift into space, and some might eventually reach the region around the Earth. Small fragments could slightly raise the risk to satellites for a while, and produce an unusual meteor shower. [pause] The Earth would not be in danger, but our satellites might need to be careful.

## Watching And Waiting
mood: mystery
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
Right now, 2024 YR4 is too far away and too faint for most telescopes to see. Astronomers expect to observe it again when it comes closer, around 2028. Those observations should tell us, almost certainly, whether it will hit the Moon or miss.

> i: space telescope | v: satellite orbit
NASA is also building a dedicated space telescope, called NEO Surveyor, designed to find hard-to-spot asteroids by the heat they give off, including dark ones that ground-based telescopes struggle to see. [pause] The goal is to find most of the city killers long before they come close.

> i: dart impact | v: dart impact
Some researchers have even suggested missions to visit it, or to deliberately push it off course to protect the Moon and nearby satellites, using the kind of technology tested in 2022 by NASA's DART mission, which crashed into an asteroid and changed its orbit.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
2024 YR4 was a real-world test of planetary defence. An asteroid was spotted, tracked, measured and assessed, and the world's response was calm and scientific. [pause] It reminded us that we live in a busy solar system. And that, for the first time in history, we are watching the sky closely enough to see what is coming.
