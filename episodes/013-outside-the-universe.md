## The Edge We Can See
mood: wonder
> v: zoom deep field | i: hubble ultra deep field
First, we need to separate two very different things. The observable universe, and the universe itself.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: early universe
The observable universe is everything close enough that its light has had time to reach us since the Big Bang. Because space has been expanding, that region now stretches about forty six billion light years in every direction. Beyond it is a horizon, like the horizon at sea. Not an edge of the world, just the limit of our view.

> v: galaxies | i: galaxy cluster
Almost every cosmologist believes the universe continues past that horizon. If you could stand on a galaxy at the very edge of our view, you would see galaxies stretching on in every direction, and your own observable universe, centred on you.

> i: universe | v: zoom out universe
So when we ask what is outside the universe, we are really asking two questions. What lies beyond what we can see? And does the universe as a whole have an outside at all?

> v: galaxies moving apart | i: expanding universe
And the observable universe is slowly shrinking in a sense. Because the expansion is accelerating, distant galaxies are being carried away faster and faster. Some of the galaxies we can see today are already so far away that light they send now will never reach us. Over billions of years, more and more of the universe will slip beyond our horizon, forever.

## Is There An Edge?
mood: mystery
> v: cosmic web simulation | i: cosmic web
Here is something counterintuitive. The universe is not expanding into anything. It is not a balloon inflating inside a bigger room. Space itself is stretching, everywhere at once, and there need not be anything outside it for that to happen.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Then what shape is it? Einstein showed that space can be curved. A universe could curve back on itself like the surface of a sphere, finite but without any edge. Or it could be flat, and stretch on forever.

> v: planck satellite | i: planck map
We can measure this. By studying patterns in the oldest light in the universe, the cosmic microwave background, the Planck satellite found that space appears to be {{almost perfectly flat|FLAT}}, to within a fraction of a percent.

> i: deep field | v: zoom deep field
That means either the universe is infinite, or it is so enormous that the part we can see is a tiny fraction of the whole, like a single grain of sand on a vast beach, where the beach looks flat because we cannot see enough of it to notice the curve.

> v: galaxies infinite | i: galaxies
If it is truly infinite, the consequences are dizzying. An infinite universe, filled with matter, might contain every possible arrangement of atoms, somewhere. Including, perhaps, distant copies of the Earth, and of you, reading this exact sentence. [pause] It is not proof. But it follows from the mathematics.

## Before The Beginning
mood: mystery
> v: big bang | i: big bang
Asking what is outside the universe is closely linked to asking what came before it. The Big Bang was not an explosion in space. It was the rapid expansion of space itself, about thirteen point eight billion years ago.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
Our best theories suggest that, in the first tiny fraction of a second, the universe went through a burst of incredibly rapid expansion called {{inflation|INFLATION}}. In less time than it takes light to cross an atom, a region smaller than a proton may have grown larger than a city.

> v: cosmic inflation | i: cosmic microwave background
Inflation explains why the universe is so smooth and flat. It also leaves faint fingerprints in the cosmic microwave background, which match what we observe remarkably well.

> i: big bang | v: big bang simulation
But what triggered inflation, and what existed before it? Here, our physics runs out. At the very beginning, gravity and quantum mechanics both become important, and we do not yet have a theory that unites them. Some physicists think time itself began at the Big Bang, so asking what came before may be like asking what is north of the North Pole.

> i: cyclic universe | v: big bang
Others propose that the Big Bang was not the beginning at all, but a bounce. A previous universe may have contracted, then rebounded into ours. Some even imagine an endless cycle of universes, each born from the remains of the last. Roger Penrose, a Nobel laureate, has argued that faint circles in the cosmic microwave background might be traces of a universe that came before ours. Most cosmologists are unconvinced. But the idea shows how open this question still is.

## The Multiverse
mood: wonder
> v: bubble universe | i: bubble nebula
And then there is the most extraordinary idea of all. Many versions of inflation suggest that once it starts, it never fully stops. In most of space, it continues forever. But here and there, small regions slow down, cool, and become universes like ours.

> i: bubble nebula | v: bubbles in space
This is called {{eternal inflation|ETERNAL INFLATION}}. In this picture, our universe is one bubble in an endlessly expanding sea, surrounded by other bubbles, each one a separate universe, perhaps with its own laws of physics.

> v: cosmic microwave background | i: cmb cold spot
Could we ever see another bubble? Some physicists have suggested that if our bubble once collided with another, it might have left a bruise in the cosmic microwave background. There is a strange, unusually cold region in that ancient light, called the Cold Spot. Most astronomers think it has a more ordinary explanation. But the search for bruises from other universes is a real field of research.

> i: galaxies | v: zoom out universe
Other theories imagine our universe as a membrane floating in higher dimensions, or as one branch among countless others in quantum mechanics. Each idea is mathematically serious. None has yet been proven.

## The Honest Answer
mood: wonder
> v: star field slow | i: dark space stars
So what is outside the universe? The honest answer is that we do not know. It may be nothing at all, not even empty space, because space itself belongs to the universe.

> i: universe | v: cosmic web simulation
It may be more of the same, galaxies stretching on forever, far beyond anything we will ever see. Or it may be an endless multiverse, where our entire cosmos is one bubble among countless others.

> v: earth from space | i: earth night
What is remarkable is that we can ask the question at all. A species living on a small planet, around an ordinary star, has worked out the age of the universe, its shape, and the first fraction of a second of its existence.

> i: hubble ultra deep field | v: zoom deep field
We may never see beyond the horizon. [pause] But the question of what lies outside will keep pulling us forward, the way the horizon has always pulled explorers to the edge of every map.
