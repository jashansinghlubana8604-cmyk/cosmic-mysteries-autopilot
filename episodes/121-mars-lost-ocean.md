Today, Mars is a frozen desert. Its air is about one percent as thick as the Earth's. Its surface is drier than any desert on our planet. Liquid water cannot last on it for long before it freezes or boils away.

> i: ancient mars water | v: mars surface
But billions of years ago, Mars looked very different. Rivers carved valleys across its surface. Lakes filled its craters. Water flowed in such volumes that it carved channels larger than any on Earth. And many scientists believe a great ocean once covered much of its northern half.

> i: mars ocean | v: mars orbit
Mars may once have been a blue world, with clouds, rain and seas, not so different from the young Earth. [pause] Then it lost almost everything.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
Where did all that water go? Some escaped into space. Some may be frozen at the poles. And in 2024, scientists suggested that an enormous amount might still be there, deep underground. [pause] This is the mystery of Mars's lost ocean.

## Rivers On A Dry World
mood: wonder
> i: mariner 9 | v: mars orbit
In 1971, NASA's Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft to orbit Mars. When the dust storms cleared, it revealed something astonishing: dry riverbeds. Branching valleys, winding channels and giant flood channels, carved into the surface.

> i: mars channels | v: mars surface
Since then, orbiters have mapped thousands of ancient valley networks across Mars, along with river deltas, where rivers once flowed into lakes and dropped their sediment, and minerals such as clays that only form in the presence of liquid water.

> i: curiosity rover | v: mars rover
In 2012, the Curiosity rover landed in Gale Crater and found rocks made from layers of fine mud, laid down at the bottom of an ancient lake that may have lasted for millions of years. It also found rounded pebbles, smoothed by flowing streams. [pause] Mars once had running water, and lots of it.

> i: jezero crater | v: mars surface
In 2021, the Perseverance rover landed in {{Jezero Crater|JEZERO CRATER}}, chosen because it contains a beautiful fan-shaped delta, the remains of a river flowing into a crater lake. Perseverance is collecting rock samples there, which scientists hope one day to bring back to Earth.

> i: sun | v: sun
There is a puzzle in all this. Four billion years ago, the young Sun was about thirty percent dimmer than it is today. Mars, further away than the Earth, should have been frozen solid. To keep water liquid, it must have had a thick atmosphere, rich in greenhouse gases, trapping the faint sunlight's warmth. [pause] Exactly how warm and wet it was is still debated.

## An Ocean In The North
mood: mystery
> i: mars topography | v: mars rotation
Mars has a strange shape. Its northern hemisphere is mostly smooth, low-lying plains, kilometres lower than the rugged, cratered highlands of the south. If Mars once had a lot of water, the north is exactly where it would have collected.

> i: mars ocean | v: mars orbit
Some scientists have proposed that an ocean, sometimes called {{Oceanus Borealis|OCEANUS BOREALIS}}, once filled those northern lowlands, more than three and a half billion years ago. They have pointed to features that look like ancient shorelines, traced around the edge of the plains.

> i: zhurong rover | v: mars rover
The idea has been hotly debated for decades. Then, in 2025, scientists analysed radar data from China's Zhurong rover, which had probed beneath the surface of the northern plains. They found buried layers that slope gently, like the sand deposits of a beach. [pause] They argued it was evidence of an ancient coastline, though not everyone is convinced.

> i: asteroid impact | v: ocean waves
Some researchers have even found signs of giant tsunamis. Deposits along the edge of the northern plains look like they were left by enormous waves, perhaps triggered by asteroid impacts into the ancient sea more than three billion years ago. [pause] If there were tsunamis, there was an ocean for them to cross.

## Where It Went
mood: dread
> i: maven spacecraft | v: spacecraft
So what happened? One big part of the answer comes from NASA's {{MAVEN|MAVEN}} spacecraft, which has orbited Mars since 2014, measuring how its atmosphere escapes into space.

> i: solar wind | v: solar wind
The Earth is protected by a strong magnetic field, which deflects most of the solar wind, the stream of charged particles from the Sun. Mars once had a magnetic field too. But around four billion years ago, it seems to have faded away, perhaps as the planet's small core cooled. Its rocks still remember it. Orbiting spacecraft have found stripes of magnetism frozen into the ancient southern crust, a fossil of the field that once protected the planet.

> i: mars atmosphere | v: mars
Without that shield, the solar wind began stripping away Mars's atmosphere, molecule by molecule. MAVEN measured that even today, Mars loses around a hundred grams of air every second. Over billions of years, especially when the young Sun was more active, much of the atmosphere was lost. [pause] And as the air thinned, liquid water could no longer survive on the surface.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
Water molecules high in the atmosphere were broken apart by sunlight, and the light hydrogen escaped into space. Scientists can see the evidence in the hydrogen left behind. Mars's water is far richer in a heavy form of hydrogen than the Earth's, a sign that much of the lighter kind has escaped.

## Hidden Underground
mood: mystery
> i: mars poles | v: mars
But not all the water was lost. Mars still has polar ice caps, made largely of water ice, and buried ice spread under large parts of its surface. If all of the ice in its polar caps melted, it could cover the planet in a layer of water tens of metres deep.

> i: mars interior | v: mars interior
And some studies suggest that much of the ancient water never left at all. Instead, it may have been locked away in minerals, chemically trapped inside clays and other rocks in the Martian crust. [pause] One 2021 study argued that this could account for a large fraction of the water Mars once had.

> i: insight lander | v: mars surface
Then, in 2024, scientists analysing data from NASA's {{InSight|INSIGHT}} lander, which listened to marsquakes, proposed something remarkable. The way seismic waves travelled through the crust suggested that rock between about eleven and twenty kilometres down might be soaked with liquid water.

> i: mars interior | v: mars
If that is right, there could be enough water hidden deep underground to cover the whole planet in an ocean one or two kilometres deep. [pause] The lost ocean of Mars may not be lost at all. It may be buried, far too deep to reach with any drill we have today.

## A Second Earth That Failed
mood: wonder
> i: early earth | v: early earth
Mars and the Earth started out not so differently. Both had water, thicker atmospheres and active interiors. But Mars is smaller. It cooled faster, lost its magnetic field and could not hold onto its air.

> i: perseverance rover | v: mars rover
That is why scientists are so interested in Mars's ancient past. If life ever began on Earth, it may also have begun on early Mars, when it had lakes and rivers. Its traces could still be preserved in the rocks of those ancient lakebeds. [pause] That is exactly what Perseverance is searching for.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
Mars is a warning, and a time capsule. It shows us what happens when a planet loses its protection. And it may hold the answer to whether life began twice in our solar system. [pause] Somewhere under those red plains, the memory of an ocean is still waiting to be read.
