## A Planet Found With Mathematics
mood: wonder
> v: neptune | i: neptune
This would not be the first time a planet was found before anyone saw it. In the 1840s, astronomers noticed that Uranus was not moving exactly as predicted. Something seemed to be tugging on it.

> i: neptune voyager | v: neptune voyager
A French mathematician, Urbain Le Verrier, calculated where an unseen planet would have to be to explain the pull. He sent his prediction to an observatory in Berlin. On the very first night they looked, in September 1846, they found it, almost exactly where he said it would be. [pause] It was Neptune.

> v: solar system | i: solar system planets
A planet discovered with a pen before it was seen through a telescope. It was one of the greatest triumphs in the history of science. And it set a precedent. When things move strangely, sometimes it is because something unseen is pulling on them.

> i: lowell observatory | v: observatory night
The success inspired a new hunt. In the early 1900s, the astronomer Percival Lowell became convinced that another planet, which he called Planet X, lay beyond Neptune. He searched for it until his death. In 1930, at his observatory, a young astronomer finally found a new world, and it was named Pluto. But Pluto turned out to be far too small to pull on anything. The real Planet X, if it existed, was never found.

## The Strange Orbits
mood: mystery
> i: sedna | v: sedna
In 2003, astronomers discovered Sedna, a reddish world about a thousand kilometres across, on one of the most extreme orbits ever seen. At its closest, it comes no nearer than seventy six times the Earth's distance from the Sun. At its furthest, it goes out to around nine hundred times that distance. One orbit takes more than ten thousand years.

> v: kuiper belt objects | i: kuiper belt
Nothing we knew of could have pushed Sedna onto that orbit. It never comes close enough to Neptune to be thrown out there. Something else must have done it.

> i: distant solar system | v: solar system orbits
As astronomers found more of these extremely distant objects, a pattern emerged. In 2016, two astronomers at Caltech, Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown, pointed out that the most distant ones had orbits that were clustered together, tilted in the same way and pointing in roughly the same direction.

> v: planet nine | i: planet nine artist
By chance, that should almost never happen. Their computer models suggested a simple explanation: a hidden planet, perhaps five to ten times the mass of the Earth, on a long, tilted orbit, hundreds of times further from the Sun than we are, sculpting the orbits of everything near it.

> v: planetary system formation | i: young planetary system
How could a planet end up so far away? One possibility is that it formed among the giant planets, close to the Sun, and was thrown outward by Jupiter or Saturn early in the solar system's history. Another is that it was captured from a passing star, when the Sun was still young and surrounded by sibling stars in its birth cluster. Either way, it would be a refugee, a planet living in exile.

## Why We Haven't Seen It
mood: mystery
> i: dark space stars | v: star field
If it is so big, why has no one found it? Because it would be astonishingly faint. At that distance, the Sun would look like just a bright star. Any sunlight that reached Planet Nine, bounced off it, and travelled all the way back to Earth would be extremely weak.

> v: telescope observatory night | i: observatory night
It would also move incredibly slowly across the sky, taking ten to twenty thousand years to complete a single orbit. And we do not know where along that orbit it is. It could be hiding against the crowded star fields of the Milky Way, where a faint dot is almost impossible to pick out.

> i: infrared sky survey | v: infrared sky
Astronomers have already searched large parts of the sky, ruling out the brightest possibilities. Each search narrows down where it can be hiding, if it is there at all.

## Is It Really There?
mood: mystery
> v: kuiper belt | i: kuiper belt objects
Not everyone is convinced. Some astronomers argue that the clustering of orbits may be an illusion, created by where telescopes happen to have looked. Telescopes tend to find objects in certain parts of the sky, which could make the orbits look more aligned than they really are.

> i: kuiper belt disk | v: debris disc
Others have suggested the pull could come from many smaller bodies, a massive disc of icy debris, rather than a single planet. And one unusual idea, published in 2019, suggested that the object might not be a planet at all, but a tiny black hole, about the size of a grapefruit, with the mass of several Earths.

> v: black hole | i: black hole artist impression
That idea is extremely speculative, but it shows how strange the puzzle is. Whatever is out there, the evidence remains indirect. [pause] Planet Nine, for now, is a shadow cast by mathematics.

> i: kuiper belt | v: kuiper belt objects
New discoveries keep complicating the story. In 2025, astronomers announced another very distant object whose orbit stretches more than a thousand times further than the Earth's from the Sun, and which does not seem to follow the expected pattern. Others have proposed a smaller hidden world, closer in, that could explain a mysterious tilt in the outer Kuiper Belt. The edge of our solar system, it turns out, is far less understood than we thought.

## The Search Is Coming
mood: wonder
> v: rubin observatory | i: rubin observatory
That may soon change. In Chile, the new Vera C. Rubin Observatory began its survey in 2025. It has the largest digital camera ever built for astronomy, and it will photograph the entire southern sky every few nights for ten years.

> i: rubin observatory sky | v: rubin observatory night
Rubin is expected to find thousands of new distant objects beyond Neptune. If Planet Nine exists, and if it is in the southern sky, Rubin has a real chance of seeing it directly. Even if it does not, the orbits of all those new objects will reveal whether the clustering is real.

> v: distant planet | i: planet nine
If it is found, it would be one of the most important discoveries in the history of planetary science. A super Earth, a type of planet common around other stars but missing from our own solar system, could be hiding in our own backyard.

> i: solar system | v: solar system
For thousands of years, people looked up and saw the same planets. [pause] Somewhere out in the dark, there may still be one more, waiting to be seen for the very first time.
