On Mars, there is a volcano so big that it would cover a country the size of Poland. So tall that its summit rises around two and a half times higher than Mount Everest. So wide that if you stood on its top, you would not be able to tell you were on a mountain at all.

> i: olympus mons mars | v: mars
Its edges are ringed by cliffs up to eight kilometres high, taller than any mountain on Earth. Its crater at the summit could swallow a city whole, several times over.

> i: olympus mons | v: mars surface
It is called Olympus Mons, the mountain of the gods. It is the largest volcano in the solar system. [pause] And when the first spacecraft arrived to orbit Mars, it was one of the only things on the whole planet that could be seen.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
How did a planet half the size of the Earth build a mountain so much bigger than anything we have? [pause] The answer tells us something important about why Mars and the Earth turned out so differently.

## Snows Of Olympus
mood: wonder
> i: mars telescope | v: mars rotation
Long before spacecraft, astronomers watching Mars through telescopes noticed a small bright spot that came and went. In the 1870s, the Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli named it {{Nix Olympica|NIX OLYMPICA}}. The Snows of Olympus. He thought it might be a snow-covered peak.

> i: mariner 9 | v: spacecraft
In November 1971, NASA's Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft ever to orbit another planet. But when it arrived, Mars had vanished. A global dust storm had swallowed the entire planet. The cameras saw nothing but a featureless, orange haze.

> i: mars dust storm | v: mars dust storm
Nothing, except four dark spots, poking up through the dust. Over the following weeks, as the storm cleared, scientists realised what they were. The summits of gigantic volcanoes, so tall that they rose above a dust storm covering a whole world. [pause] The biggest of them was the old Snows of Olympus.

> i: mars channels | v: mars orbit
When the dust finally settled, Mariner 9 mapped the whole planet and changed our picture of Mars forever. Not a dead, cratered world like the Moon, but a planet with giant volcanoes, a canyon longer than any on Earth, and dried-up channels that looked as though water had once flowed across the surface in torrents. [pause] Mars had a past. And Olympus Mons was its grandest monument.

## A Mountain Beyond Imagination
mood: wonder
> i: olympus mons | v: olympus mons
Olympus Mons rises around {{22 kilometres|22 KM}} above the average surface of Mars. Mount Everest is about eight point eight kilometres above sea level. Even Mauna Kea in Hawaii, which is about ten kilometres tall when measured from its base on the sea floor, is less than half its height.

> i: olympus mons mars | v: mars orbit
And it is enormous sideways too. It is around six hundred kilometres across, roughly the width of the whole of Arizona, or the distance from London to Edinburgh. Its base is surrounded by a giant cliff, called an escarpment, up to around eight kilometres high in places.

> i: olympus mons caldera | v: mars surface
At the top lies a {{caldera|CALDERA}}, a collapsed crater left behind when underground magma chambers emptied. It is about eighty kilometres long and around three kilometres deep, made of several overlapping craters from different eruptions. [pause] You could drop the city of London into it and still have room to spare.

> i: olympus mons | v: mars surface
But here is the strangest part. The slopes of Olympus Mons are gentle, only around five degrees on average. And it is so wide that the curvature of Mars hides most of it. Standing on the summit, the edges of the mountain would lie beyond the horizon. [pause] You would be on top of the tallest volcano in the solar system and see only a broad plain stretching away into the haze.

## Why So Big?
mood: mystery
> i: hawaii volcano | v: volcano
On Earth, the biggest volcanoes are shield volcanoes, like those of Hawaii, built by runny lava flowing out again and again over long periods. Olympus Mons is the same kind of volcano. Just much, much bigger. So why?

> i: earth tectonic plates | v: earth
One reason is plate tectonics. On the Earth, the crust is broken into plates that slowly drift. Hawaii sits above a hot spot deep in the mantle, but the Pacific plate keeps moving over it. So instead of one giant volcano, the hot spot builds a chain of islands. [pause] Each volcano is carried away before it can grow too large.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
Mars has no moving plates. Its crust stays still. So a hot spot beneath it keeps feeding the same volcano for hundreds of millions, possibly billions, of years. Lava piles on lava, again and again, in the same place.

> i: mars surface | v: mars
And Mars has weaker gravity, only about thirty eight percent of the Earth's. A mountain on Mars can grow far taller before its own weight makes it sag and spread. [pause] Stillness and weak gravity, together, built a giant.

## The Tharsis Bulge
mood: mystery
> i: tharsis | v: mars rotation
Olympus Mons does not stand alone. Nearby lies the {{Tharsis|THARSIS}} region, a vast volcanic plateau thousands of kilometres across, rising about ten kilometres above the surrounding land. Along its crest stand three more giant volcanoes in a neat row, each of them taller than anything on Earth.

> i: tharsis montes | v: mars orbit
Tharsis is so massive that it may have changed the planet itself. Some scientists think its enormous weight caused the whole outer shell of Mars to slowly roll, shifting the planet's poles to new positions. [pause] A pile of lava so heavy it may have tipped a world.

> i: valles marineris | v: mars orbit
And on the edge of Tharsis begins {{Valles Marineris|VALLES MARINERIS}}, a canyon system about four thousand kilometres long and up to seven kilometres deep. Stretched across the United States, it would reach from coast to coast. The forces that raised Tharsis may also have helped tear the crust apart to make it.

## Is It Still Alive?
mood: mystery
> i: olympus mons | v: olympus mons
So is Olympus Mons dead? Probably not completely. Counting the craters on its lava flows tells scientists how old they are. Some flows near the volcano appear surprisingly young, perhaps only tens of millions of years old, or less. Geologically, that is yesterday.

> i: olympus mons | v: mars surface
The mountain itself was built slowly, over perhaps billions of years, by countless eruptions, each adding a thin new skin of lava over the last. Most of what we see today is the result of patient, quiet flows, not giant explosions. [pause] A volcano assembled one layer at a time, for longer than complex life has existed on Earth.

> i: mars interior | v: mars interior
Elsewhere on Mars, NASA's InSight lander detected marsquakes, and some appeared to come from a region with signs of very recent volcanic activity. Mars still seems to have heat inside it, even if much less than it once did.

> i: mars surface | v: mars
Imagine climbing Olympus Mons. You would start on the plains, face the great cliff at its base, then begin a gentle walk uphill that would last for weeks. At the summit, the thin air would be even thinner, and you would often be above the dust that colours the Martian sky. [pause] The sky overhead would be darker, and on a clear day, perhaps tinged deep blue at sunset.

> i: olympus mons mars | v: mars rotation
The largest volcano we know of, on a planet smaller than ours, built by stillness and patience over billions of years. [pause] One day, perhaps, a human being will stand on its summit, look out at an endless plain, and realise that the ground beneath them is the tallest mountain in the solar system.
