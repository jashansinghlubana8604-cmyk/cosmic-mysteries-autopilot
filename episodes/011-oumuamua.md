In October 2017, a telescope in Hawaii spotted a small, faint object racing past the Sun. Within days, astronomers realised something extraordinary. [pause] It did not belong to our solar system.

> v: interstellar object trajectory | i: solar system orbit
It had come from the space between the stars, falling in from the darkness at a speed that no planet, comet or asteroid of ours could ever reach. It was the first visitor from another star system that humanity had ever seen.

> v: oumuamua tumbling | i: elongated asteroid

## A Messenger From Afar
mood: mystery
> v: observatory hawaii | i: pan-starrs telescope
On the 19th of October 2017, astronomer Robert Weryk was checking images from the Pan-STARRS telescope on the summit of Haleakalā, in Hawaii. The telescope's job is to scan the sky for asteroids that might one day threaten Earth.

> v: asteroid moving | i: asteroid
In the images was a dot, moving quickly. Nothing unusual about that. Telescopes like Pan-STARRS find new asteroids all the time. But when astronomers calculated its path, the numbers made no sense.

> v: comet orbit animation | i: solar system orbits
Everything that orbits the Sun moves in a closed loop, an ellipse, coming back again and again. This object was not on a loop. It was on an open curve, called a hyperbola. It had swung around the Sun once, and it was leaving, {{forever|FOREVER}}.

> i: star field | v: flight through stars
The only way to move like that is to arrive from outside the solar system. It had been travelling through interstellar space for perhaps hundreds of millions of years, and by pure chance its path had crossed ours.

> i: vega star lyra | v: star zoom
Tracing its path backwards, astronomers found that it had arrived from the direction of the bright star Vega, in the constellation Lyra. But Vega was not where it is now when 'Oumuamua would have passed it, hundreds of thousands of years ago. Its true home is lost. It could have wandered the galaxy for longer than there have been humans on Earth.

> v: earth from space | i: earth moon
It had already made its closest pass by the Sun in September, unseen. In mid October it swept past the Earth at about sixty times the distance to the Moon, and that is when it was finally caught.

> v: oumuamua artist impression | i: oumuamua
It was given a Hawaiian name, 'Oumuamua, which roughly means a scout, or a messenger from afar arriving first. And by the time anyone knew it was there, it was already heading back out into the dark.

## The Wrong Shape
mood: wonder
> v: telescope night observatory | i: very large telescope night
Astronomers had only a few weeks before it would become too faint to see. Telescopes around the world turned to watch it, including the Very Large Telescope in Chile and the Hubble Space Telescope.

> v: rotating asteroid | i: asteroid shape
The first surprise was its brightness. As 'Oumuamua tumbled through space, the amount of sunlight it reflected changed by a factor of about {{ten|10x}}, every few hours. Ordinary asteroids change brightness by a little as they spin. This was enormous.

> v: oumuamua tumbling | i: oumuamua artist impression
The simplest way to explain that is an extremely elongated object, perhaps up to ten times longer than it is wide, tumbling end over end. Later studies suggested it might instead be flat, like a pancake or a disc. Either way, it was a shape we had never seen in any natural body.

> i: reddish asteroid | v: asteroid closeup
It was small, a few hundred metres long at most, with a reddish surface, the colour of objects that have been exposed to cosmic radiation for a very long time. Every image we have of it is a single point of light. The famous cigar-shaped pictures are artists' impressions, because no telescope could resolve it.

## The Push Nobody Could See
mood: mystery
> v: comet tail sun | i: comet tail
Then came the result that turned a curiosity into a mystery. In 2018, a team led by Marco Micheli published a careful analysis of 'Oumuamua's path. It was not moving exactly as gravity predicted.

> v: object accelerating away from sun | i: oumuamua leaving solar system
As it left the Sun behind, it was being pushed outward by a tiny extra force. Something was giving it a gentle shove, away from the Sun.

> v: comet outgassing | i: comet jets
Comets do this all the time. As sunlight warms them, ice turns to gas and erupts from their surface, acting like a small rocket engine. But comets also grow tails and clouds of gas and dust that we can see. 'Oumuamua had {{nothing|NO TAIL}}. No gas, no dust. The Spitzer Space Telescope looked for the gases a comet should produce, and found none.

> i: spitzer space telescope | v: space telescope
So 'Oumuamua was accelerating like a comet, while looking like a bare rock. Something was pushing it. And no one could see what. [pause] That is when the most famous hypothesis appeared.

## A Sail Of Light?
mood: dread
> v: light sail spacecraft | i: solar sail
Avi Loeb was the chair of Harvard's astronomy department. In late 2018, he and his colleague Shmuel Bialy published a paper with a startling suggestion. What if 'Oumuamua was a {{light sail|LIGHT SAIL}}?

> i: solar sail | v: solar sail deployment
A light sail is a very thin, very large sheet that is pushed by the pressure of sunlight. Humans have already flown them as experiments. A sail like that would be extremely flat, would accelerate away from the Sun without any gas, and would tumble as it travelled. [pause] Exactly like 'Oumuamua.

> v: alien probe artist | i: interstellar probe
In this view, 'Oumuamua could be a piece of technology from another civilisation. Perhaps a working probe, or perhaps debris, like a broken sail drifting between the stars after its makers were long gone.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope array
It was a serious paper, but it was also an extraordinary claim, and most astronomers did not accept it. Radio telescopes listened for any signal from 'Oumuamua as it passed. They heard nothing at all.

> v: comet ice | i: frozen nitrogen pluto
Natural explanations followed. One idea was that it was a fragment of frozen nitrogen, chipped off a Pluto-like world in another solar system. Nitrogen ice would turn to gas that is very hard to detect. Another idea, published in 2023, suggested it was an icy body that had trapped hydrogen gas inside its ice over millions of years of cosmic radiation. As the Sun warmed it, the hydrogen escaped silently, giving exactly the small push that was measured.

> i: comet nucleus | v: comet nucleus
That explanation fits the data remarkably well. But we will never be able to check. By the time any of these ideas were published, 'Oumuamua was far too distant to observe.

## More Visitors Are Coming
mood: wonder
> v: comet 2I/Borisov | i: comet borisov
In 2019, a second interstellar visitor was found, called 2I Borisov. This one was easy to understand. It had a tail, it released gas, it was simply a comet from another star, and it behaved exactly like one.

> v: interstellar comet | i: comet 3I/ATLAS
Then, in July 2025, a third was spotted, named {{3I ATLAS|3I/ATLAS}}, moving even faster through our solar system. It, too, turned out to be an active comet, though its unusual chemistry and speed made it a fascinating target for telescopes around the world.

> v: rubin observatory | i: rubin observatory
And a new observatory, the Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile, has begun scanning the entire southern sky every few nights. Astronomers expect it to find many more interstellar objects, perhaps several every year.

> v: spacecraft intercept comet | i: comet interceptor
Plans already exist to chase one down. The European Space Agency's Comet Interceptor mission is designed to wait in space and then race to meet a newly discovered visitor. Next time, we might get close enough to see what one really looks like.

> i: oumuamua leaving | v: star field
As for 'Oumuamua, it is still out there, coasting away from the Sun at tens of kilometres every second, heading back toward the stars it came from. It will never return. [pause] Whatever it was, a shard of alien ice or something far stranger, it was the first messenger. [pause] And we only had a few weeks to read the message.
