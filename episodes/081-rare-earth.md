Our galaxy contains hundreds of billions of stars. Most of them have planets. Billions of those planets are rocky worlds, about the size of the Earth, in orbits where water could be liquid.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
With numbers like that, it seems almost certain that the galaxy must be full of life. Forests. Oceans teeming with creatures. Perhaps other minds, looking up at other skies.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
But in 2000, a palaeontologist and an astronomer published a book that argued the opposite. Simple life, like bacteria, might be common. But complex life, animals, plants, intelligence, might be astonishingly rare. Perhaps unique. [pause] Because the Earth may be the product of a long chain of lucky accidents.

> i: pale blue dot | v: earth
The idea is called the {{Rare Earth hypothesis|RARE EARTH}}. And the more we learn about our planet's history, the more fascinating, and unsettling, it becomes. [pause] Are we a lucky exception?

[title]

## The Right Place In The Galaxy
mood: mystery
> i: milky way center | v: milky way center
The book was written by palaeontologist Peter Ward and astronomer Donald Brownlee. Their first point was about location. Not every part of the galaxy is safe.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Near the crowded centre of the Milky Way, stars are packed tightly together. Supernova explosions and other violent events are frequent, and radiation could repeatedly sterilise planets. Far out in the galaxy's edges, there are fewer of the heavy elements needed to build rocky planets in the first place.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
Between them lies what some call a {{galactic habitable zone|GALACTIC HABITABLE ZONE}}. A ring of the galaxy that is calm enough and rich enough for life. Our Sun sits comfortably inside it, about twenty six thousand light-years from the centre. [pause] Not too close. Not too far.

> i: sun | v: sun
And our star itself is unusually calm. The Sun is more stable and less prone to violent flares than many stars, especially the red dwarfs that make up most of the galaxy. Its steady light has barely flickered for billions of years.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
Timing matters too. The first stars were made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. The heavier elements needed for rocky planets, iron, silicon, oxygen, had to be built up by generations of stars living and dying. The Earth formed about four and a half billion years ago, when the galaxy had already been enriched for billions of years. [pause] Too early, and there may not have been enough material to build a world like ours.

## A Planet Built Just Right
mood: wonder
> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
Then there is the Moon. It is unusually large compared with the planet it orbits. Its gravity helps keep the Earth's tilt steady, so our seasons stay mild and predictable over millions of years. Without it, some studies suggest the Earth's axis could swing much more wildly, causing drastic climate shifts.

> i: plate tectonics | v: earth tectonic plates
And then {{plate tectonics|PLATE TECTONICS}}. The Earth's crust is broken into moving plates that slowly recycle rock, water and carbon between the surface and the interior. Over millions of years, this acts like a thermostat, helping to keep the climate within a range where liquid water survives. We have not yet confirmed plate tectonics on any other world.

> i: earth magnetic field | v: earth magnetic field
The Earth also has a strong magnetic field, generated by its churning liquid iron core, which helps shield the surface and atmosphere from the solar wind. Mars seems to have lost its field long ago, and much of its atmosphere with it.

> i: earth oceans | v: ocean waves
And the Earth has just the right amount of water. Enough to fill oceans, but not so much that it drowns every continent. Too much water, and land might never have emerged. [pause] Each of these traits on its own seems ordinary. Together, they look like a winning lottery ticket.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotation
Even Jupiter may have played a part. Its enormous gravity shaped where the other planets formed, and it may have helped steer some comets and asteroids away from the inner solar system, while also flinging others towards us. Scientists still argue whether Jupiter has been a shield or a slingshot. [pause] But without it, our solar system would be a very different place.

## The Slow Road To Animals
mood: mystery
> i: early earth | v: early earth
Here is the part that surprises people most. Life appeared on Earth quite early, probably more than three and a half billion years ago. But for most of the Earth's history, that is all there was. Single cells. Microbes. Slime.

> i: stromatolites | v: ocean
Then, around two and a half billion years ago, tiny organisms that made energy from sunlight began pumping oxygen into the air. It took hundreds of millions of years for oxygen to build up. And complex cells, the kind that make up plants and animals, may have arisen from a single, extraordinary event, when one microbe engulfed another and the two began living as one.

> i: cambrian explosion | v: ocean
Complex animals only appeared around six hundred million years ago. The sudden burst of animal diversity called the {{Cambrian explosion|CAMBRIAN EXPLOSION}} happened about five hundred and forty million years ago. [pause] For around three billion years, the Earth was alive, but nothing on it could see, swim or think.

> i: clock | v: time lapse
If you squeezed the Earth's whole history into one day, complex animals would appear in the late evening. And humans would turn up in the final few seconds before midnight. [pause] On this view, the hard part was not starting life. It was getting from microbes to anything more.

## The Case Against
mood: mystery
> i: exoplanet artist impression | v: exoplanet
Not everyone agrees. Critics point out that we only have one example of a living planet: our own. It is hard to know which of the Earth's features are truly necessary, and which simply happened here.

> i: exoplanet | v: exoplanet
Life might find other ways. A world without a large moon might still have stable enough seasons. A planet without plate tectonics might regulate its climate differently. Life might thrive in oceans under ice, where none of the surface conditions matter at all.

> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
And the numbers are staggering. Results from the Kepler mission suggest that a significant fraction of Sun-like stars may have Earth-sized planets in their habitable zones. That could mean billions in our galaxy alone. [pause] Even if complex life is rare, rare out of billions is still a lot.

> i: earth | v: earth rotation
There is also a philosophical trap. We exist, so of course we find ourselves on a planet where everything went right. Any intelligent observer, anywhere, would look back at their own history and see a long chain of lucky breaks. [pause] That does not necessarily mean the chain is rare.

## What We Will Find
mood: wonder
> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
Within our lifetimes, we may begin to get answers. Telescopes like Webb are already studying the atmospheres of rocky planets around other stars. Future observatories are being designed to directly photograph Earth-like worlds and look for the chemical signs of life.

> i: exoplanet atmosphere | v: exoplanet atmosphere
If we find oxygen, methane and water vapour together on a planet around another star, it would be a powerful hint that something is alive there. If we look at hundreds of such worlds and find nothing, the Rare Earth idea will grow stronger.

> i: pale blue dot | v: earth from space
Either way, this question changes how we see our home. If the Earth is common, we are part of a living galaxy. If it is rare, then every forest, every ocean and every person is part of something precious beyond measure. [pause] One small blue world, where, after billions of years of chance, the universe finally learned to look at itself.
