## The Speed Limit
mood: wonder
> i: albert einstein | v: spacetime curvature
In 1905, Albert Einstein proposed something radical. The speed of light in empty space is the same for everyone, no matter how fast they are moving. Chase a beam of light at half its speed, and it still races away from you at the full speed of light.

> i: clock | v: time lapse
To make that work, space and time themselves have to change depending on how you move. Moving clocks tick slower. Moving objects shrink along their direction of motion. And the faster you go, the harder it becomes to go faster.

> i: apollo 10 | v: rocket launch
Humans have barely scratched this limit. The fastest any people have ever travelled was the crew of Apollo 10 in 1969, at about forty thousand kilometres per hour on their way back from the Moon. That is around four thousandths of one percent of the speed of light. [pause] The fastest spacecraft ever built, the Parker Solar Probe, has reached about six hundredths of one percent.

> i: particle accelerator | v: particle accelerator
But tiny particles can go much faster. At the Large Hadron Collider near Geneva, protons are accelerated to {{99.999999%|99.999999%}} of the speed of light. At those speeds, all of Einstein's predictions show up clearly in the data, every day.

## The View From The Front
mood: wonder
> i: star field warp | v: star field
Imagine you are on a ship travelling at ninety nine percent of the speed of light. Look out of the front window. Something strange is happening to the stars. They seem to crowd towards the centre of your view.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
This is called {{aberration|ABERRATION}}. Because you are moving so fast, light arriving from the sides appears to come from further ahead, the way rain falling straight down seems to slant towards you when you run through it. The faster you go, the more of the universe seems to pile up in front of you.

> i: star field warp | v: star field
The light from those stars would also be shifted towards the blue, squeezed by your motion, the way the pitch of an approaching siren rises. Stars ahead would look bluer and brighter. Light that was once invisible infrared would shift into colours you can see. Visible light would shift into ultraviolet and beyond. [pause] The view ahead would be a brilliant, crowded patch of light.

> i: deep space | v: deep space flight
And behind you, the opposite. Stars would thin out, redden and dim, until the view backwards became almost completely dark. [pause] Science fiction sometimes shows a rainbow ring of stars around a fast ship, a so-called starbow. Careful calculations show there would be no neat ring. Just a bright, blueshifted crowd ahead, and darkness behind.

## Time Bends
mood: mystery
> i: clock | v: clock
Now look at your watch. It ticks normally. Your heart beats normally. Everything on your ship feels completely ordinary. But compared with clocks back on Earth, your time is passing much more slowly.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
At ninety nine percent of light speed, every year on board equals about seven years on Earth. At ninety nine point nine nine percent, about seventy. Push to ninety nine point nine nine nine nine percent, and one year aboard covers around seven hundred on Earth.

> i: milky way galaxy | v: milky way time-lapse
At that kind of speed, you could cross the entire Milky Way, a hundred thousand light-years, in a little over a hundred and forty years of your own time. Push even closer to light speed, and you could make the trip within a single human lifetime. [pause] But when you arrived, a hundred thousand years would have passed back home. Everyone you knew, every country, every language, would be long gone.

> i: deep space flight | v: deep space flight
From your point of view on the ship, it would not feel like time had slowed. Instead, the distance ahead of you would seem to shrink. The galaxy would be squashed along your direction of travel, like an accordion. [pause] Both descriptions are correct. They are two views of the same strange reality.

## The Dangers
mood: dread
> i: interstellar medium | v: deep space flight
There is a catch. Space is not completely empty. There is about one atom of hydrogen in every few cubic centimetres between the stars. At everyday speeds, it is harmless. At nearly the speed of light, every atom you hit becomes a high-energy particle, slamming into your ship like cosmic radiation.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
And the faint afterglow of the Big Bang, the cosmic microwave background, would be blueshifted ahead of you. At extreme speeds, that gentle microwave glow would turn into a blast of ultraviolet light and even X-rays, pouring onto the front of your ship.

> i: space debris | v: space debris
A single grain of dust, smaller than a grain of sand, would hit with the energy of a large explosion. Any real ship would need enormous shielding at the front. [pause] At nearly the speed of light, the universe itself becomes a weapon.

> i: rocket launch | v: rocket launch
And then there is the fuel. To push just one kilogram to ninety nine point nine nine nine nine percent of the speed of light, you would need around a tenth of all the energy humanity uses in an entire year. A ship carrying people, supplies and shielding would need far, far more. [pause] The laws of physics allow the journey. The energy bill makes it almost impossible.

## What Light Experiences
mood: wonder
> i: star field | v: deep space flight
And what about light itself? Physicists say a photon does not have a point of view in the same way we do. But the mathematics hints at something poetic. At the speed of light, time would not pass at all. The distance between departure and arrival would shrink to nothing.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
A photon of light from the very edge of the observable universe has been travelling for more than thirteen billion years, from our point of view. [pause] In a sense, its whole journey, from its birth to the moment it hits your eye, happens in no time at all.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
So the speed of light is not just a speed limit. It is the boundary where space and time stop behaving the way we expect. [pause] We will probably never travel close to it. But just knowing what we would see tells us that the universe is far stranger, and far more beautiful, than our everyday senses suggest.
