## Mirror Images In The Maths
mood: mystery
> i: spacetime curvature | v: spacetime curvature
In 1916, just months after Einstein published general relativity, the German physicist {{Karl Schwarzschild|KARL SCHWARZSCHILD}} found the first exact solution to its equations. It described the space around a single, non-spinning mass. Hidden inside it was the idea of an event horizon, a boundary nothing can escape. The black hole.

> i: spacetime curvature | v: spacetime curvature
Remarkably, Schwarzschild worked it out while serving as an artillery officer on the Russian front in the First World War. He sent his solution to Einstein in a letter, and Einstein was astonished that anyone had found an exact answer so quickly. [pause] Within months, Schwarzschild was dead from an illness he caught at the front. He never knew what his mathematics would one day describe.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
For decades, physicists studied only half of that solution. In 1960, the mathematician Martin Kruskal found a way to describe the whole thing at once. And it turned out to contain more than anyone had expected.

> i: white hole | v: wormhole
The full solution had four regions. Our universe. The inside of a black hole. And two more. A second, separate universe on the other side. And a region that behaves exactly like a black hole running backwards in time. [pause] A {{white hole|WHITE HOLE}}.

> i: white hole | v: white hole
In this picture, a black hole and a white hole are two halves of the same object. Things fall into the black hole in the future. Things fly out of the white hole in the past. [pause] It is perfectly valid mathematics. Whether it describes anything real is another question entirely.

## Why They Seem Impossible
mood: dread
> i: star collapse | v: star collapse
Black holes have a natural birth. When a giant star runs out of fuel, its core collapses, and if it is heavy enough, nothing can stop it from becoming a black hole. We understand that process. We have watched its aftermath.

> i: white hole | v: white hole
White holes have no such origin. There is no known process that would create one. In the mathematics, a white hole simply exists from the beginning, already spewing out matter. That is not how anything in our universe seems to work.

> i: entropy | v: time lapse
They also seem to break one of the deepest laws in physics, the second law of thermodynamics. Disorder in the universe always tends to increase. Eggs break; they do not unbreak. A white hole would be like watching smoke pour back into a chimney and reassemble into a log. [pause] Running the universe in reverse.

> i: white hole | v: gamma ray burst
And there is a practical problem. Calculations from the 1970s suggested that a white hole would be wildly unstable. The slightest bit of matter falling towards it from outside would pile up at its edge and make it collapse almost instantly. [pause] Possibly into a black hole.

## The Bounce
mood: mystery
> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
So why do some physicists still take white holes seriously? Because of a question no one can yet answer. What happens at the centre of a black hole?

> i: singularity | v: black hole
Einstein's equations say that everything falling in is crushed into a point of infinite density, a {{singularity|SINGULARITY}}. But most physicists think that is a sign the theory has broken down, not a real description. At that scale, quantum physics must take over. And we do not yet have a theory that combines the two.

> i: white hole | v: white hole
In 2014, physicists Carlo Rovelli and Hal Haggard proposed a striking idea based on one attempt at that theory. Perhaps a collapsing star never reaches infinite density. Perhaps, at some incredibly tiny size, it bounces. [pause] And a black hole slowly turns into a white hole, releasing everything it once swallowed.

> i: time dilation | v: black hole
The strange part is time. Seen from inside, the bounce might happen almost instantly. Seen from outside, thanks to the extreme stretching of time near a black hole, it could take billions of years, or far longer. [pause] Every black hole in the universe might be a white hole in slow motion, waiting to explode.

## Could We See One?
mood: mystery
> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
If black holes really do turn into white holes, some of the smallest and oldest ones, formed in the early universe, might be ending right now. Researchers have wondered whether this could produce brief, violent flashes of radiation that our telescopes might catch.

> i: gamma ray burst artist impression | v: gamma ray burst
In 2006, astronomers saw a long gamma-ray burst, called {{GRB 060614|GRB 060614}}, that behaved strangely. Bursts that long usually come with a supernova. This one did not seem to. A few researchers suggested it might have been a white hole. Most think it had a more ordinary cause. [pause] But it showed that people are looking.

> i: dark matter | v: dark matter
Others have proposed an even more surprising idea. Tiny white holes, left over after microscopic black holes from the early universe finished their bounce, might be scattered everywhere, too small to see, but heavy enough to feel. [pause] Some have even suggested they could make up part of the mysterious dark matter. That idea is highly speculative.

## Was The Big Bang One?
mood: wonder
> i: big bang | v: big bang
And then there is the biggest white hole idea of all. Think about what a white hole does. It is a region that suddenly pours out matter and energy, from a past that nothing outside can reach.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
Now think about the Big Bang. A moment when all the matter and energy in our universe appeared to pour out from a single, extraordinarily dense state. Some physicists have pointed out the resemblance. Could the beginning of our universe have been some kind of white hole?

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
Some even take it a step further, speculating that our universe might have been born from the inside of a black hole in another, larger universe. There is no evidence for this. It sits at the very edge of what science can test. [pause] But it is a thrilling thought. Every black hole might be a seed. Every beginning, an ending somewhere else.

> i: white hole | v: deep space flight
So, are white holes real? The honest answer is that we do not know. They exist in our best equations. They break some of our deepest rules. And they may be hiding in the one place we cannot see: at the end of every black hole's life. [pause] If one ever appears, it will be the brightest message the universe has ever sent, from a door that only opens outwards.
