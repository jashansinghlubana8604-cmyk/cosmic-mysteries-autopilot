For billions of years, our solar system was a closed room. Everything we had ever seen, every planet, every moon, every comet, was born here, around the same Sun, out of the same cloud of gas and dust.

> i: interstellar comet borisov | v: comet
Then, in just eight years, three strangers walked in. Three objects falling through our skies on paths that could only mean one thing. They had come from other stars. [pause] They were never ours.

> i: comet hubble | v: comet tail
Each one was completely different from the last. One was so strange that serious scientists argued about whether it could be alien technology. One was an almost perfect comet with the wrong ingredients. And the third may be older than the Earth itself.

> i: 3i atlas | v: comet
These are the only pieces of other solar systems we have ever been able to touch with our telescopes. [pause] And they are telling us that the space between the stars is not empty. It is full of travellers.

## How You Spot A Stranger
mood: mystery
> i: solar system orbits | v: solar system orbits
Everything that belongs to our solar system moves in a loop. Planets go round in near circles. Comets swing out to the far dark and fall back again, sometimes after thousands of years, but they always come back. They are bound to the Sun.

> i: interstellar object trajectory | v: comet orbit
An object from another star does not loop. It falls in on a path that is open at both ends, a curve called a {{hyperbola|HYPERBOLA}}. It is moving too fast for the Sun to catch it. It swings around once, and then it leaves, and it never returns.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
For decades, astronomers expected that such visitors must exist. When planets form, they fling huge amounts of icy debris out into space. Trillions of comets must have been thrown out of every young solar system in the galaxy. Some of them, surely, would wander through ours.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
But none had ever been seen. Our telescopes were not watching enough of the sky, often enough, to catch something small, dark and moving fast. [pause] Until October 2017.

## The First: A Shape That Made No Sense
mood: mystery
> i: oumuamua | v: oumuamua
A telescope in Hawaii called Pan-STARRS spotted a faint dot racing past the Earth. It was already on its way out. Astronomers named it {{'Oumuamua|'OUMUAMUA}}, a Hawaiian word for a messenger from afar arriving first.

> i: oumuamua artist impression | v: oumuamua
Its brightness changed wildly as it tumbled, by a factor of about ten. That meant its shape was extreme. Either a long thin cigar, or a flat pancake. Nothing like any comet or asteroid we had ever studied.

> i: oumuamua | v: comet
It showed no tail and no gas. It looked like a rock. And yet, as it left, it was being pushed slightly away from the Sun, as if something was jetting off its surface. [pause] A rock that behaved like a comet. A comet that looked like a rock.

> i: oumuamua artist impression | v: star field
Some suggested it was a fragment of frozen hydrogen, or of nitrogen ice chipped off a world like Pluto. One Harvard astronomer even proposed it might be a thin artificial sail. Most scientists think the likeliest answer is natural, perhaps hydrogen gas trapped in its ice and escaping as it warmed. [pause] But it was gone before we could find out. We will never see it again.

## The Second: An Amateur's Comet
mood: wonder
> i: interstellar comet borisov | v: comet
Two years later, in August 2019, an amateur astronomer named {{Gennady Borisov|GENNADY BORISOV}} was scanning the sky from Crimea with a telescope he had built himself. He found a fuzzy object near the Sun. A comet. And its path was wide open.

> i: 2i borisov hubble | v: comet tail
This time, there was no doubt about what it was. {{2I/Borisov|2I/BORISOV}} had a glowing head and a long tail, just like the comets of our own solar system. It was, at first glance, almost reassuringly normal.

> i: comet hubble | v: comet
But when telescopes analysed its gas, they found far more carbon monoxide than in almost any comet born around our Sun. Carbon monoxide only freezes in the very coldest places. Borisov must have formed in the outer, frozen edge of a planetary system, perhaps around a small, cool red star.

> i: interstellar comet borisov | v: comet
Then, as it passed the Sun, Hubble watched a chunk break away from it. It was falling apart in front of us, giving us a glimpse of its insides. [pause] For the first time, we were reading the chemistry of another solar system.

## The Third: Older Than The Sun
mood: mystery
> i: 3i atlas | v: comet
On the first of July 2025, a survey telescope in Chile, part of a network built to spot dangerous asteroids, picked up a third visitor. It was named {{3I/ATLAS|3I/ATLAS}}. And it was moving faster than either of the others, at around sixty kilometres per second.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
Its speed and direction gave astronomers a clue to where it came from. It seemed to be arriving from the older, thicker part of our galaxy's disc. If that is right, 3I/ATLAS could be billions of years older than our solar system. [pause] It may have been wandering between the stars since before the Earth existed.

> i: comet tail | v: comet
As it warmed, it came alive. Telescopes, including Webb, found its gas unusually rich in carbon dioxide compared with water. Others detected nickel vapour streaming from it, a metal also seen around Borisov. It brightened quickly, and its behaviour kept surprising the teams following it.

> i: mars | v: mars orbit
In October 2025, it passed within about thirty million kilometres of Mars. Spacecraft orbiting the red planet turned their cameras away from the ground to look at it. Probes in deep space watched it round the Sun from angles no telescope on Earth could reach. [pause] It was the most closely watched interstellar object in history.

> i: 3i atlas hubble | v: comet
Like 'Oumuamua before it, 3I/ATLAS attracted wild claims that it might be artificial. The evidence pointed the other way: it looked and behaved like a comet, just a very old and unusual one. [pause] And then, like the others, it began its long journey out of our solar system, never to come back.

## The Next One
mood: wonder
> i: vera rubin observatory | v: telescope dome
So why did we see three in eight years, after centuries of seeing none? Partly because our telescopes got better. And a new one is about to change everything. The {{Vera Rubin Observatory|VERA RUBIN OBSERVATORY}} in Chile photographs the entire southern sky every few nights, deeper than any survey before it.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
Astronomers expect it to find interstellar visitors regularly, perhaps several every few years. Some will be big, some small, some fresh comets, some ancient rocks. Each one a sample from a different star.

> i: comet interceptor | v: spacecraft
The European Space Agency is preparing a spacecraft called Comet Interceptor, designed to wait in space for the right target and then race out to meet it. If an interstellar object comes along at the right time, we might finally see one up close. [pause] We might even fly through its tail.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Think about what these visitors really are. Pieces of planets and moons and frozen worlds we will never see, around stars whose light we cannot even separate from the crowd. They have crossed light-years of darkness for thousands, maybe billions of years. [pause] And for a few months, they pass through our sky and let us read their story. Then they are gone. Back to the dark between the suns.
