## Number Seven: A Perfect Fit
mood: wonder
> i: solar eclipse corona | v: solar eclipse
At number seven is something you can see from your own planet: a total solar eclipse. The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon. But it is also about four hundred times farther away. [pause] So in our sky, they appear almost exactly the same size.

> i: solar eclipse | v: total solar eclipse
When the Moon passes in front of the Sun, it covers the bright disc just perfectly, leaving the Sun's glowing outer atmosphere, the {{corona|CORONA}}, shining around a black hole in the sky. Of all the planets and moons we know, no other place offers such an exact match.

> i: moon | v: moon orbit
And it is temporary. The Moon is drifting away from the Earth by about four centimetres every year. In the distant future, perhaps several hundred million years from now, it will look too small to cover the Sun completely. [pause] We live in the era of perfect eclipses.

> i: solar eclipse | v: total solar eclipse
Even now, totality is a rare sight for any one place. The Moon's dark shadow is only a narrow strip on the Earth's surface, usually less than two hundred kilometres wide. On average, a given spot on Earth sees a total eclipse only about once every three to four hundred years. [pause] Many people travel across the world, just to stand inside that shadow for a few minutes.

## Six And Five: Strange Stars
mood: mystery
> i: supernova explosion | v: supernova explosion
At number six is a kind of explosion so rare it only got a name in 2018. That June, a survey telescope spotted a new light in a galaxy two hundred million light-years away. It was given a random code name, {{AT2018cow|AT2018COW}}, and became known simply as the Cow.

> i: supernova | v: star explosion
The Cow brightened far faster than any normal supernova, reaching its peak in just a few days and shining many times brighter. It glowed blue-hot and faded almost as quickly. [pause] Only a handful of similar events have been found since, with nicknames like the Koala and the Tasmanian Devil.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
Astronomers call them fast blue optical transients. They might be a star being torn apart by a black hole, or a dying star giving birth to a black hole or neutron star inside it. [pause] Nobody is yet sure.

> i: star | v: star twinkling
At number five is {{Przybylski's Star|PRZYBYLSKI'S STAR}}, around three hundred and fifty light-years away. In 1961, the astronomer Antoni Przybylski examined its light and found almost no iron. Instead, it was full of rare elements like holmium, elements that are scarce almost everywhere else.

> i: star spectrum | v: star
Some researchers have even claimed traces of elements so unstable they should have decayed away long ago. Those claims are disputed. [pause] But the star's chemistry is unlike almost any other ever measured, and its strange surface remains one of the great puzzles of stellar astronomy.

## Number Four: Two Lighthouses
mood: epic
> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
At number four is a pair of dead stars. A {{pulsar|PULSAR}} is a spinning neutron star that sweeps beams of radio waves across space like a lighthouse. Thousands are known. Some orbit other stars, and some orbit other neutron stars.

> i: double pulsar | v: neutron star binary
But in 2003, astronomers using the Parkes radio telescope in Australia found a system where both neutron stars are pulsars, and both beams sweep past the Earth. [pause] It is still the only known {{double pulsar|DOUBLE PULSAR}}.

> i: neutron star binary | v: neutron star binary
The two stars whirl around each other every two and a half hours. Each is heavier than the Sun, squeezed into a ball about the size of a city. Their orbit is slowly shrinking as they lose energy in gravitational waves, exactly as Einstein's theory predicts. [pause] In about eighty five million years, they will collide.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope
Timing the two cosmic clocks has given one of the most precise tests of general relativity ever made. One of the beams even disappeared from view in 2008, because the pulsar's spin axis is slowly wobbling. [pause] This one system is a laboratory nature built only once, as far as we know.

## Three And Two: Explosions Seen Twice
mood: mystery
> i: einstein cross | v: gravitational lensing
At number three is a supernova that we have seen five times. In 2014, Hubble photographed a galaxy cluster and spotted four images of the same exploding star, arranged in a cross around a foreground galaxy.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
The cluster's gravity was bending the light like a giant lens, sending it along different paths of different lengths. Astronomers predicted another path would bring a fifth image into view roughly a year later. In December 2015, it appeared, right on schedule. [pause] The supernova was named {{Refsdal|REFSDAL}}, after the astronomer who first imagined such an event in 1964.

> i: milky way | v: milky way
At number two is something we have not seen for more than four hundred years: a supernova inside our own galaxy. Astronomers think one should happen every few decades. [pause] The last one anyone recorded was in {{1604|1604}}, before the telescope was invented.

> i: supernova remnant | v: supernova remnant
Others have happened since, hidden behind clouds of dust. We have found the glowing remains of an explosion from around the year nineteen hundred that nobody saw. [pause] When the next one is visible, it may be bright enough to see in daylight, and every telescope on Earth will turn towards it.

> i: supernova 1987a | v: supernova explosion
The closest we have come in modern times was in 1987, when a star exploded in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy orbiting our own. It was visible to the naked eye from the southern hemisphere. Detectors deep underground caught about two dozen ghostly particles called neutrinos from the explosion, hours before its light arrived. [pause] A galactic supernova would send thousands.

## Number One: The Rarest Of All
mood: epic
> i: exoplanet | v: exoplanet
At number one is the rarest thing we have ever found. More than six thousand planets have now been confirmed around other stars. Telescopes have searched their atmospheres for signs of biology. Radio dishes have listened for signals for decades.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
And so far, in the entire universe, we know of exactly {{one|ONE}} place with life. [pause] One planet with oceans on its surface, a breathable atmosphere and living things.

> i: exoplanet atmosphere | v: james webb telescope
Astronomers have reported possible hints on distant worlds, such as gases in the atmosphere of the planet K2-18b that, on Earth, are made by living things. But those findings are uncertain and strongly debated. [pause] Not one has been confirmed.

> i: earth | v: earth rotating
That does not mean life is truly rare. It may be common, and we simply have not found it yet. But until we do, Earth holds a record no other place can match.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from moon
Out of two trillion galaxies, one planet, orbiting one ordinary star, has produced beings that can look out at the rest and wonder what else is there. [pause] Until we find another, the rarest thing in the known universe is the world beneath your feet, and you.
