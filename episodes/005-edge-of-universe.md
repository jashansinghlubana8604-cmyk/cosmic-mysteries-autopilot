## Leaving Home
mood: wonder
> v: earth rotation from space | i: earth moon
We begin at home, on a small rocky planet travelling around an ordinary star. The light reflected from the Moon reaches us in just over one second. That will be our measuring stick: the speed of light, three hundred thousand kilometres every second.

> v: sun | i: sun solar flare
The Sun is about eight light minutes away. When you look at it, you are seeing it as it was eight minutes ago. If it vanished, we would not know for eight minutes.

> v: jupiter | i: jupiter
Jupiter is around forty light minutes away, Neptune about four light hours. Beyond them is the Kuiper Belt, a ring of icy worlds that includes Pluto.

> v: voyager spacecraft | i: voyager deep space
Further still is Voyager 1, the most distant object humanity has ever built, launched in 1977. After nearly half a century of travel, it is now almost exactly {{one light-day|1 LIGHT-DAY}} from home. In cosmic terms, it has barely left the front door.

> i: oort cloud | v: oort cloud
Around the whole solar system, far beyond the planets, is thought to be a vast spherical shell of icy bodies, the Oort Cloud, stretching perhaps a light year or more from the Sun. That is roughly where the Sun's gravity finally gives way to the rest of the galaxy.

## Among The Stars
mood: wonder
> v: star field slow | i: star field
The nearest star is Proxima Centauri, a small red dwarf {{4.2 light years|4.2 LY}} away. If you shrank the Sun to the size of a grain of sand, Proxima would be another grain of sand around thirty kilometres away. That is how empty space is.

> i: proxima centauri | v: proxima centauri planet
Proxima has at least one planet about the size of Earth. Our fastest spacecraft would need several thousand years to reach it.

> v: milky way galaxy | i: milky way panorama
Keep going, past thousands of stars, past glowing clouds of gas where new stars are being born, and the Milky Way begins to reveal its shape. A flat spiral of between one and four hundred billion stars, about a hundred thousand light years across.

> i: milky way artist impression | v: milky way galaxy rotation
Our Sun sits about twenty six thousand light years from the centre, in a quiet outer region of one spiral arm. It takes the Sun around two hundred and thirty million years to complete one orbit around the galaxy. The last time it was here, the first dinosaurs were just appearing on Earth.

## Islands In The Dark
mood: wonder
> v: andromeda galaxy | i: andromeda galaxy
Leave the Milky Way, and the darkness becomes deeper. The nearest large galaxy is Andromeda, {{two and a half million light years|2,500,000 LY}} away. Its light, reaching your eyes tonight, began its journey before humans existed.

> i: local group galaxies | v: galaxy group
The Milky Way and Andromeda are the two largest members of a small family of galaxies called the Local Group, along with dozens of smaller dwarf galaxies. In several billion years, Andromeda and the Milky Way may merge into one giant galaxy.

> v: virgo cluster | i: virgo cluster
Further out, about fifty million light years away, is the Virgo Cluster, a swarm of more than a thousand galaxies. Our Local Group is being pulled toward it.

> i: laniakea supercluster | v: galaxy flow simulation
And all of this is part of something even larger, a supercluster called {{Laniakea|LANIAKEA}}, a Hawaiian word meaning immeasurable heaven. It stretches about five hundred million light years and contains around a hundred thousand galaxies. The Milky Way is a single speck on its outer edge.

## The Cosmic Web
mood: wonder
> v: cosmic web simulation | i: cosmic web
Now step back far enough, billions of light years, and something remarkable appears. Galaxies are not scattered randomly. They form long filaments and walls, wrapped around enormous empty voids, like the threads of a vast web, or the foam of a sea.

> i: dark matter simulation | v: dark matter simulation
This is the {{cosmic web|THE COSMIC WEB}}, the largest structure in the universe. Its shape was set by invisible dark matter, which pulled ordinary matter into these threads over billions of years.

> v: zoom deep field | i: hubble deep field
As we travel further, we are also travelling back in time. The light from galaxies ten billion light years away left them ten billion years ago. We see them young, crowded and blue, full of newborn stars, often colliding with one another.

> i: webb deep field | v: zoom webb deep field
The James Webb Space Telescope has now seen galaxies whose light left them more than thirteen and a half billion years ago, just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. They are the first lights in the cosmic dark.

## The Last Light
mood: wonder
> v: early universe | i: early universe
Beyond the first galaxies, there are no more stars to see. But there is still light. The oldest light in the universe, released when the cosmos was just three hundred and eighty thousand years old, and hot enough to glow.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Today that light has been stretched by the expansion of space into faint microwaves, arriving from every direction in the sky. It is the {{cosmic microwave background|THE OLDEST LIGHT}}, and it marks the edge of what we can ever see.

> v: zoom out universe | i: universe
Because space has been expanding all this time, the places where that light came from are now much further away than its travel time suggests. The edge of the observable universe today lies about {{forty six billion light years|46,000,000,000 LY}} away in every direction, a sphere about ninety three billion light years across.

> i: galaxies | v: galaxies moving apart
Inside that sphere are hundreds of billions of galaxies, perhaps more. And every one of them is moving away from every other, as the space between them grows.

## Beyond The Edge
mood: mystery
> v: star field slow | i: dark space stars
So what lies beyond the edge? The honest answer is that we do not know, and in a sense, we can never know. The observable universe is not a wall. It is a horizon. It is simply as far as light has had time to travel to us since the beginning.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web simulation
Most cosmologists believe the universe continues far beyond it, with more galaxies, more stars, more of the cosmic web, perhaps forever. Measurements of the geometry of space suggest it is extremely flat, which means the whole universe could be vastly larger than the part we can see, or even infinite.

> v: zoom out galaxy | i: deep field galaxies
And because the expansion of the universe is accelerating, most of the galaxies we can see today will eventually drift beyond our horizon. Billions of years from now, astronomers in our galaxy may look up and see only their own corner of the cosmos, with no trace that the rest ever existed.

> i: earth night | v: earth from space
We live in a brief window of cosmic history when we can see almost all the way back to the beginning. [pause] And from one small planet, around one ordinary star, a species only a few hundred thousand years old has managed to map the whole of it.
