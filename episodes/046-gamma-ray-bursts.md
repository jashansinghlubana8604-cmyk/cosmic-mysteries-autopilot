## Found By Spy Satellites
mood: mystery
> i: vela satellite | v: satellite orbit
In 1963, the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain signed a treaty banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere and in space. But how could anyone check that the other side was keeping its promise? The Americans launched a series of satellites called {{Vela|VELA}}, designed to watch for the flash of gamma rays that a nuclear explosion would produce.

> i: vela satellite | v: satellites orbit
On the second of July 1967, two of them recorded a flash of gamma rays. But it did not look like any nuclear weapon. It had two peaks, and it lasted far too long. More flashes followed, over the years. Scientists at Los Alamos slowly worked out the directions they came from.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
They did not come from the Earth. Or the Moon. Or anywhere in the solar system. They were coming from deep space. In 1973, the discovery was declassified and published. [pause] Spy satellites, watching for bombs, had found the biggest explosions in the universe.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
For more than twenty years, nobody knew what they were. Each burst came from a random direction, lasted a few seconds, and vanished without a trace. Some scientists thought they came from inside our galaxy. Others thought they came from the far edge of the universe. [pause] If it was the far edge, the energy involved would be almost impossible to believe.

## Two Kinds Of Death
mood: dread
> i: bepposax | v: satellite
In 1997, an Italian and Dutch satellite called BeppoSAX caught the fading X-ray glow left behind by a burst, and pinned down its exact position. Telescopes on the ground followed up, and found a faint galaxy at that spot, billions of light-years away. [pause] The far edge had won.

> i: hypernova | v: star collapse
We now know there are two main kinds of gamma-ray bursts. Long ones, lasting more than a couple of seconds, happen when a very massive, rapidly spinning star collapses at the end of its life. Its core falls in to form a black hole, and jets of matter blast out of the poles at almost the speed of light.

> i: neutron star merger | v: neutron star merger
Short ones, lasting less than two seconds, happen when two neutron stars, the city-sized remnants of dead stars, spiral into each other and merge. In 2017, astronomers caught one of these at the same moment as the gravitational waves from the collision. [pause] Light and ripples in space itself, arriving together.

> i: gamma ray burst artist impression | v: black hole jet
In both cases, the energy is focused into two narrow beams. If one of those beams happens to point at the Earth, we see a gamma-ray burst. In just a few seconds, a burst can release more energy than our Sun will give off in its {{entire ten billion year life|10 BILLION YEARS}}.

## The Burst You Could See
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky
Gamma rays are invisible to our eyes. But some bursts also produce a brief flash of visible light. And on the nineteenth of March 2008, one of them was bright enough to see without a telescope.

> i: bootes constellation | v: night sky stars
{{GRB 080319B|GRB 080319B}} came from a galaxy about seven and a half billion light-years away. For roughly thirty seconds, it shone faintly in the constellation Boötes, at the very limit of what the human eye can see from a dark site.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
Think about that. The light had been travelling since before the Earth existed. Before the Sun was born. And for half a minute, anyone looking at the right patch of a dark sky could have seen it with their own eyes. [pause] Billions of light-years, crossed, and still visible. It was the most distant object ever seen with the naked eye.

## The BOAT
mood: dread
> i: swift telescope | v: swift satellite
Which brings us back to October 2022. The burst was detected by NASA's Fermi and Swift satellites, and named {{GRB 221009A|GRB 221009A}}. Its light was so intense that it saturated the instruments built to measure it.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
It came from a galaxy in the constellation Sagitta, about two billion light-years away. Relatively close, for a gamma-ray burst. That, and the fact that its jet was pointed almost straight at us, made it by far the brightest ever recorded.

> i: earth atmosphere | v: earth from space
Its radiation reached the Earth's ionosphere, the electrically charged upper layer of our atmosphere, and disturbed it for a few minutes, on the side of the planet that was facing it. A star dying two billion light-years away briefly changed the sky above our heads.

> i: lhaaso observatory | v: observatory
A detector on a Tibetan mountainside called LHAASO recorded particles of light from the burst carrying trillions of times more energy than visible light. Some theories said light that energetic should not survive the trip across the universe. [pause] The BOAT kept breaking the rules.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Astronomers expected to see a supernova as the glow faded, the remains of the exploding star. Webb found it, but it looked surprisingly ordinary. [pause] Why such a normal-looking explosion produced such an extraordinary burst is still being worked out.

## Could One Hit Us?
mood: dread
> i: gamma ray burst artist impression | v: black hole jet
So what if a gamma-ray burst happened in our own galaxy, with its beam aimed at the Earth? Scientists have studied this question carefully. A burst within a few thousand light-years could strip away a large part of the ozone layer, which shields us from the Sun's ultraviolet light.

> i: earth from space | v: earth
Some researchers have even suggested that a burst might have helped trigger a mass extinction about four hundred and fifty million years ago, when most life lived in the oceans. That idea is possible, but unproven. [pause] There is no direct evidence of such a burst in the rock record.

> i: wolf rayet star | v: star field
The good news is that these explosions are rare in galaxies like ours, and they need very particular stars, heavy, fast-spinning and low in heavy elements. There is no known star nearby that is clearly pointed our way and ready to go. [pause] The odds of being hit in our lifetimes are tiny.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: deep space flight
But somewhere in the universe, every day, a star dies this way. Its core collapses, a black hole is born, and two beams of light brighter than a billion billion Suns punch out into space. [pause] Most of them never reach us. The BOAT did. And for a few minutes in 2022, the whole planet was standing in its light.
