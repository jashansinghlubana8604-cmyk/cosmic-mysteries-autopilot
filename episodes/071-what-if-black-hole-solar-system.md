> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
Somewhere in our galaxy, there are probably around a hundred million black holes. The remains of giant stars that collapsed long ago. Most of them are wandering alone through the dark, swallowing almost nothing, giving off almost no light.

> i: rogue black hole | v: black hole
They are invisible. You could point the best telescope on Earth straight at one and see nothing at all. Just black, against black.

> i: solar system | v: solar system
So imagine one of them is heading our way. A black hole about ten times the mass of the Sun, drifting into our solar system at fifty kilometres per second. [pause] How would we know? What would it do to the planets? And what would happen to us?

> i: gravitational lensing black hole | v: black hole
This is not a prediction. The odds of it happening are extremely small. But following it step by step shows just how much power hides in an object smaller than a city. [pause] Let's watch it arrive.

[title]

## The First Warning
mood: mystery
> i: oort cloud | v: oort cloud
The first sign would come thousands of years before it arrived. Far beyond Pluto, the Sun is surrounded by a vast, faint shell of icy bodies called the {{Oort Cloud|OORT CLOUD}}, stretching perhaps a light-year or more into space. Trillions of frozen comets, circling slowly.

> i: comet | v: comet
As the black hole approached, its gravity would tug on those comets, knocking many of them out of their ancient orbits. Some would be flung out into interstellar space. Others would begin the long fall towards the Sun.

> i: comet tail | v: comet
Astronomers would notice, over decades, that more and more comets were arriving, all seeming to come from the same general direction of the sky. [pause] A pattern that only something massive and unseen could explain.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: gravitational lensing
And then, the black hole itself would betray its position, not by shining, but by bending light. As it passed in front of distant stars, its gravity would magnify and distort them, a flicker astronomers call microlensing. That is exactly how, in 2022, astronomers confirmed the first isolated black hole wandering through our galaxy. [pause] We would see the stars behind it warp. And we would know.

## Getting Closer
mood: dread
> i: neptune | v: neptune rotation
At fifty kilometres per second, a black hole would take thousands of years to cross the Oort Cloud. But once it reached the outer planets, things would speed up. In just a few years, it would cross the orbits of the giants.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
Up close, it would look like a perfectly dark sphere, about sixty kilometres across. Smaller than many cities. But around it, the stars would be smeared into rings and arcs, a {{lens of gravity|A LENS OF GRAVITY}}, as light from behind bent around it.

> i: saturn | v: saturn rings
Its pull would be enormous. Ten times the mass of the Sun. Any planet it passed near would have its orbit ruined. Neptune or Uranus might be flung out of the solar system entirely. Saturn might be dragged into a wild, stretched path. [pause] Planets that had been moving in steady circles for four and a half billion years would scatter like marbles.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotation
And a planet that came too close would be captured. Pulled into orbit around the black hole, or worse, torn apart by its tides and drawn into a glowing disc of debris. For a few days, that disc would blaze in X-rays as material spiralled in. [pause] The black hole would finally light up, feeding on a world.

## The Earth's Orbit
mood: dread
> i: earth orbit | v: earth orbit
Now for the part that matters most. The black hole would not have to come anywhere near the Earth to destroy life on it. It would only have to change our orbit.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
The Earth stays habitable because it orbits the Sun in a nearly circular path, at just the right distance. If a passing black hole stretched that orbit, we might swing much closer to the Sun for part of the year and far further away for the rest. Scorching summers. Freezing winters. [pause] A change of just a few percent in our distance could be devastating.

> i: frozen earth | v: frozen earth
If it pulled us further out, the oceans would freeze. If it pulled us closer, they would boil. And if the black hole passed close enough, it might fling the Earth out of the solar system altogether, sending it into the dark as a frozen {{rogue planet|ROGUE PLANET}}.

> i: sun | v: sun
The Sun itself would be at risk too. If the black hole passed within a few million kilometres, its tides would stretch the Sun, ripping away its outer layers and pulling them into a swirling, brilliant disc. [pause] The death of our star, seen from the Earth, as a spiral of fire around an invisible point.

## What Would We See?
mood: dread
> i: night sky | v: night sky
For people on Earth, the strangest thing would be how little there was to see. No glowing monster in the sky. Just stars that moved in impossible ways, smearing into arcs around a patch of darkness.

> i: gravitational lensing black hole | v: gravitational lensing
Near the black hole, there would be a ring of light, made of starlight bent all the way around it, called an {{Einstein ring|EINSTEIN RING}}. Inside it, a small circle of perfect black. A hole cut out of the sky.

> i: earth tides | v: ocean waves
Our tides would change too. The oceans would feel a second pull, rising and falling in new rhythms as the black hole passed. Even the Moon's orbit might be disturbed. [pause] And then, after a few years of chaos, it would be gone. Moving away at fifty kilometres per second, back into the dark, leaving a wrecked solar system behind it.

## How Worried Should We Be?
mood: mystery
> i: gaia bh1 | v: star field
Here is the reassuring truth. The nearest known black hole, Gaia BH1, is about one and a half thousand light-years away. Space is vast, and black holes are spread incredibly thinly through it. The chance of one passing through our solar system in the lifetime of the Sun is extremely small.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Stars pass near the solar system far more often than black holes, every few hundred thousand years or so, a light-year or more away. One of them, a small star called Gliese 710, is expected to pass through the outer Oort Cloud in about one point three million years, shaking loose a shower of comets. [pause] That is the kind of visitor we actually expect.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
But the thought experiment reveals something profound. A black hole does not need to swallow you to destroy you. It only needs to pass by. Its power is not in its darkness. It is in its gravity.

> i: rogue black hole | v: black hole
And they are out there. The lone black hole found in 2022, through microlensing, is about five thousand light-years away, drifting through the galaxy at around forty five kilometres per second. It weighs about seven times as much as the Sun, and nobody would ever have known it existed if it had not happened to pass in front of a distant star. [pause] How many more are wandering unseen?

> i: solar system | v: solar system
Our solar system has been calm for billions of years. Planets in near-circular orbits. A stable star. A blue world exactly where it needs to be. [pause] And all of it depends on one simple thing. Nothing very large has come too close. So far.
