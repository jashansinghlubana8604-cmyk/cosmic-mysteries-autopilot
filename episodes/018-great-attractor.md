## A Universe In Motion
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The first clue came from the oldest light in the universe. The cosmic microwave background is the faint glow left over from the Big Bang, and it fills the entire sky, almost perfectly evenly.

> v: planck satellite | i: cmb map
Almost. When astronomers measured it carefully, they found that one side of the sky was very slightly warmer than the other. The difference was tiny, around one part in a thousand. But it was there, and it had a simple explanation.

> v: doppler effect | i: redshift
We are moving. Light from the direction we are heading in gets slightly compressed, and looks hotter. Light from behind us gets stretched, and looks cooler. It is the same effect that makes a passing siren drop in pitch. From that warm side of the sky, astronomers could calculate our speed, and our direction.

> v: local group animation | i: andromeda galaxy
Once you remove the motion of the Earth, and of the Sun around the galaxy, what remains is the motion of our whole Local Group of galaxies. About {{600 kilometres per second|600 KM/S}}, toward a region of sky in the southern constellations of Hydra and Centaurus.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster zoom
For a whole group of galaxies to move that fast, something enormous must be tugging on it. In the 1980s, a team of seven astronomers, nicknamed the Seven Samurai, set out to map how hundreds of galaxies were moving. They found that we are not alone. Galaxies across a huge region of space are streaming in the same direction, all flowing toward a single point.

> v: galaxy flow simulation | i: galaxy distribution
Measuring this was painstaking work. For every galaxy, they needed two numbers. How fast it appears to move away from us, which comes from its redshift, and how far away it really is, which is much harder to measure. The difference between the two reveals each galaxy's own motion through space, on top of the expansion of the universe. Galaxy by galaxy, those small leftover motions added up to a river.

> v: dark matter simulation | i: dark matter map
In 1987, one of them, Alan Dressler, gave that point a name. [pause] {{The Great Attractor|THE GREAT ATTRACTOR}}.

## The Hidden Part Of The Sky
mood: mystery
> v: milky way panorama | i: milky way panorama dust
There was just one problem. When astronomers pointed their telescopes toward the Great Attractor, they could barely see anything.

> i: dark dust lanes milky way | v: milky way dust
The Great Attractor lies directly behind the disc of our own galaxy. Looking toward it means looking through the thickest part of the Milky Way, through clouds of gas and dust and a dense wall of foreground stars.

> i: milky way plane | v: zoom milky way plane
Astronomers call this region the {{Zone of Avoidance|ZONE OF AVOIDANCE}}. Our own galaxy blocks roughly a fifth of the entire sky. Anything lying behind it is hidden, like a city on the far side of a mountain range in fog.

> v: x-ray telescope | i: x-ray galaxy cluster
And so, for years, the thing pulling on our galaxy was a gravitational force without a face. Astronomers knew how strongly it pulled and roughly where it was. They simply could not see what it was made of.

> v: radio telescope dish | i: radio telescope
The answer came from light that can pass through dust. X-ray telescopes picked out the hot gas inside galaxy clusters hidden behind the Milky Way. Radio telescopes, like the Parkes dish in Australia, listened for the radio glow of hydrogen in distant galaxies. In 2016, one survey alone uncovered {{883 galaxies|883 GALAXIES}} hiding behind our galaxy, a third of them never seen before.

> i: norma cluster | v: galaxy cluster
At the heart of the Great Attractor sits the Norma Cluster, a dense crowd of galaxies about 220 million light years away, part of a vast concentration of matter. Its total mass is estimated at around {{ten thousand trillion Suns|10,000,000,000,000,000 SUNS}}. Much of it is invisible dark matter.

## Our Home Has A Name
mood: wonder
> v: cosmic web simulation | i: cosmic web
In 2014, a team led by Brent Tully mapped the motions of eight thousand galaxies, and drew something no one had drawn before. Instead of grouping galaxies by where they are, they grouped them by where they are going.

> v: galaxy flow simulation | i: laniakea supercluster
Like mapping rivers by the valleys they drain into, they traced the flows of galaxies across space, and found the boundary of the basin we live in. Inside it, everything flows toward the same place. [pause] Toward the Great Attractor.

> i: laniakea | v: zoom out universe
They named this structure {{Laniakea|LANIAKEA}}, a Hawaiian word meaning immeasurable heaven. It is our home supercluster. It is around 500 million light years across and contains about a hundred thousand galaxies. The Milky Way is a small speck on its outer edge.

> i: virgo cluster | v: virgo cluster
Before this, astronomers had thought of our home as the Virgo Supercluster, centred on the giant Virgo Cluster of galaxies. Laniakea swallowed it whole. Virgo turned out to be just one branch, a tributary feeding into a much larger flow.

> v: zoom out milky way | i: local supercluster
And the Great Attractor is not a single object. It is the gravitational valley at the centre of Laniakea, the lowest point that all of these galaxies are sliding toward.

## Pushed And Pulled
mood: mystery
> i: shapley supercluster | v: galaxy cluster zoom
The story did not end there. Even the Great Attractor turned out not to explain all of our motion.

> v: dark matter simulation | i: galaxy supercluster
Far beyond it, about 650 million light years away, lies the Shapley Supercluster, the most massive concentration of galaxies in our part of the universe. It is pulling too, on us, and on the Great Attractor itself.

> v: empty space | i: dark space
And in 2017, astronomers found something even stranger in the opposite direction. A huge region almost empty of galaxies, which they named the {{Dipole Repeller|DIPOLE REPELLER}}.

> i: cosmic void | v: cosmic void simulation
Empty space does not literally push. But because a void has less matter than its surroundings, everything around it is pulled away toward the denser regions. The effect is the same as a push. So we are being pulled by the Great Attractor and Shapley in front of us, and pushed by this emptiness behind us. Together, they explain most of the way we move.

## Will We Ever Arrive?
mood: wonder
> i: dark matter map | v: dark matter
There is still debate about how much each of these regions contributes, and some surveys have found hints of motions on even larger scales, flows that some astronomers suspect extend beyond anything our maps currently show. If those flows are real, the full story of where we are going may be bigger than the Great Attractor, bigger than Shapley, and bigger than Laniakea itself.

> v: expanding universe | i: expanding universe
So are we falling into the Great Attractor? Will the Milky Way one day reach it?

> v: galaxies moving apart | i: dark energy
The answer is no. The universe is expanding, and that expansion is speeding up, driven by the mysterious force we call dark energy. Over billions of years, it will stretch the space between the galaxy clusters of Laniakea faster than gravity can pull them together.

> v: andromeda milky way collision | i: andromeda milky way collision
Our close neighbour Andromeda will still collide with the Milky Way, in about four or five billion years. But the Great Attractor will slowly drift away from us, until, in the far future, it disappears beyond the edge of what can be seen.

> v: zoom deep field | i: deep field
For now, though, it is still there, hidden behind the stars and dust of our own galaxy. A gravitational valley the size of thousands of galaxies, steering everything we know across the cosmos. [pause] We will never see it with our own eyes. But every second of every day, we are falling toward it.
