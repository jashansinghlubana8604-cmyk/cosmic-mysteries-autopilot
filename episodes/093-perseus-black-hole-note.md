## The Space Between Galaxies
mood: mystery
> i: perseus cluster | v: galaxy cluster
The {{Perseus Cluster|PERSEUS CLUSTER}} is one of the most massive objects in the nearby universe. It contains thousands of galaxies, held together by gravity, about two hundred and fifty million light-years from Earth.

> i: galaxy cluster xray | v: galaxy cluster
The space between those galaxies is not empty. It is filled with a vast, thin cloud of gas, heated to tens of millions of degrees. So hot that it glows in X-rays rather than visible light. In fact, there is more mass in this hot gas than in all the stars of all the galaxies in the cluster.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Why is it so hot? Because of gravity. As the cluster formed, gas fell into its enormous gravitational well from all directions, colliding and compressing, heating up as it fell, the way air heats up in a bicycle pump. [pause] Billions of years later, it is still blazing at temperatures several times hotter than the core of the Sun.

> i: ngc 1275 | v: galaxy zoom
At the heart of the cluster sits a giant galaxy called {{NGC 1275|NGC 1275}}. And at the heart of that galaxy is a supermassive black hole, hundreds of millions of times the mass of the Sun.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Because the gas fills the whole cluster, sound can travel through it, just as it travels through air. The gas is incredibly thin, far emptier than any vacuum we can make on Earth. [pause] But over millions of light-years, it is enough.

## Ripples In The Gas
mood: wonder
> i: chandra xray | v: chandra xray
In 2003, a team led by the astronomer Andrew Fabian used NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory to take a long, deep look at the Perseus Cluster. When they processed the image to bring out subtle details, they saw something remarkable.

> i: perseus cluster chandra | v: galaxy cluster
Around the central black hole, there were ripples in the hot gas. Concentric rings, like the circles that spread out when you drop a stone into a pond. Each ring was a region where the gas was slightly denser and hotter, alternating with regions slightly thinner. [pause] Exactly what sound waves look like.

> i: perseus cluster chandra | v: galaxy cluster
From the spacing of the ripples, tens of thousands of light-years apart, and the speed of sound in the hot gas, the team worked out the pitch. Each wave took around ten million years to pass. Translated into music, that corresponds to a B flat, fifty seven octaves below middle C.

> i: piano | v: music
To put that in perspective, a piano's lowest note is only about four octaves below middle C. Each octave halves the frequency. The Perseus note is more than a million billion times deeper than the lowest sound a human ear can detect. [pause] It is the deepest note ever found anywhere in the cosmos.

## How A Black Hole Sings
mood: dread
> i: black hole jet | v: black hole jet
How does a black hole make sound? Not by itself. Black holes swallow everything, including sound. The sound is made by what the black hole does to its surroundings.

> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
As gas falls towards the black hole, some of it is flung back out in two powerful jets, moving at close to the speed of light. These jets blast into the hot gas of the cluster, carving out enormous bubbles, hundreds of thousands of light-years across in some cases.

> i: perseus cluster cavities | v: galaxy cluster
Chandra can see them. Dark cavities in the X-ray glow, where the jets have pushed the gas aside. As each bubble is inflated, it shoves the surrounding gas outward, sending a pressure wave rippling through the cluster. [pause] Every burst of activity from the black hole is like a beat on a gigantic drum.

> i: black hole jet | v: black hole jet
The energy involved is almost impossible to picture. Inflating a single one of these giant bubbles takes as much energy as millions of exploding stars, delivered over millions of years. [pause] All of it powered by matter spiralling into one black hole, which is itself far smaller than the galaxy around it.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
And because the black hole has been doing this repeatedly for billions of years, the cluster is filled with the echoes of its outbursts. The black hole is not singing on purpose. It is shouting into a room the size of millions of galaxies, and the room is still ringing.

## Why It Matters
mood: mystery
> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
This is not just a curiosity. Those sound waves may solve a major puzzle in astronomy. The hot gas in clusters like Perseus should be slowly cooling, radiating its heat away as X-rays. As it cools, it should sink towards the central galaxy and form huge numbers of new stars.

> i: ngc 1275 | v: galaxy zoom
But that does not happen nearly as much as expected. The central galaxies of big clusters form far fewer stars than the cooling gas should produce. Something is keeping the gas hot.

> i: perseus cluster chandra | v: galaxy cluster
The sound waves, and the bubbles that make them, may be the answer. As the waves spread out, they carry energy from the black hole into the surrounding gas and gradually heat it. [pause] The black hole, a tiny point at the centre, may be acting as the thermostat for a structure millions of light-years wide.

## Hearing The Unhearable
mood: wonder
> i: chandra xray | v: chandra xray
In 2022, NASA turned the Perseus data into something people could actually hear. Scientists took the real sound waves found in the X-ray image and raised their pitch by fifty seven and fifty eight octaves, multiplying their frequency by hundreds of quadrillions, until they reached the range of human hearing.

> i: perseus cluster | v: galaxy cluster
The result was an eerie, rumbling, groaning drone, like something alive, breathing deep inside the cluster. It spread around the world. Millions of people listened to the sound of a black hole for the first time. [pause] What they heard was real, just sped up beyond imagination.

> i: m87 jet | v: m87 jet
Other clusters and galaxies show similar ripples and bubbles. Wherever giant black holes sit in hot gas, they seem to make it ring. The universe is not as silent as we thought. In the right places, it hums.

> i: perseus cluster chandra | v: galaxy cluster
Somewhere out there, right now, a black hole is blowing another bubble into the gas around it, and another pressure wave is beginning its slow journey outward. Ten million years for a single beat. [pause] A note that began before the Earth was born, and will still be sounding long after the Sun has gone.
