Forty one light-years from Earth, there is a planet where a year lasts less than a day. It whips around its star once every eighteen hours, so close that the star fills a huge part of its sky.

> i: lava planet | v: lava
Its day side is hotter than the inside of a volcano. Hot enough that the surface is thought to be molten. Not a lava lake here and there, but a global ocean of liquid rock, glowing under a permanent sun.

> i: super earth lava | v: exoplanet
For a while, some scientists even suggested it might be made largely of carbon, with a layer of diamond beneath its surface. The press called it the diamond planet. [pause] The truth turned out to be different, and in some ways, even stranger.

> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
In 2024, the James Webb Space Telescope found something on this world that had never been seen before on a rocky planet beyond our solar system. [pause] It may have an atmosphere, breathed out by its own lava.

## A Star You Can See
mood: wonder
> i: cancer constellation | v: night sky
The planet orbits a star called {{55 Cancri|55 CANCRI}}, in the constellation Cancer, the crab. On a very dark, clear night, far from city lights, you can just see the star with your own eyes. A faint point of yellow-orange, slightly cooler and smaller than our Sun.

> i: binary star | v: binary star
It is not alone. A small red dwarf star orbits it from far away. And the main star has at least five planets. One of them is a giant, several times the mass of Jupiter, orbiting far out. Others are closer in. [pause] And the innermost is the strangest of them all.

> i: 55 cancri e | v: exoplanet orbit
It is called {{55 Cancri e|55 CANCRI e}}. In 2015, it was given a proper name, Janssen, after a Dutch spectacle maker connected to the invention of the telescope. It is about twice as wide as the Earth and about eight times as heavy, a type of world called a super-Earth.

> i: exoplanet transit | v: exoplanet transit
When it was first detected, astronomers thought its year was nearly three days long. In 2011, they realised they had been fooled by gaps in their observations. The real orbit was much shorter. [pause] About {{18 hours|18 HOURS}}. A birthday every three quarters of a day.

## Skimming The Star
mood: dread
> i: lava planet | v: lava planet
To orbit that fast, 55 Cancri e has to be extremely close to its star. About two and a half million kilometres. More than twenty times closer than Mercury is to our Sun.

> i: 55 cancri e | v: exoplanet
From its surface, the star would appear dozens of times wider than our Sun looks from Earth, a seething wall of light filling a vast stretch of sky. And because the planet is almost certainly tidally locked, one side would face that star forever, and the other would never see it.

> i: lava | v: lava
In 2016, astronomers used the Spitzer Space Telescope to make a rough temperature map. The day side reached over two thousand degrees, hot enough to melt most kinds of rock. The night side was much cooler, though still glowing hot by any normal standard. [pause] A planet split between endless furnace and endless dusk.

> i: lava planet | v: lava
On the day side, the surface is thought to be a {{magma ocean|MAGMA OCEAN}}. A sea of molten rock, possibly hundreds of kilometres deep, churning under an unending sunrise. [pause] If you could stand on its shore, the waves would be made of liquid stone.

> i: lava planet night side | v: lava planet
Walk towards the night side, and the sun sinks slowly to the horizon, then disappears. The ground hardens into dark, glassy crust, still hot enough to glow faintly red. Some models suggest that rock vapour boiled off the day side could drift here, cool, and fall back as a fine rain of minerals. [pause] A night where it snows stone.

## The Diamond Planet
mood: mystery
> i: diamond | v: diamond
In 2012, a team led by astronomer Nikku Madhusudhan proposed something sensational. Based on the planet's size and mass, and some early estimates of its star's chemistry, 55 Cancri e might be unusually rich in carbon.

> i: diamond | v: diamond
On a carbon-rich planet, under that much pressure and heat, much of the carbon could be squeezed into graphite and diamond. Perhaps a third of the planet's mass. The headlines wrote themselves. A planet made of diamond, forty light-years away, worth more than all the wealth on Earth.

> i: 55 cancri e | v: exoplanet
But later measurements of the star suggested it was not as carbon-rich as first thought. And new observations of the planet pointed to a different picture. [pause] The diamond planet was probably never really a diamond planet. But the real story was just beginning.

## Breathing Lava
mood: mystery
> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
Rocky planets around other stars are hard to study. They are small, and their atmospheres, if they have any, are thin. For years, astronomers had hunted for a rocky exoplanet with an atmosphere of its own and found none they could confirm.

> i: 55 cancri e | v: exoplanet atmosphere
In 2024, a team used Webb to measure the heat coming from 55 Cancri e's day side as the planet passed behind its star. If it were a bare rock, the day side should have been hotter than they saw. Something was carrying heat away from the hottest spot. [pause] Something like an atmosphere.

> i: webb miri | v: webb telescope
The measurement itself is astonishing. Webb watched the system as the planet slipped behind its star and the total light dropped, by a tiny fraction. That dip was the planet's own glow, measured from more than forty light-years away. [pause] The difference between a bare rock and a world with air was hidden in a change of a few parts in a hundred thousand.

> i: lava planet | v: exoplanet atmosphere
The data suggested a thick layer of gas, likely rich in {{carbon dioxide or carbon monoxide|CO₂ · CO}}. But the planet is so hot, and so close to its star, that any original atmosphere should have been blasted away long ago. So where did this one come from?

> i: lava | v: volcano
The leading idea is that it comes from below. The magma ocean releases gases as it bubbles and churns, constantly topping up the atmosphere as the star strips it away. [pause] It may be the first rocky world outside our solar system known to have air at all, and it is breathed out by an ocean of molten rock.

## A Window Into Our Past
mood: wonder
> i: early earth | v: early earth
Here is why that matters. Billions of years ago, the young Earth was also covered in a global ocean of magma, after its formation and after the giant impact that created the Moon. Our planet's first atmosphere may have been released by that molten rock.

> i: 55 cancri e | v: lava planet
55 Cancri e could be showing us something like the infant Earth, frozen in that violent early state forever, because it is too close to its star to ever cool down. [pause] A planet stuck in its own birth.

> i: lava planet | v: exoplanet
Its brightness has also been seen to change from one observation to the next, in ways that are hard to explain. Some scientists suspect giant volcanic eruptions, or clouds of vaporised rock coming and going. [pause] It may be a world of constant upheaval.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
So the next time you see the faint stars of Cancer, remember. Around one of them, eighteen hours at a time, a molten world is racing around its sun, breathing out an atmosphere from a sea of liquid stone. [pause] Not a diamond. Something much rarer. A glimpse of what the Earth once was.
