Almost every big moon in our solar system follows the same rule. It orbits in the same direction its planet spins. Our Moon does. Jupiter's moons do. Saturn's moons do. It is the natural result of moons forming from the same spinning disc as their planets.

> i: triton voyager | v: neptune
One giant moon breaks that rule. It circles its planet backwards, against the spin, on a steeply tilted path. It is far out at the edge of the solar system, orbiting Neptune, and it is bigger than Pluto.

> i: triton | v: triton
Its surface is one of the coldest ever measured. And yet when a spacecraft flew past it, it saw dark plumes erupting kilometres high into a thin sky. [pause] This is {{Triton|TRITON}}. A moon that was never supposed to be here.

> i: neptune | v: neptune rotation
Everything about it suggests it was born somewhere else, then stolen by Neptune. And its theft may have wrecked an entire family of moons. [pause] This is the story of a captured world, and how it will end.

## Found In Seventeen Days
mood: wonder
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
Neptune was discovered on the twenty third of September 1846, by calculation and a telescope in Berlin. Just seventeen days later, on the tenth of October, an English brewer and amateur astronomer named William Lassell spotted a moon beside it.

> i: triton | v: triton
For well over a century, Triton remained a faint point of light. Astronomers could tell that it was large, but very little else. They worked out its strange backwards orbit. They guessed it might be icy. [pause] And then they waited, for one of the longest journeys in the history of exploration.

> i: voyager 2 | v: voyager 2
On the twenty fifth of August 1989, Voyager 2 swept past Neptune and, a few hours later, Triton. It was the spacecraft's final planetary encounter, twelve years after launch. What it saw was one of the most bizarre landscapes in the solar system.

> i: voyager 2 | v: spacecraft flight
By then, Voyager 2 was four and a half billion kilometres from home. Its radio signals took more than four hours to reach the Earth. Engineers had reprogrammed it from afar to take long, steady exposures in the dim light, compensating for the motion of the spacecraft. [pause] Every image of Triton was a feat of patience across the solar system.

## A World Colder Than Almost Anywhere
mood: dread
> i: triton surface | v: triton
Triton is about two thousand seven hundred kilometres across, bigger than Pluto. Its surface reflects most of the sunlight that hits it, and it receives about nine hundred times less sunlight than the Earth. Voyager measured its surface at about {{minus 235 degrees|−235°C}}.

> i: triton | v: triton
At that temperature, nitrogen, the gas that makes up most of the air you breathe, freezes solid. Triton's surface is coated in nitrogen ice, along with frozen methane and carbon monoxide. Its south pole was covered in a pinkish cap of frost.

> i: triton cantaloupe terrain | v: triton
And part of its surface looks like nothing else anywhere. A huge region covered in rounded dimples and crisscrossing ridges, like the skin of a melon. Scientists named it {{cantaloupe terrain|CANTALOUPE TERRAIN}}. It may have formed as blobs of warmer ice slowly rose through the crust, like wax in a lava lamp.

> i: triton surface | v: triton
There are very few impact craters on Triton. That means its surface is young, perhaps only tens of millions of years old in places. [pause] Something has been renewing it, even out here, in the deep freeze.

## Geysers In The Cold
mood: mystery
> i: triton geysers | v: triton
Then came the biggest surprise. In Voyager's images, scientists spotted dark streaks across the polar cap, and at least two plumes rising straight up from the surface, about {{eight kilometres high|8 KM HIGH}}, before being caught by thin winds and carried sideways for more than a hundred kilometres.

> i: triton geysers | v: geyser
Geysers, on a world colder than almost anywhere in the solar system. One leading explanation is a kind of solar-powered greenhouse. Faint sunlight passes through clear nitrogen ice and warms darker material underneath. Nitrogen gas builds up beneath the ice, until it bursts through, carrying dark dust with it. [pause] Other scientists think the energy may come from inside Triton itself.

> i: triton | v: triton
Triton also has an atmosphere, mostly nitrogen, but incredibly thin, about seventy thousand times thinner than the air at the Earth's surface. Thin as it is, it has hazes and even faint clouds. [pause] A frozen world with weather.

> i: triton surface | v: triton
Imagine standing there. Neptune hangs in the black sky, a huge pale blue globe, many times wider than the full Moon appears from Earth. The ground under your feet is frozen nitrogen, crunching like snow. And on the horizon, a dark column rises silently into the thin air, then bends away in the wind. [pause] A geyser erupting in a sky colder than any winter on Earth.

## Stolen From The Kuiper Belt
mood: mystery
> i: kuiper belt | v: kuiper belt
Why does Triton orbit backwards? The best explanation is that it did not form around Neptune at all. It probably began its life in the {{Kuiper Belt|KUIPER BELT}}, the zone of icy worlds beyond Neptune where Pluto lives. Its size, its make-up and its surface all look remarkably like Pluto's.

> i: binary kuiper belt object | v: kuiper belt
But a planet cannot easily grab a passing object. It would simply fly past. One clever idea is that Triton was once half of a pair, two icy worlds orbiting each other. When the pair passed close to Neptune, the planet's gravity pulled them apart, capturing Triton and flinging its partner away.

> i: neptune | v: neptune rotation
The capture would have been violent. Triton's orbit would at first have been wildly stretched, and the tides from Neptune would have flexed and heated its interior enormously while the orbit settled into a circle. That heat may have melted parts of the moon, and may even have created a hidden ocean that survives today beneath the ice. [pause] Some scientists consider Triton a possible ocean world.

> i: nereid | v: neptune
And the capture may have wrecked Neptune's original moons. A giant intruder sweeping through the system would have scattered or destroyed many of the moons that formed there. Neptune's small moon Nereid, with its wildly stretched orbit, may be one of the survivors. [pause] Triton may have been a home-wrecker.

## Falling To Its Death
mood: dread
> i: triton | v: triton
Because Triton orbits backwards, Neptune's tides do not push it away the way our Moon is pushed away from the Earth. They drag it inward instead. Very slowly, Triton is spiralling closer to its planet.

> i: neptune rings | v: neptune
In perhaps three to four billion years, it will cross a line where Neptune's gravity becomes stronger than the forces holding the moon together. Triton will be {{torn apart|TORN APART}}. Its fragments will spread around Neptune into a vast system of rings, possibly grander than Saturn's.

> i: spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
We have not been back since 1989. Scientists have proposed missions to return, including one called Trident, designed to fly through Triton's thin atmosphere and look for signs of an ocean and active plumes. It was not selected. [pause] For now, Voyager's few hours of images are almost all we have.

> i: triton voyager | v: triton
A world stolen from the Kuiper Belt. Orbiting the wrong way. Erupting in the deep cold. Hiding, perhaps, an ocean. And slowly falling towards the planet that captured it. [pause] Triton is a reminder that even the quiet edge of the solar system has a violent history. And a dramatic ending still to come.
