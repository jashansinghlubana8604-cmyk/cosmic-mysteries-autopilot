Mercury is the planet closest to the Sun. The smallest of the eight. A grey, cratered ball that looks, at first glance, almost exactly like our own Moon. Most people barely think about it.

> i: mercury planet | v: mercury rotation
But Mercury is one of the strangest worlds in the solar system. It is shrinking, cracking its own surface as it goes. Its core is so enormous it makes up most of the planet. Its days are longer than its years.

> i: mercury surface | v: mercury
And despite being the closest planet to the Sun, where daytime temperatures can melt lead, it has ice. Billions of tonnes of it, hidden at its poles. [pause] Mercury breaks almost every rule we expected a small, hot planet to follow.

> i: mercury | v: mercury
Only two spacecraft have ever studied it up close, and a third has been on a long journey there. [pause] This is the planet nobody pays attention to. And it may hold the key to how planets like ours were made.

## The Hardest Place To Reach
mood: mystery
> i: sun | v: sun
You might think Mercury would be easy to visit. It is not that far away. But it is one of the hardest places in the solar system to get to. Because falling towards the Sun makes a spacecraft speed up, enormously. To go into orbit around Mercury, you have to brake against all that speed.

> i: mariner 10 | v: spacecraft
The first visitor, {{Mariner 10|MARINER 10}}, flew past Mercury three times in 1974 and 1975, photographing less than half its surface. For more than thirty years, that was all we had.

> i: messenger spacecraft | v: messenger spacecraft
Then NASA's {{MESSENGER|MESSENGER}} spacecraft took more than six years and several flybys of the Earth, Venus and Mercury itself to slow down enough to enter orbit in 2011. It mapped the entire planet and studied it for four years, until it ran out of fuel and crashed into the surface in 2015.

> i: bepicolombo | v: bepicolombo
A third mission, the European and Japanese {{BepiColombo|BEPICOLOMBO}}, launched in 2018 on a winding seven-year journey with nine planetary flybys. It is due to settle into orbit around Mercury in late 2026, with its main science work starting after that. [pause] Getting to the smallest planet has taken longer than getting to Saturn.

## A Day Longer Than A Year
mood: wonder
> i: mercury rotation | v: mercury rotation
Mercury orbits the Sun once every eighty eight Earth days. That is its year. But it spins slowly, turning exactly three times for every two trips around the Sun. And that strange rhythm produces something bizarre.

> i: mercury surface | v: sunrise
From one sunrise to the next takes about one hundred and seventy six Earth days. {{A day lasts two years|1 DAY = 2 YEARS}}. Stand on the surface, and the Sun crawls across the sky so slowly that it takes months to set.

> i: sun | v: sun
And in some places, something even stranger happens. Because Mercury's orbit is stretched, its speed around the Sun changes. Near its closest approach, its orbital motion briefly outpaces its spin. [pause] From certain spots, you would see the Sun rise, stop, sink back down, and then rise again.

> i: mercury surface | v: mercury
During the day, the surface reaches around four hundred and thirty degrees. With almost no atmosphere to hold the heat, the night drops to around minus one hundred and eighty. [pause] Nowhere else in the solar system swings between such extremes.

> i: caloris basin | v: mercury surface
Mercury's surface also carries the scar of one of the biggest impacts in the solar system. The {{Caloris Basin|CALORIS BASIN}} is about one and a half thousand kilometres across. The shock of that impact seems to have travelled right through the planet. On the exact opposite side, the ground is a jumble of broken hills that scientists simply call the weird terrain.

## The Shrinking Planet
mood: mystery
> i: mercury core | v: mercury interior
Mercury has a secret inside. Its iron core is gigantic, stretching out to about eighty five percent of the planet's radius. Imagine a peach, where the stone takes up almost the whole fruit. [pause] No other rocky planet in our solar system is so dominated by its core.

> i: mercury magnetic field | v: magnetic field
And that core is at least partly molten. Mariner 10 discovered that Mercury has a magnetic field, about one percent as strong as the Earth's. It was a surprise. Such a small planet was expected to have frozen solid inside long ago. [pause] Strangely, the field is not centred on the planet, but shifted far to the north.

> i: mercury interior | v: mercury interior
As that huge core slowly cools over billions of years, it contracts. And as it shrinks, the whole planet shrinks with it. The rocky crust on top has to crumple to fit, like the skin of a drying apple.

> i: mercury scarp | v: mercury surface
MESSENGER found the evidence everywhere. Giant cliffs, called scarps, where one part of the crust has been shoved up over another. Some run for hundreds of kilometres and rise more than a kilometre high. Scientists estimate that Mercury's radius has shrunk by up to {{seven kilometres|7 KM}} since its crust formed.

> i: mercury surface | v: mercury
And it may still be shrinking. MESSENGER also spotted small, fresh-looking scarps, so young they must have formed recently in geological terms. [pause] Mercury is probably still quaking, still cracking, right now.

## Ice Near The Sun
mood: wonder
> i: mercury poles | v: mercury
In 1991, astronomers bounced radar signals off Mercury and found something that made no sense. Bright patches at the poles, the kind of reflection you get from ice.

> i: mercury poles messenger | v: mercury
MESSENGER confirmed it. Mercury is barely tilted, so the floors of some craters near its poles never see sunlight. They stay colder than minus one hundred and seventy degrees, forever. Inside them, there is water ice. Estimates suggest {{tens to hundreds of billions of tonnes|BILLIONS OF TONNES}}, possibly delivered by comets.

> i: mercury hollows | v: mercury surface
MESSENGER also found something no one had ever seen on any other world. Shallow, bright depressions with sharp edges, called hollows. They look as though the ground itself has been evaporating, perhaps as sunlight bakes away volatile materials inside the rock. [pause] Mercury may be losing its surface to the Sun, a little at a time.

> i: mercury surface | v: mercury
The planet is also richer in elements like sulphur and potassium than expected for a world formed so close to the Sun. Those elements should have been driven off by the heat. [pause] Which raises the biggest question of all.

## Where Did Mercury Come From?
mood: mystery
> i: planet collision | v: planet collision
Why is Mercury mostly core? One idea is a colossal collision early in the solar system's history, which stripped away much of its rocky outer layers, leaving the dense metal heart behind.

> i: mercury | v: mercury
But the surprising amounts of volatile elements argue against such a violent, scorching past. Another idea is that Mercury formed further from the Sun and later moved inward. Or that the region it formed in was simply rich in iron. Nobody knows which story is true.

> i: diamond | v: diamond
And one study in 2024 suggested something remarkable. Mercury's crust seems to contain a lot of carbon, in the form of graphite. Under the enormous pressure deep inside, near the boundary with the core, some of that carbon may have been squeezed into a layer of {{diamond|DIAMOND}}, perhaps kilometres thick. [pause] A small, scorched planet, possibly hiding a shell of diamond around its heart.

> i: mercury messenger | v: mercury rotation
A day that lasts two years. A shrinking, cracking crust. Ice in the shadows near the Sun. A gigantic metal core, and maybe diamonds. [pause] Mercury is not a dull grey rock. It is a puzzle sitting right next to our star. And we are only beginning to solve it.
