Right now, above your head, there are more than ten thousand working satellites. They bring you weather forecasts, GPS directions, phone calls, television, internet and warnings of storms. Modern life depends on them.

> i: space debris | v: space debris
But they are not alone up there. They share space with tens of thousands of pieces of junk large enough to track, and many millions of smaller fragments. Dead satellites. Spent rocket stages. Bolts. Flakes of paint. Shards from explosions and collisions.

> i: space debris | v: space debris
All of it is moving at around twenty eight thousand kilometres an hour. Ten times faster than a bullet. At that speed, a piece of metal the size of a marble can hit with the force of a falling piano. [pause] And every collision makes more debris.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
In 1978, a NASA scientist warned that one day this could spiral out of control. One collision causing another, then another, until whole regions of space become a lethal storm of shrapnel. [pause] He was describing a chain reaction that could trap humanity on the ground for generations.

## A Warning From 1978
mood: dread
> i: donald kessler | v: earth orbit
The scientist was {{Donald Kessler|DONALD KESSLER}}. In 1978, together with his colleague Burton Cour-Palais, he published a paper asking a simple question. What happens as we keep putting more objects into orbit?

> i: sputnik | v: rocket launch
It had only been twenty one years since Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, went up in 1957, a polished metal ball beeping around the Earth. Since then, every launch had left something behind. Rocket bodies, covers, broken satellites. Nobody had planned for any of it to come down.

> i: space debris | v: space debris
Their answer was alarming. If the number of objects in a popular orbit grows large enough, collisions between them become inevitable. Each collision shatters both objects into hundreds or thousands of fragments. Those fragments then hit other objects, creating even more fragments.

> i: space debris earth | v: space debris
Past a certain point, the process feeds itself. Even if we stopped launching anything at all, the debris would keep multiplying, collision after collision. That runaway cascade became known as the {{Kessler syndrome|KESSLER SYNDROME}}.

> i: satellites orbit | v: satellites orbit
It would not happen in a single dramatic afternoon, the way it is shown in films. It would unfold over years or decades. [pause] Slowly, silently, a band of space around the Earth would become too dangerous to use.

## Collisions Already Happening
mood: dread
> i: satellite | v: satellite
For a while, the warning seemed theoretical. Then, on the tenth of February 2009, two satellites collided over Siberia. An active American communications satellite, {{Iridium 33|IRIDIUM 33}}, and a dead Russian military satellite, Kosmos 2251.

> i: satellite collision | v: space debris
They hit each other at around eleven kilometres per second. Both were destroyed instantly, creating more than two thousand pieces of trackable debris. It was the first accidental collision between two whole satellites. [pause] Many of those fragments are still in orbit today.

> i: anti satellite test | v: rocket launch
Some of the worst debris was created on purpose. In 2007, China tested an anti-satellite weapon by destroying one of its own old weather satellites. The test created more than three thousand trackable fragments, one of the biggest debris events in history. In 2021, a Russian anti-satellite test created more than a thousand more.

> i: international space station | v: international space station
The International Space Station has had to move out of the way of debris dozens of times. On several occasions, astronauts have sheltered in their docked spacecraft, ready to leave quickly, as fragments passed close by. [pause] Space junk is not a future problem. It is already shaping how we live in orbit.

## Why Tiny Things Are Deadly
mood: dread
> i: space debris | v: space debris
At orbital speeds, even very small objects are dangerous. The Space Shuttle had windows replaced more than once after being struck by tiny flecks of paint. The Space Station's windows are covered in small chips.

> i: whipple shield | v: international space station
To protect itself, the Station uses layered armour called {{Whipple shields|WHIPPLE SHIELDS}}. A thin outer layer breaks up an incoming particle into a spray of fragments, which then spread out and hit the inner wall more gently. But no shield can stop larger debris.

> i: space debris earth | v: space debris
Estimates suggest there are around a million pieces of debris between one and ten centimetres across, too small to track reliably but big enough to destroy a satellite. And there may be well over a hundred million smaller ones. [pause] Many satellites operate every day in a field of invisible bullets.

> i: radar station | v: radio telescope
To keep track of the larger pieces, military and civilian radar stations around the world watch the sky constantly, cataloguing tens of thousands of objects and predicting where each one will be. When two objects are forecast to pass too close, satellite operators get a warning and may fire their thrusters to dodge. [pause] Every day, someone somewhere is steering a satellite out of the way of junk.

## The Crowded Sky
mood: mystery
> i: starlink satellites | v: satellites orbit
The number of satellites is now growing faster than ever. Companies are launching enormous constellations of thousands of satellites to provide internet access around the world. In just a few years, the number of active satellites has multiplied.

> i: satellites orbit | v: satellites orbit
Supporters point out that these modern satellites are designed to move out of the way of debris automatically, and to drop out of orbit and burn up at the end of their lives. Low orbits also clean themselves. Below about six hundred kilometres, the thin upper atmosphere drags debris down within years.

> i: earth atmosphere | v: earth from space
But higher up, things can stay for centuries. Debris above about a thousand kilometres may orbit for longer than human civilisation has existed. [pause] Whatever we leave up there, our distant descendants will have to deal with.

> i: gps satellite | v: satellites orbit
If a runaway cascade ever did take hold in a crowded orbit, the cost would be felt on the ground. Weather forecasting, navigation, global communication, disaster monitoring and climate research all depend on satellites. [pause] Losing access to some orbits would be like losing a piece of the planet's nervous system.

> i: solar flare | v: sun
And the Sun plays a role too. When it is active, it heats and swells the Earth's upper atmosphere, increasing drag and pulling more junk down. During quiet periods, debris lingers longer. [pause] The fate of our orbits is tied to the rhythm of our star.

## Cleaning Up
mood: wonder
> i: debris removal spacecraft | v: spacecraft
Scientists and engineers are now working on ways to clean up. Spacecraft that can approach a dead satellite, grab it with arms, nets or magnets, and drag it down to burn up in the atmosphere.

> i: debris removal spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
In 2024, a Japanese spacecraft from the company Astroscale flew close to an abandoned rocket stage and photographed it in detail, a first step towards capturing large debris. The European Space Agency has also been developing a mission to remove a piece of junk from orbit.

> i: satellites orbit | v: satellites orbit
New rules are also being introduced. In the United States, satellites in low orbit are now expected to be removed within five years after their missions end. The goal is simple: stop making the problem worse, while we learn to clean up what is already there.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
The danger is real, but it is not hopeless. The Kessler syndrome is a slow-motion threat, and we can see it coming. [pause] Space is the only path off our planet. The question is whether we will keep that path open, or slowly bury it under our own rubbish.
