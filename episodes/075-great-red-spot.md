## How Old Is It?
mood: mystery
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
In 1665, the astronomer Giovanni Cassini saw a dark spot on Jupiter. For years afterwards, astronomers kept tracking what they called the Permanent Spot. Then, early in the 1700s, it disappeared from the records.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotation
For a long time, people assumed Cassini's spot and today's Great Red Spot were the same storm, making it more than three hundred and fifty years old. But in 2024, a study of old drawings and observations concluded that they were probably different storms. The spot Cassini saw seems to have faded away.

> i: great red spot | v: jupiter storm
The Great Red Spot we know has been watched continuously since at least {{1831|1831}}. So it is around two centuries old, at the very least. [pause] Still, by far the longest-lived storm we have ever observed anywhere.

> i: jupiter clouds | v: jupiter clouds
On Earth, even the biggest hurricanes last a few weeks at most. They lose power when they move over land, where there is no warm ocean to feed them. Jupiter has no land. [pause] A storm there can spin for centuries, because nothing ever stops it.

## A Storm Bigger Than Worlds
mood: wonder
> i: great red spot juno | v: jupiter storm
The Great Red Spot is not like a hurricane. It spins the other way, and it is a high-pressure system, a giant {{anticyclone|ANTICYCLONE}}, pushing up from below and spreading out at the top. It turns once every six days or so.

> i: great red spot | v: jupiter storm
Its outer winds race at more than four hundred kilometres per hour, and in places much faster. In the late 1800s, it was so wide that around three Earths could have fitted across it side by side. Today it is only a little wider than one.

> i: jupiter storm | v: jupiter clouds
Imagine flying into it. At the edge, a wall of cloud racing past faster than a jet airliner, shredding anything in its path into turbulence. But towards the centre, the winds slow down. The eye of the Great Red Spot is strangely calm, a slowly turning sea of reddish haze, surrounded by a ring of chaos wider than the Earth.

> i: juno spacecraft | v: juno spacecraft
And it is deep. In 2019, NASA's Juno spacecraft flew directly over it, and used precise measurements of Jupiter's gravity and microwave light to probe beneath the clouds. The storm extends roughly three hundred to five hundred kilometres down. [pause] Deeper than the Earth's oceans many times over. Shallow compared to Jupiter itself.

> i: jupiter clouds | v: jupiter clouds
It also heats the sky above it. In 2016, astronomers discovered that the upper atmosphere directly above the spot is hundreds of degrees hotter than elsewhere. They think waves of energy rising from the turbulent storm below, like sound waves, are breaking high in the atmosphere and heating it. [pause] The storm is so loud, in a sense, that it cooks the air above it.

## Why Is It Red?
mood: mystery
> i: great red spot | v: jupiter storm
Here is a surprising fact. Nobody is quite sure why the Great Red Spot is red. Most of Jupiter's clouds are made of ammonia ice, which is white. The red must come from something else.

> i: jupiter clouds | v: jupiter clouds
One idea is that the storm lifts chemicals, perhaps compounds containing ammonia and sulphur, high into the upper atmosphere. There, ultraviolet sunlight breaks them apart and turns them into reddish molecules, like a sunburn on the clouds.

> i: great red spot hubble | v: jupiter rotation
The colour also changes over time. Some years it is deep brick red. Other years pale salmon or almost beige. [pause] We have been staring at it for two hundred years, and its colour is still a mystery.

> i: great red spot juno | v: juno spacecraft
On the tenth of July 2017, Juno passed about nine thousand kilometres above the storm and sent back the most detailed images of it ever taken. Up close, it was not a smooth red oval at all, but a tangle of dark filaments, swirling bands and bright cloud tops twisting around each other. [pause] The closer we looked, the stranger it became.

## The Shrinking Storm
mood: dread
> i: jupiter great red spot hubble | v: jupiter storm
When Voyager flew past Jupiter in 1979, the Great Red Spot was about twenty three thousand kilometres across, almost twice the width of the Earth. By 2017, Hubble measured it at around sixteen thousand. It keeps getting smaller.

> i: great red spot | v: jupiter storm
It is also changing shape, from a long oval into something closer to a circle. In 2024, Hubble watched it closely for months and found that it {{wobbles|IT WOBBLES}}, its size and shape jiggling back and forth over a cycle of about ninety days, like a giant bowl of jelly.

> i: jupiter storm | v: jupiter clouds
Why is it shrinking? One idea is that the storm survives by swallowing smaller storms that drift into it, gaining energy from each meal. In recent decades, fewer small storms may have been feeding it. [pause] It might simply be starving.

> i: great red spot | v: jupiter rotation
Some scientists think it could keep shrinking until it fades away within a few decades. Others think it will settle down at a stable smaller size, and keep spinning for a very long time. [pause] We may be the generation that watches it disappear. Or the one that sees it hold on.

## Storms That Turn Red
mood: wonder
> i: oval ba | v: jupiter storm
The Great Red Spot is not the only storm with a red heart. In 2000, three smaller white storms on Jupiter merged into one. A few years later, in 2005 and 2006, the new storm turned red too. Astronomers nicknamed it {{Red Spot Junior|RED SPOT JR.}}

> i: jupiter clouds | v: jupiter clouds
It is about the width of the Earth, and its reddening may have happened because it grew strong enough to lift material high enough for sunlight to colour it. [pause] Perhaps the Great Red Spot itself began life like that, as a smaller white storm that grew powerful enough to blush.

> i: jupiter juno | v: jupiter rotation
And Juno has shown that Jupiter is full of storms. Cyclones cluster around both poles in strange geometric patterns, each as wide as a continent. White ovals, brown barges and swirling filaments crowd the bands. [pause] The Great Red Spot is simply the oldest and grandest of them.

> i: great red spot | v: jupiter storm
Next time you see Jupiter through a telescope, look for a small orange dot on the lower half of the planet. It will look tiny and calm. [pause] It is a storm wider than our whole planet, spinning for at least two centuries, and slowly, quietly, getting smaller. Look while it is still there.
