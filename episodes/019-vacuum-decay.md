## The Field Inside Everything
mood: wonder
> v: cosmic web simulation | i: galaxy cluster
To understand this, we need to talk about empty space. Physicists call it the vacuum. And it turns out the vacuum is not really empty.

> i: particle collisions | v: particle accelerator lhc
In the 1960s, several physicists, including Peter Higgs, proposed that the universe is filled with an invisible field. As particles move through it, they interact with it, and that interaction is what gives them mass. Without it, electrons would have no mass, atoms could not hold together, and nothing we know could exist.

> v: large hadron collider | i: cern large hadron collider
In 2012, at CERN, the Large Hadron Collider smashed protons together at enormous energies and found the particle predicted by this theory, the {{Higgs boson|HIGGS BOSON}}. The field was real. Peter Higgs and François Englert received the Nobel Prize the following year.

> i: galaxy | v: spiral galaxy zoom
Like any field, the Higgs field has an energy. And like a ball resting in a valley, it settles at the lowest energy it can find. That resting place determines the masses of particles, the strength of the forces, the chemistry of atoms. [pause] It determines everything.

> v: early universe | i: cosmic microwave background
The Higgs field has not always rested where it is now. In the first instants after the Big Bang, the universe was so hot that the field sat at a different value entirely, and particles had no mass at all. Only as the universe cooled did the field settle into its present valley, and the world of atoms became possible.

## A Ball In The Wrong Valley
mood: mystery
> v: nebula zoom | i: nebula
But what if the valley we are sitting in is not the lowest one? What if, somewhere beyond a hill, there is a deeper valley that the field has never reached?

> i: dark nebula | v: dark nebula zoom
Physicists call our current state a false vacuum, a resting place that seems stable, but is not the most stable possible. The deeper valley would be the true vacuum. As long as the hill between them is high enough, the universe stays where it is. Perhaps for longer than the age of the universe, many times over.

> v: cosmic microwave background | i: cosmic microwave background
And here is the unsettling part. When physicists take the measured mass of the Higgs boson, and the mass of the heaviest known particle, the top quark, and calculate the shape of those valleys, the answer lands in a strange place.

> i: deep field | v: deep field zoom
Our universe appears to sit right near the boundary between stable and unstable. Not safely stable. Not obviously doomed. Metastable. As if the universe has been built, by chance or by something we do not understand, right on the edge.

## The Bubble
mood: dread
> v: expanding bubble | i: bubble nebula
In quantum physics, a ball does not always need to climb over a hill. It can, very rarely, tunnel straight through it. So if our vacuum is metastable, somewhere, someday, a tiny region of space could tunnel into the true vacuum.

> i: supernova remnant bubble | v: supernova shockwave
That region would not stay small. It would form a bubble, and the bubble would grow, expanding at almost the speed of light, converting everything in its path. Inside it, the Higgs field would have a different value. Particles would have different masses. Chemistry, as we know it, would no longer work.

> v: shockwave expanding | i: shock wave nebula
Because the wall of the bubble moves at nearly the speed of light, there would be no warning. No light could outrun it to tell us it was coming. One moment, the universe would be exactly as it is. The next moment, it would not. In 1980, the physicists Sidney Coleman and Frank De Luccia described this as the ultimate ecological catastrophe.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
And even the bubble could not consume everything. The universe is expanding, and the most distant galaxies are being carried away from us faster than any bubble could ever follow. If it began far enough away, it would never reach us at all.

## Should We Be Worried?
mood: wonder
> v: milky way time-lapse | i: milky way over observatory
So, should you lose sleep over this? Almost certainly not.

> i: deep field galaxies | v: zoom deep field
The same calculations that suggest our universe is metastable also estimate how long it should last. The numbers are almost impossible to imagine, with far more than a hundred zeros. The universe is about fourteen billion years old. The expected lifetime of our vacuum, if these calculations are right, is longer than that by an amount so vast it has no meaningful comparison.

> v: particle collisions | i: lhc collisions
Some people worried that particle accelerators might trigger vacuum decay. But nature already runs experiments far more powerful. Cosmic rays smash into the Moon, the planets and the stars at energies far beyond anything we can produce, and have done so for billions of years. The universe is still here.

> i: particle physics detector | v: cern detector
And the calculation itself depends on physics we do not yet fully understand. A tiny change in the measured mass of the top quark could move us safely into stability. Undiscovered particles, or new forces, could reshape the valleys entirely. Dark matter, gravity at the smallest scales, and physics beyond the Standard Model could all change the answer.

> v: zoom deep field | i: hubble ultra deep field
There is even a strange comfort in the numbers. If a bubble of true vacuum had ever formed anywhere in our past light cone, close enough to reach us, we would not be here to talk about it. Every star you see tonight is a quiet sign that, so far, the universe has held.

## Living On The Edge
mood: wonder
> v: earth from space | i: earth night
What vacuum decay really shows us is something profound. The laws of physics feel permanent, carved into the foundation of reality. But they may be more like the shape of a landscape, which happened to settle one way, and could, in principle, have settled differently.

> i: spiral galaxy | v: galaxy rotation
The fact that our universe allows stars, planets and living things depends on the precise values of fields we can barely measure. And somehow, those values ended up in a narrow range where all of this is possible, right on the edge between stability and change.

> v: star forming region | i: pillars of creation
Physicists are measuring the Higgs boson and the top quark more precisely every year, trying to find out exactly where we stand. Future colliders may answer the question for good. [pause] Until then, we live in a universe that may be quietly balanced on a knife edge, and has been, for every moment of history.

> i: deep field | v: zoom out universe
And perhaps that is the most remarkable thing of all. Out of all the ways the universe could have been, it settled into one that allowed us to exist, to look up, and to ask whether it will last.
