Tonight, the sky is full of stars. Hundreds of billions of galaxies are shining across the universe. Stars are being born in glowing clouds. It feels permanent.

> v: zoom out universe | i: galaxies

## The Next Few Billion Years
mood: wonder
> v: sun | i: sun solar flare
Let us start close to home. Our Sun is about four and a half billion years old, roughly halfway through its life. But it is slowly brightening. In about a billion years, it will be bright enough to boil away the Earth's oceans. Our planet will become a desert, long before the Sun dies.

> v: red giant sun | i: red giant
In about five billion years, the Sun will run out of hydrogen in its core. It will swell into a {{red giant|RED GIANT}}, perhaps two hundred times wider than it is now, engulfing Mercury and Venus, and possibly the Earth.

> i: planetary nebula | v: planetary nebula
Then it will gently puff away its outer layers into space, creating a glowing shell of gas, a planetary nebula, beautiful for a few tens of thousands of years. What remains will be a white dwarf, a hot, dense core about the size of the Earth, slowly cooling forever.

> v: andromeda milky way collision | i: andromeda milky way collision
Meanwhile, the Milky Way and Andromeda may collide. Recent studies suggest that collision is less certain than once thought. If it happens, the two spirals would merge into one giant elliptical galaxy over a few billion years. Stars are so far apart that they would almost never actually hit each other.

> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
The Earth's own story will end long before then. The Moon is slowly drifting away from us, about four centimetres every year, and our days are slowly getting longer. Long before the Sun becomes a red giant, the oceans will be gone and the surface will be too hot for complex life. Whatever lives on Earth in the far future will have to find another home, or end with the planet.

## The Great Fading
mood: wonder
> v: galaxies moving apart | i: expanding universe
On larger scales, the universe is expanding, and that expansion is accelerating, driven by dark energy. Galaxies beyond our local group are being carried away from us faster and faster.

> i: deep field galaxies | v: zoom deep field
In about a hundred billion years, every galaxy outside our local group will have slipped beyond our horizon. Their light will never reach us again. Astronomers then, if there are any, would see a single giant galaxy, alone, surrounded by endless darkness. They might never discover that the universe was once full of other galaxies.

> v: star forming region | i: star forming region
Inside galaxies, star formation is slowing too. The great clouds of gas that build stars are being used up. The universe already makes far fewer new stars today than it did ten billion years ago, and the decline will continue.

> i: red dwarf | v: red dwarf star
The longest living stars are small red dwarfs, like Proxima Centauri. They burn their fuel so slowly that they can shine for trillions of years. Some will outlive the Sun a thousand times over. But eventually, perhaps around {{a hundred trillion years|100 TRILLION YEARS}} from now, even the last of them will run dry.

## The Degenerate Era
mood: dread
> v: white dwarf | i: white dwarf
After the last star dies, the universe enters a long, dark age. It will be filled with the remains of stars: cooling white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes, drifting through the dark.

> i: black dwarf | v: white dwarf cooling
White dwarfs will slowly lose their heat until they become black dwarfs, cold, dark spheres of carbon and oxygen. None exist yet. The universe is not old enough for even one white dwarf to have cooled completely.

> v: galaxy dark | i: galaxy
Over unimaginable stretches of time, galaxies will slowly fall apart. Close encounters between dead stars will fling most of them out into intergalactic space, while the rest spiral into the central black holes.

> i: black hole | v: black hole simulation
Some physicists suspect that even protons, the building blocks of atoms, might eventually decay, after vastly longer than the current age of the universe. Experiments have not seen it happen, and it may never happen at all. But if it does, matter itself will slowly dissolve, leaving behind only light, particles and black holes.

> v: galaxy | i: elliptical galaxy
Time itself almost loses meaning in this era. Events that we would call impossibly rare, happening once in a trillion trillion years, will happen over and over again. The universe will be dark, but it will not be completely still. Every so often, two dead stars will collide, and for a brief moment, a new star might flare to life, a tiny spark in an endless night.

## The Black Hole Era
mood: mystery
> v: black hole accretion disc | i: supermassive black hole
Then the universe will belong to black holes. Nothing will shine except the occasional flash as something falls in. For an almost endless age, black holes will be the only large objects left.

> i: hawking radiation | v: black hole evaporating
But black holes are not eternal either. In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that they slowly leak energy, through what is now called {{Hawking radiation|HAWKING RADIATION}}. They glow, unimaginably faintly, and slowly lose mass.

> v: black hole simulation | i: black hole
A black hole like the ones made by dead stars would take around ten to the power of sixty seven years to evaporate. That is a one followed by sixty seven zeros. The giants at the centres of galaxies would take ten to the power of a hundred years, a number called a googol.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
At the very end of its life, each black hole would shrink faster and faster, then vanish in a final burst of radiation. [pause] The last flash of light the universe will ever see.

## The Long Darkness
mood: wonder
> v: star field slow | i: dark space stars
After that, there will be almost nothing. Scattered particles and faint light, stretched by the expansion of space until it is colder than anything we can imagine. No stars, no planets, no black holes. This is the {{heat death|HEAT DEATH}} of the universe, the most likely ending according to our best measurements.

> i: expanding universe | v: galaxies moving apart
There are other possibilities. If dark energy grows stronger over time, it could eventually tear apart galaxies, stars, planets, and even atoms, in a Big Rip. If it weakens and reverses, the universe might one day collapse back on itself, in a Big Crunch. Recent measurements hint that dark energy may be changing, so the final chapter is not yet written.

> v: earth from space | i: earth night
Whatever the ending, we live in an extraordinary moment. The universe is only around thirteen point eight billion years old. Compared to the ages to come, it has barely begun. This is the era of light, of stars and galaxies and planets where life can arise.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
We are here, in the brief, bright morning of the cosmos. [pause] And for now, the stars are still shining.
