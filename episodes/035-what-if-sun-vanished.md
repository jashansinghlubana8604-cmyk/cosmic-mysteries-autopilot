## Eight Minutes Of Ignorance
mood: mystery
> v: sun | i: sun
Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to cross the hundred and fifty million kilometres between the Sun and the Earth. The light hitting your face right now left the Sun before you started watching this video.

> v: light travel | i: sunlight earth
So when the Sun vanishes, its last light is still on its way. For eight minutes, a final wave of sunshine continues to fall on the Earth, while the place it came from is already empty. [pause] We would be living in the past without knowing it.

> i: earth orbit | v: earth orbit
And here is something even stranger. The Earth would keep orbiting too. Einstein showed that changes in gravity also travel at the speed of light. So for those same eight minutes, the Earth keeps curving around a Sun that no longer exists, steered by a gravity that is only an echo.

> i: earth from space | v: earth night
Then, at exactly the same instant, two things happen. The sky goes dark. And the Earth lets go. [pause] Freed from the Sun's pull, our planet stops curving and flies off in a straight line, at about thirty kilometres every second, out into the galaxy.

## The First Day
mood: dread
> v: night sky | i: night sky stars
On the day side of the planet, people would see the Sun go out. No sunset. No dimming. Just a sudden, total darkness, and the stars appearing in the middle of the afternoon.

> v: milky way time-lapse | i: milky way
And they would be the most beautiful stars anyone had ever seen. No sunlight scattering in the air. No glow on the horizon. Across the whole planet, at the same moment, the Milky Way would blaze overhead. [pause] For a few hours, it would be the most spectacular sky in human history. And almost nobody would be in the mood to enjoy it.

> i: full moon | v: moon
The Moon would go dark too. It shines only by reflecting the Sun. Within a second or so it would vanish from the sky, although it would still be there, still orbiting the Earth, because its bond to us does not depend on the Sun at all.

> i: jupiter | v: saturn rings
The planets would linger a little longer. Their light takes longer to reach us. Mars might shine on for several minutes. Jupiter for more than half an hour. Saturn for over an hour. And then, one by one, like lights switched off in a distant city, {{they would go out|LIGHTS OUT}}.

> v: city night earth | i: earth city lights
Power grids that depend on solar energy would fail at once. Satellites would keep running for a while on their batteries. Phone networks would be overwhelmed. But the most dangerous change would be silent. [pause] The temperature would begin to fall, and it would never stop.

## The Long Freeze
mood: dread
> i: frozen earth | v: snow storm
The Earth would not freeze instantly. The ground and the oceans hold a huge amount of heat. But without the Sun to top it up, that heat would bleed away into space.

> i: frozen earth | v: ice
Scientists estimate that within about a week, the average temperature at the surface would drop below minus fifteen degrees. Within a year, it could fall to around {{minus seventy degrees|−73°C}}, colder than the coldest night ever measured in Antarctica.

> v: plants | i: forest
Photosynthesis would stop the moment the light disappeared. Most plants would die within weeks. Some giant trees, with huge stores of sugar locked inside them, might survive for years, standing in the dark, slowly starving. Then the animals that ate the plants, and the animals that ate those animals.

> i: frozen ocean | v: ice ocean
The oceans would begin freezing from the top down. But here is a twist. Ice floats, and it is a good insulator. Once a thick layer formed, it would act like a blanket over the water beneath. Kept warm from below by the heat of the Earth's interior, {{the deep oceans|THE DEEP OCEANS}} could stay liquid for an extraordinarily long time.

> i: frozen earth | v: snow
Far, far in the future, the air itself would get cold enough to freeze. The oxygen and nitrogen we breathe would fall out of the sky as snow and settle on the ice. [pause] The Earth would end up as a dark, silent ball, wrapped in frozen air.

## Who Survives
mood: mystery
> v: hydrothermal vent | i: hydrothermal vents
Not everything would die. At the bottom of the ocean, there are communities of life that have never needed sunlight. Around {{hydrothermal vents|HYDROTHERMAL VENTS}}, cracks in the sea floor gushing hot, mineral-rich water, microbes live on chemical energy from inside the Earth.

> v: deep sea | i: deep ocean
Tube worms, crabs, strange pale shrimp. They feed on those microbes, in total darkness, kilometres below the surface. Under a lid of ice, warmed by the planet's own heat, they could carry on for millions, perhaps billions of years. [pause] The last life on Earth might look nothing like us.

> i: geothermal power | v: geothermal
And humans? It would be brutal, but not necessarily the end. Places like Iceland already heat homes with energy from underground. Deep shelters powered by geothermal heat or nuclear reactors could keep small groups alive, growing food under artificial light.

> v: submarine | i: nuclear submarine
Nuclear submarines can stay underwater for months, making their own air and water. With enough planning, a few people might survive in sealed habitats for a very long time. [pause] But it would be survival. Not a life anyone would recognise.

## A Planet Adrift
mood: wonder
> i: rogue planet | v: rogue planet
Meanwhile, the whole solar system would be coming apart. Every planet would sail away along the line it was travelling at the moment the Sun's gravity vanished. The comets and asteroids would scatter. The family the Sun had held together for billions of years would simply drift apart.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
But the Earth would not stop. It would carry on around the centre of the Milky Way, as it always has, a dark, frozen world with its Moon still faithfully at its side. A {{rogue planet|ROGUE PLANET}}, one of billions wandering between the stars.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Every so often, over millions of years, it might pass near another star. For a few thousand years that star would grow brighter in the sky, a faint point warming nothing. Then it would fade again behind us. [pause] The nearest stars are so far away that even passing close, they would never bring back the warmth we lost.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
And here is the humbling part. Astronomers think there may be more planets drifting alone in our galaxy than there are stars. Some may once have had suns of their own. Some may even have warm, hidden oceans under their ice. [pause] Our imaginary frozen Earth would be in very good company.

> v: sun | i: sun solar dynamics observatory
Of course, none of this will happen. The Sun cannot vanish. It will shine steadily for billions of years more. [pause] But the next time it rises, remember those eight minutes. Everything you love is running on light that left a star a few minutes ago. And it has never once failed to arrive.
