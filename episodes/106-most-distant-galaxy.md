Light takes time to travel. When you look at the Moon, you see it as it was a little over a second ago. The Sun, eight minutes ago. The nearest stars, years ago. The further away something is, the further back in time you are looking.

> i: hubble deep field | v: deep field zoom
So if you could look far enough, you could see the universe as a baby. Galaxies being born. The first stars switching on. A cosmos only a few hundred million years old.

> i: jades webb | v: webb deep field
In the last few years, the James Webb Space Telescope has been doing exactly that, breaking record after record for the most distant galaxy ever seen. > i: early universe galaxy | v: early universe

## Reading The Redshift
mood: wonder
> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
How do astronomers know how far away a galaxy is? They measure its {{redshift|REDSHIFT}}. As light travels across the expanding universe, its waves are stretched. Blue light becomes red, red becomes infrared. The longer the journey, the more the light is stretched.

> i: galaxy spectrum | v: galaxy zoom
For the most distant galaxies, the stretching is so extreme that light which left them as ultraviolet arrives as infrared. That is why Webb, built to see infrared light, is perfect for this hunt. Hubble, which sees mostly visible light, could only just reach into this era.

> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
The most reliable way to measure redshift is to split a galaxy's light into a spectrum and look for the fingerprints of particular elements, shifted to longer wavelengths. Webb's instrument NIRSpec can do this for very faint objects. [pause] Without a spectrum, a distance is just an educated guess.

> i: webb telescope mirror | v: webb telescope
These galaxies are almost unimaginably faint. To see them at all, Webb's six and a half metre golden mirror must stare at the same patch of sky for many hours, sometimes days, gathering a trickle of photons that have crossed the whole universe. [pause] Many of the most distant galaxies appear as just a few faint pixels.

## Hubble's Record
mood: wonder
> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
For years, the champion was a galaxy called {{GN-z11|GN-z11}}, found by Hubble and announced in 2016. It appeared to shine about four hundred million years after the Big Bang. Hubble had pushed itself to the very limit of its abilities to see it.

> i: gn-z11 | v: galaxy zoom
GN-z11 was surprisingly bright for such an early galaxy. Astronomers wondered whether it was a fluke, a rare monster, or a sign that the early universe was busier than models predicted. [pause] They would have to wait for Webb to find out.

> i: gn-z11 | v: black hole accretion disk
When Webb finally studied GN-z11 in detail, in 2023, it found part of the answer. At the galaxy's heart, there seemed to be a supermassive black hole, around a million and a half times the mass of the Sun, feeding vigorously. [pause] One of the earliest black holes ever seen, already growing just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.

> i: webb launch | v: rocket launch
Webb launched on Christmas Day 2021. Within months of starting science observations in 2022, it was finding candidate galaxies that seemed even further away than GN-z11. Some early claims turned out to be wrong, galaxies at much smaller distances disguised by dust. Others held up. [pause] The race had begun.

## The New Champions
mood: wonder
> i: jades webb | v: webb deep field
In May 2024, the {{JADES|JADES}} team, the JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey, announced a galaxy called JADES-GS-z14-0. Its light left it around two hundred and ninety million years after the Big Bang. It became the most distant galaxy ever confirmed.

> i: early universe galaxy | v: early universe
It was also surprisingly big and bright, with light spread across more than a thousand light-years, suggesting it was made mostly of stars rather than powered by a black hole. And in observations with the ALMA telescope, astronomers detected oxygen in it. [pause] Oxygen is made inside stars. So even this early, generations of stars must already have lived and died.

> i: webb deep field | v: deep field zoom
Then, in 2025, another team announced an even more distant galaxy, called {{MoM-z14|MoM-z14}}, seen about two hundred and eighty million years after the beginning. Again, bright. Again, more developed than expected. [pause] Each new record pushes the dawn of galaxies even earlier.

> i: jades webb | v: webb deep field zoom
And there are candidates further still, galaxies that look as though they might be from even earlier times, waiting for spectra to confirm their distances. Many will turn out to be closer than they seem. Some may break the record again.

## Too Much, Too Soon
mood: mystery
> i: early universe galaxy | v: early universe
The biggest surprise is how many bright galaxies Webb is finding in the first few hundred million years. Before Webb, most models predicted that early galaxies would be small, faint and rare. Instead, there seem to be more bright ones than expected.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
That does not break the Big Bang theory. But it does mean our understanding of how the first galaxies formed needs work. Perhaps stars formed much more efficiently in the early universe. Perhaps the first stars were brighter and more massive than today's. Perhaps young black holes added extra light.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
There was another surprise in 2025. In one very distant galaxy, Webb caught a particular glow from hydrogen that should have been smothered by the thick fog of neutral gas filling the early universe. Its presence suggested that the galaxy had already cleared a bubble of space around itself. [pause] The universe may have started becoming transparent sooner than we thought.

> i: first stars | v: early universe
Somewhere beyond these galaxies lies the true frontier: the very first stars, called Population Three, made only of hydrogen and helium from the Big Bang. No one has ever seen one directly. [pause] Webb may be getting close.

## Looking At Our Origins
mood: wonder
> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
These distant galaxies are not just records. They are baby pictures of galaxies like our own. Our Milky Way was once a small, messy collection of stars, forming in the dark, just like these.

> i: star cluster | v: star cluster
Imagine standing on a planet inside one of these infant galaxies, if any planets had formed yet. The sky would be crowded with young, hot, blue stars, many of them far heavier than our Sun, burning furiously and dying quickly. [pause] A galaxy in its wild childhood, nothing like the calm, ancient Milky Way we know.

> i: webb deep field | v: webb deep field zoom
The light from them has been travelling for more than thirteen and a half billion years. When it set out, there was no Sun, no Earth, no Milky Way as we know it. And because space has been expanding all that time, those galaxies are now more than thirty billion light-years away.

> i: hubble deep field | v: galaxy zoom
There is a limit to how far back we can see with light. Before galaxies, before stars, there was a period when the universe was dark, and before that, a time when it was an opaque, glowing fog. [pause] We are now seeing almost to the edge of the first light. The universe's first galaxies are coming into view, one faint red dot at a time.
