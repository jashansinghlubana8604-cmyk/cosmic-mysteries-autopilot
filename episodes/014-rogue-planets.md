## Worlds Without A Home
mood: wonder
> v: solar system formation | i: protoplanetary disc
Every planet we grew up learning about circles a star. Planets form in the discs of gas and dust that surround newborn stars, and they stay there, held in orbit, for billions of years.

> v: planetary system formation | i: young planetary system
But young planetary systems are violent places. Giant planets form, migrate inward and outward, and pass close to one another. Sometimes, one planet's gravity flings another out like a stone from a sling.

> i: ejected planet | v: planet ejected
Once a planet is thrown out fast enough, it never comes back. It leaves its star forever and drifts into the galaxy, carrying with it whatever it was, rock, ice, ocean, atmosphere, into the cold.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter
Even our own solar system may have lost planets this way. Some computer models of the early solar system work best if there was once a fifth giant planet, thrown out by Jupiter long ago. If so, a lost sibling of Jupiter and Saturn may still be out there, billions of years into its exile.

> v: orion nebula zoom | i: orion nebula
Other rogue planets may never have had a star at all. Inside clouds of gas like the Orion Nebula, small clumps can collapse on their own and form bodies too small to become stars, born alone from the very beginning.

> i: star cluster nebula | v: star forming region
Either way, these worlds wander freely. Their only connection to anything is the gentle gravity of the galaxy itself, which carries them on a vast, slow orbit around the Milky Way, just like the Sun.

## How Do You See Something Dark?
mood: mystery
> v: gravitational lensing | i: gravitational lens
A planet produces almost no light of its own. Without a star nearby to illuminate it, a rogue planet is essentially invisible. So how could anyone find one?

> i: einstein ring | v: gravitational lensing animation
The answer comes from Einstein. Mass bends light. When a rogue planet drifts exactly in front of a distant star, its gravity acts like a tiny magnifying glass, and the star briefly brightens, for a few hours or days, then fades back to normal. Astronomers call this {{microlensing|MICROLENSING}}.

> v: telescope observatory night | i: telescope milky way
The events are extremely rare and impossible to predict. So surveys stare at enormous numbers of stars toward the centre of the galaxy, night after night, waiting for one to flicker in exactly the right way.

> v: star brightening | i: bright star
The shorter the flash, the smaller the object. A star like the Sun might brighten a distant star for weeks. A planet like Jupiter, for a day or two. A world the size of Earth, for just a few hours. Each event happens once, and never repeats.

> i: milky way centre | v: zoom milky way centre
Over the past fifteen years, they have caught dozens of these brief flashes, each one the fingerprint of an unseen world passing silently in front of a star. From how often they happen, astronomers can estimate how many rogue planets there must be. [pause] The numbers are staggering.

> v: star field | i: milky way panorama
Recent estimates suggest that small rogue worlds, around the size of Earth or Mars, may be several times more common than planets bound to stars. If that is right, the Milky Way could hold hundreds of billions, perhaps trillions, of wandering planets.

## The Webb Telescope Surprise
mood: wonder
> i: orion nebula webb | v: orion nebula webb
In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope looked deep into the Orion Nebula, a stellar nursery thirteen hundred light years away, and found hundreds of objects with masses similar to giant planets, glowing faintly in infrared light, with no star of their own.

> i: orion nebula trapezium | v: orion nebula zoom
Young planets are still warm from their formation, which is why Webb could see them. And among them was something nobody expected: dozens of these objects appeared to be orbiting each other in pairs. Two planets, with no star, circling one another in the dark.

> v: binary planets | i: binary planet
Astronomers nicknamed them {{JuMBOs|JUMBOS}}, Jupiter-mass binary objects. They should not exist easily. A pair of planets that gentle is hard to throw out of a star system without being torn apart.

> v: webb telescope | i: james webb space telescope
Webb could see them because it was built to see heat, not visible light. Its golden mirror, more than six metres wide, is cooled far below freezing in the shadow of a giant sunshield, so it can pick up the faint warmth of objects no other telescope can detect.

> i: orion nebula | v: orion nebula
Later studies have questioned whether many of them are truly planet pairs, or more distant objects in the background. The debate is ongoing. But either way, Webb has shown us that worlds without stars are being born, right now, in nebulae we can see from Earth.

## Could Anything Live There?
mood: mystery
> i: frozen planet | v: ice planet
At first, a rogue planet sounds like the most hostile place imaginable. Far from any star, its surface would freeze to just a few dozen degrees above absolute zero.

> v: planet interior | i: planet core
But a planet is not only warmed by sunlight. The decay of radioactive elements deep inside can keep a planet's interior warm for billions of years. Our own Earth still glows with heat left from its formation and from radioactivity.

> i: ocean under ice | v: europa ocean
If a rogue planet held on to a thick blanket of hydrogen, that atmosphere could trap the heat like an enormous duvet. Some scientists have calculated that this might keep liquid water oceans on its surface, under a permanently black sky.

> i: europa | v: europa jupiter moon
Even without that, an ocean could hide beneath a shell of ice, warmed from below, as we believe happens on moons in our own solar system, like Europa and Enceladus. Life there, if it existed, would never know that stars exist.

> i: deep sea vents | v: hydrothermal vent
On Earth, entire ecosystems thrive around volcanic vents at the bottom of the ocean, in total darkness, powered by chemistry rather than sunlight. If life can survive there, then sunlight may not be as essential as we once thought.

> v: planet drifting space | i: planet drifting
A living rogue planet could even wander between star systems over millions of years, carrying its biology across the galaxy like a sealed lifeboat. It is speculation. But it is not impossible.

## Waiting To Be Found
mood: wonder
> v: nancy grace roman telescope | i: roman space telescope
A new telescope is coming. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is designed to watch hundreds of millions of stars toward the galactic centre. Astronomers expect it to discover hundreds of rogue planets, perhaps more, including worlds as small as Mars.

> i: brown dwarf | v: brown dwarf
Some lonely worlds are already close to us. Just over seven light years away is a dark, cold object called {{WISE 0855|WISE 0855}}, only a few times the mass of Jupiter, one of the coldest objects ever found outside the solar system, with clouds that may contain water ice.

> i: oort cloud | v: oort cloud
Finding them matters for more than curiosity. Knowing how many planets are thrown out of their systems tells us how violent planet formation really is, and how unusual our own calm solar system might be.

> v: star field slow | i: dark space stars
And it is entirely possible that the nearest rogue planet to us is even closer, still undiscovered, drifting silently between the Sun and the nearest stars, too dark for any telescope yet to see.

> i: rogue planet | v: rogue planet
Every night, when you look up at the sky, you see thousands of stars. [pause] What you cannot see are the worlds between them, perhaps far more numerous than the stars themselves, wandering forever in the dark.
