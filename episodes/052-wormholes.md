Here is the surprising part. Wormholes did not come from science fiction. They came from Einstein's own equations. [pause] The real question is not whether physics allows them. It is whether nature would ever let one stay open.

## A Bridge In The Equations
mood: mystery
> i: albert einstein | v: spacetime curvature
In 1915, Albert Einstein published general relativity. Its central idea is that gravity is not a force pulling things together. It is the shape of space and time itself, bent by matter and energy. Planets orbit the Sun because they are following the curves it makes.

> i: spacetime curvature | v: spacetime curvature
If space can bend, then in principle it can bend in very strange ways. In fact, only a year after Einstein's theory appeared, an Austrian physicist named Ludwig Flamm noticed that one of its first solutions could be read as a funnel joining two separate sheets of space. Almost nobody paid attention. In 1935, Einstein and his colleague {{Nathan Rosen|NATHAN ROSEN}} found a solution to the equations that connected two separate regions of space through a kind of throat. A bridge.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
It became known as the Einstein-Rosen bridge. In 1957, the physicist John Wheeler gave this kind of structure a catchier name. {{Wormhole|WORMHOLE}}. Like a worm boring through an apple, instead of crawling all the way around its surface.

> i: wormhole | v: wormhole
But there was a catch. The Einstein-Rosen bridge pinches shut almost instantly. It opens and collapses faster than light could cross it. [pause] Anything trying to go through would be crushed in the throat. It was a bridge that nobody could ever walk across.

## What It Would Take
mood: dread
> i: wormhole artist impression | v: wormhole
In 1988, physicist {{Kip Thorne|KIP THORNE}} and his student Michael Morris asked a different question. Forget whether wormholes form naturally. What would it take to build one that a person could actually travel through?

> i: wormhole | v: spacetime curvature
Their answer was startling. You could keep a wormhole open, in theory, but its throat would need to be lined with something very strange. A kind of matter with negative energy, which pushes space apart instead of pulling it together. They called it {{exotic matter|EXOTIC MATTER}}.

> i: casimir effect | v: physics laboratory
Does negative energy exist? Surprisingly, a little. In a famous effect predicted in 1948, two metal plates placed extremely close together in a vacuum are pushed towards each other, because the space between them holds less energy than the space outside. In that thin gap, the energy is, in a sense, below zero.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotation
But the amounts are tiny. Some estimates suggest that holding open a wormhole just a metre wide would need negative energy equivalent to something like the mass of {{Jupiter|JUPITER}}. Nobody knows how to gather that much, or whether it is even possible. [pause] The universe may simply not allow it.

> i: wormhole | v: wormhole
And even if you could hold one open, it might be fragile. Some calculations suggest that the moment anything passed through, the extra mass would start to pinch the throat. Light falling into one mouth could also pile up and grow in energy, building into a deadly flash. [pause] A wormhole might be a door that slams shut on the first person who tries to use it.

## A Time Machine
mood: dread
> i: wormhole | v: wormhole
And then Thorne and his colleagues noticed something even stranger. If you could build a traversable wormhole, you could turn it into a time machine.

> i: spacecraft deep space | v: spacecraft flight
Here is how. Leave one mouth of the wormhole on the Earth. Put the other on a spaceship and fly it away at close to the speed of light, then bring it back. Because of time dilation, less time passes for the travelling mouth. [pause] Now the two ends are connected across time, as well as space. Step through one, and you come out in the past.

> i: clock | v: time lapse
That creates every paradox you have ever heard of. Going back and preventing your own birth. Bringing back information that was never created by anyone. Physicists do not like paradoxes.

> i: stephen hawking | v: black hole
In 1992, Stephen Hawking proposed what he called the {{chronology protection conjecture|CHRONOLOGY PROTECTION}}. The idea is that the laws of physics conspire to stop time machines from ever forming. Perhaps the wormhole would be destroyed by a storm of energy the moment it became a time machine. [pause] He joked that the best evidence for this is that we have not been invaded by tourists from the future.

## Inside The Movies
mood: wonder
> i: wormhole interstellar | v: wormhole
In the film Interstellar, Kip Thorne worked as a science adviser. He insisted that the wormhole be shown the way the equations describe it. Not as a hole, but as a sphere, a crystal ball through which you can see a distorted image of a distant galaxy.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: light bending
Light from the far side would pour through the throat and be bent by its gravity, so the whole sky of another place would be wrapped around the sphere. Flying into it would feel like falling into a reflection. [pause] It is one of the most accurate pictures of an impossible object ever made.

> i: sagittarius a star orbits | v: stars orbiting black hole
Could we spot a real one? Some physicists have suggested ways. A wormhole might bend light in a very particular pattern. And if one existed near the black hole at the centre of our galaxy, the stars orbiting there might feel a tiny extra tug from objects on the other side. [pause] So far, nothing like this has ever been seen.

## Entangled Space
mood: mystery
> i: quantum entanglement | v: quantum
In 2013, physicists Juan Maldacena and Leonard Susskind proposed a bold idea. In quantum physics, two particles can be entangled, linked so that measuring one instantly tells you about the other, no matter how far apart they are. Einstein called it spooky action at a distance.

> i: wormhole | v: wormhole
Maldacena and Susskind suggested that entanglement and wormholes might be two descriptions of the same thing. That every pair of entangled particles might be connected by a microscopic, non-traversable wormhole. They summed it up as {{ER equals EPR|ER = EPR}}, after the two famous papers of 1935.

> i: quantum computer | v: quantum computer
In 2022, a team used a Google quantum computer to simulate the behaviour of a tiny wormhole in a simplified model universe. It made headlines around the world. But it was a simulation, not a real tunnel through space, and some physicists questioned what it really showed.

> i: wormhole artist impression | v: deep space flight
So, could wormholes really exist? The honest answer is: maybe, in the equations. Maybe, at the tiniest scales, woven into the fabric of space itself. But a tunnel big and stable enough for a ship to fly through would need ingredients the universe may never provide. [pause] For now, the stars are as far away as they look. But the mathematics keeps whispering that there might be another way.
