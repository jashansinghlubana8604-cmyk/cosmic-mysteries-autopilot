The Sun has been shining for about four and a half billion years. Steady. Reliable. It has warmed every dinosaur, every forest, every human being who has ever lived.

> i: sun | v: sun
But the Sun is not eternal. It is a machine burning fuel, and the fuel is limited. One day, it will run out. And when it does, the Sun will not simply fade away.

> i: red giant star | v: red giant
It will swell to hundreds of times its current size. It will swallow the inner planets. It will blow its outer layers into space in a glowing shell of gas. [pause] And what is left will be a dead, glowing ember the size of the Earth.

> i: planetary nebula | v: planetary nebula
Astronomers know this story in remarkable detail, because they have watched it happen to other stars all over the galaxy. [pause] So let's watch it happen to ours.

[title]

## A Slow Brightening
mood: mystery
> i: sun | v: sun
Right now, the Sun is in the middle of its life, fusing hydrogen into helium in its core. Every second, it turns about six hundred million tonnes of hydrogen into helium, releasing the energy that lights our sky.

> i: sun core | v: sun interior
As helium builds up in the core, the core slowly shrinks and heats up, and fusion speeds up. So the Sun is gradually getting brighter. About one percent every hundred million years. Too slow for anyone to notice. [pause] But over billions of years, it adds up.

> i: earth from space | v: earth
In around a billion years, the Sun will be about ten percent brighter than today. That may not sound like much. But scientists think it will be enough to start the Earth's oceans evaporating faster than they can rain back down. Water vapour, a powerful greenhouse gas, will trap more heat. And the planet will begin to dry out.

> i: venus | v: venus
In the long run, the Earth may become something like Venus. Hot, dry and lifeless, long before the Sun itself begins to die. [pause] The end of life on Earth is not five billion years away. It may be closer to one.

## The Red Giant
mood: dread
> i: sun | v: sun
In about five billion years, the hydrogen in the Sun's core will run out. Fusion there will stop. Without that outward push, the core will contract and heat up, while hydrogen keeps burning in a shell around it.

> i: red giant star | v: red giant
And the outer layers of the Sun will swell. Slowly at first, then faster, over hundreds of millions of years, until the Sun becomes a {{red giant|RED GIANT}}. Its surface will cool and turn red, but because it will be so enormous, it will be thousands of times brighter than today.

> i: mercury | v: mercury
Its surface will reach out more than a hundred times further than it does now. Mercury will be swallowed first. Then Venus. And the Earth? That is surprisingly hard to answer. [pause] It depends on a race.

> i: earth sun red giant | v: red giant
As the Sun swells, it will also lose a large fraction of its mass, blown away in a strong wind. With less gravity holding them, the planets will drift outward. The Earth's orbit will grow. But tides raised on the giant Sun may drag the Earth back in. Several studies suggest the Earth probably loses the race and is swallowed. [pause] Others say it might just escape, as a scorched cinder skimming the Sun's swollen edge.

## A Brief Spring In The Outer System
mood: wonder
> i: titan | v: titan
As the Sun swells and brightens, the warm zone of the solar system will move outward too. For a while, places that are frozen today will thaw.

> i: europa | v: europa
The icy moons of Jupiter could see their surfaces melt. And Saturn's moon {{Titan|TITAN}}, with its thick atmosphere and its lakes of liquid methane, could warm enough for liquid water to pool on its surface, perhaps for a few hundred million years.

> i: titan surface | v: titan
Some scientists have imagined that Titan, rich in complex carbon chemistry, could become a place where the chemistry of life gets a second chance. [pause] At the end of the Sun's life, the solar system might briefly have a new habitable world, far from where the old one used to be.

> i: kuiper belt | v: kuiper belt
Out in the Kuiper Belt, icy bodies like Pluto would warm up and release thin atmospheres. The whole outer solar system would glow, briefly, in the light of the dying star.

## The Shell Of Light
mood: wonder
> i: red giant star | v: red giant
After its red giant phase, the Sun's core will become hot enough to start fusing helium into carbon and oxygen. The Sun will shrink a little and burn steadily for about a hundred million years more. Then the helium in the core will run out too.

> i: planetary nebula | v: planetary nebula
The Sun will swell once more, and its outer layers will become unstable, pulsing and puffing off into space. Eventually, they will drift away entirely, lit up by the exposed, white-hot core. For a few thousand years, the Sun will be surrounded by a glowing cloud of gas, a {{planetary nebula|PLANETARY NEBULA}}.

> i: helix nebula | v: helix nebula
We can see this happening elsewhere. The Helix Nebula looks like a giant eye in space. The Ring Nebula, a glowing smoke ring. Each is the final breath of a star like ours. Studies suggest the Sun is just heavy enough to make a faint one of its own. [pause] Our star's last act will be to paint the darkness.

> i: ring nebula webb | v: planetary nebula
Inside that shell will be carbon, oxygen and nitrogen, made inside the Sun over billions of years. They will drift out into the galaxy, mix with other clouds, and perhaps one day become part of new stars, new planets, and new life. [pause] The Sun will die. But its atoms will not.

## The Ember
mood: dread
> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
What remains at the centre will be the Sun's exposed core. A {{white dwarf|WHITE DWARF}}. Roughly half the Sun's mass, crushed into a ball about the size of the Earth. A teaspoon of it would weigh several tonnes.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
It will no longer make any energy. It will just glow with leftover heat, at first hotter than a hundred thousand degrees, then slowly cooling, over billions and billions of years. Eventually, over a timescale far longer than the current age of the universe, it will fade to a cold, dark lump: a black dwarf. [pause] The universe is not old enough for a single black dwarf to exist yet.

> i: white dwarf planet | v: exoplanet
And here is a hopeful twist. Astronomers have found planets orbiting white dwarfs, including a giant planet circling one very closely, and in 2024, a planet about the mass of the Earth, orbiting a white dwarf at a distance a bit further than the Earth is from the Sun. Planets can survive their star's death.

> i: sun solar dynamics observatory | v: sun
So if the Earth survives the red giant, it may end up circling a tiny, dim white star, frozen and silent, for longer than the universe has existed. [pause] Five billion years from now. Plenty of time. But every sunrise you see is part of a story with an ending. Enjoy the light while it lasts.
