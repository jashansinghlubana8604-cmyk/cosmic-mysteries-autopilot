## A Group In Pegasus
mood: wonder
> i: pegasus constellation | v: night sky
In 1877, the French astronomer Édouard Stephan was using a new telescope at the Marseille Observatory when he noticed a tight cluster of faint, fuzzy objects in the constellation Pegasus. Five of them, packed together.

> i: stephan's quintet | v: galaxy zoom
At the time, nobody knew that galaxies were separate islands of stars beyond our own. Stephan simply recorded them as nebulae. It would take another half century before astronomers realised that objects like these were entire galaxies, each containing billions of stars.

> i: stephan's quintet | v: galaxy collision
Stephan's Quintet became famous as one of the first known {{compact groups|COMPACT GROUP}} of galaxies, small gangs packed so close together that they are almost bound to interact. In 1982, the astronomer Paul Hickson catalogued a hundred of these tight groups across the sky. Stephan's Quintet is number ninety two on his list. Four of the five are about two hundred and ninety million light-years away. [pause] The light we see from them left before the first dinosaurs walked the Earth.

> i: stephan's quintet webb | v: galaxy zoom
Webb's view of the group was stitched together from observations in near and mid infrared light, revealing details no earlier telescope had seen: hidden star clusters, glowing dust, and the faint background galaxies scattered behind. The finished mosaic covers an area of sky roughly a fifth of the width of the full Moon. [pause] A tiny patch of sky, packed with a story.

## The Impostor
mood: mystery
> i: ngc 7320 | v: galaxy zoom
The fifth galaxy, called {{NGC 7320|NGC 7320}}, looks like part of the group. But its light is not shifted as far towards the red as the others. That means it is much closer to us, about forty million light-years away, just a fraction of the distance.

> i: stephan's quintet webb | v: galaxy collision
In Webb's image, the difference is obvious. NGC 7320 is sharp and detailed, with individual stars and clouds of gas clearly visible. The others look softer, further away, with countless distant galaxies peeking through behind them. [pause] It is a bystander, photobombing a collision happening hundreds of millions of light-years behind it.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
That kind of chance alignment is surprisingly common in astronomy. Objects that look close together on the sky can be separated by enormous distances along our line of sight. [pause] The sky is a flat picture of a very deep universe.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
The impostor once caused a famous argument. In the 1960s and 70s, the astronomer Halton Arp argued that galaxies with very different redshifts, like those in Stephan's Quintet, were really sitting together, and that redshift did not reliably measure distance. If he had been right, much of modern cosmology would have collapsed. [pause] Careful measurements of NGC 7320's individual stars eventually settled it. It really is in the foreground.

## The Crash
mood: dread
> i: galaxy collision | v: galaxy collision
The real action involves the other four. Two of them, called NGC 7318a and NGC 7318b, are crashing into each other right now. And NGC 7318b is plunging through the group at enormous speed, millions of kilometres per hour.

> i: galaxy shock wave | v: galaxy collision
As it ploughs through the gas left behind by earlier encounters, it creates a gigantic {{shock wave|SHOCK WAVE}}, like a sonic boom, but on a cosmic scale. The shock front stretches for tens of thousands of light-years and heats the gas to millions of degrees, making it glow in X-rays.

> i: spitzer telescope | v: galaxy collision
In 2006, NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope revealed just how dramatic this shock is. It found a vast glowing region of hydrogen molecules, larger than the Milky Way, heated by the collision. [pause] One of the largest shock fronts ever seen in the universe, created by a single galaxy barging through a crowd.

> i: stephan's quintet webb | v: galaxy merger
Webb's infrared eyes also saw the shock in the light of warm hydrogen molecules, and showed where it is triggering bursts of new star formation, in glowing clusters strung along the tidal tails. [pause] Destruction and creation, happening in the same place, at the same time.

> i: galaxy merger | v: galaxy merger
Long, faint streams of stars stretch away from the galaxies, flung out by gravity during previous close passes. These tidal tails are like skid marks, recording the history of encounters going back hundreds of millions of years.

## The Black Hole In The Group
mood: mystery
> i: ngc 7319 | v: black hole accretion disk
The galaxy at the top of the group, NGC 7319, has a feeding supermassive black hole at its centre, about twenty four million times the mass of the Sun. It is pulling in gas and releasing energy equivalent to the light of around forty billion Suns.

> i: black hole jet | v: black hole jet
Webb looked deep into its heart and saw the region around the black hole in extraordinary detail. It saw gas being driven outward from the black hole, in a powerful wind. [pause] Studying galaxies like this helps astronomers understand how black holes and galaxies influence each other as they grow.

> i: galaxy collision | v: galaxy collision
Collisions like these funnel gas towards the centres of galaxies, where it can feed black holes and ignite bursts of star formation. Over billions of years, the galaxies of Stephan's Quintet will probably merge into one larger galaxy. [pause] We are watching a moment in a very long story.

## A Star In The Movies
mood: wonder
> i: stephan's quintet | v: galaxy zoom
Stephan's Quintet has a surprising claim to fame. In the 1946 film It's a Wonderful Life, the opening scene shows heaven as a view of the night sky, with galaxies talking to each other. One of the images used for that heavenly conversation was Stephan's Quintet.

> i: hubble deep field | v: galaxy zoom
Generations of viewers have seen it every Christmas without knowing they were looking at a galactic collision. [pause] Gentle angels, chatting in a group of galaxies that are, in reality, tearing each other apart.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
And there is a lesson in it for us. Galaxy collisions are not rare. They are one of the main ways galaxies grow. Our own Milky Way has swallowed smaller galaxies throughout its history, and may one day merge with Andromeda.

> i: stephan's quintet webb | v: galaxy collision
Stephan's Quintet shows us what that looks like. Shock waves, streams of stars, newborn clusters and a hungry black hole, all frozen in a single image by light that left two hundred and ninety million years ago. [pause] A cosmic car crash, still unfolding, as slowly and as beautifully as anything in the universe.
