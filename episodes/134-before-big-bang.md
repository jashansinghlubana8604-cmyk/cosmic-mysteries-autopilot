> i: big bang | v: big bang
About thirteen point eight billion years ago, the universe we see was squeezed into an incredibly hot, dense state. Then it expanded and cooled, forming atoms, stars, galaxies, planets, and eventually us. That much, cosmologists are confident about.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
But the question everyone asks next is the hardest one in all of science. What came before? What was there before the Big Bang? What made it happen?

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The honest answer is that nobody knows. Our best theories break down as we approach the very beginning. But physicists have not given up. They have developed some remarkable ideas. [pause] Some say there was no before. Some say there was another universe, collapsing. Some say the universe has been bouncing forever.

> i: deep field | v: deep field zoom
Let's look at the leading answers to the biggest question of all. [pause] And at the clues that might, one day, tell us which one is right.

## Where Our Theories Break
mood: mystery
> i: albert einstein | v: spacetime curvature
If you run the expansion of the universe backwards using Einstein's general relativity, everything gets closer, hotter and denser, until at one moment the density becomes infinite. Physicists call this a {{singularity|SINGULARITY}}.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
The idea of a beginning is surprisingly recent. In 1927, the Belgian physicist and priest Georges Lemaître proposed that the universe started from what he called a primeval atom, a single dense state that broke apart. Many scientists at the time disliked the idea, partly because it sounded too much like a moment of creation. [pause] The evidence has since shown that something like it really happened.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
But most physicists do not think the universe really began at an infinite point. A singularity is usually a sign that a theory has been pushed beyond its limits. At those extreme densities, quantum effects must become important, and we do not yet have a working theory that combines gravity with quantum physics.

> i: big bang | v: big bang
So when someone asks what came before the Big Bang, the real answer is that our maps run out. [pause] Everything beyond that edge is a careful guess, built from the physics we do understand.

## No Before At All
mood: wonder
> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
One answer is that the question itself may not make sense. Stephen Hawking compared it to asking what is north of the North Pole. You can walk north, and north, until you reach the pole. But there is nothing further north. The direction simply ends.

> i: stephen hawking | v: black hole
In 1983, Hawking and the physicist James Hartle proposed the {{no-boundary proposal|NO-BOUNDARY PROPOSAL}}. In their picture, near the very beginning, time behaves like a direction in space. The universe has no sharp starting edge, no first moment. It is smooth and rounded, like the bottom of a bowl.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
If that is right, then asking what happened before the Big Bang is like asking what is south of the South Pole. There is no before. Strikingly, a version of this idea is very old. Around sixteen hundred years ago, the philosopher Augustine of Hippo argued that time was created along with the world, so asking what came before creation made no sense. [pause] Time itself came into existence with the universe. Other physicists, like Alexander Vilenkin, have explored related ideas in which the universe emerges from nothing through a quantum process.

## The Big Bounce
mood: mystery
> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
Another family of ideas says the Big Bang was not the beginning, but a transition. Perhaps there was a universe before ours, which was contracting, collapsing inward. When it reached an extreme density, quantum effects made it bounce, and it began expanding again. [pause] Our Big Bang would be that bounce.

> i: big bang | v: big bang
Some versions come from a theory called loop quantum gravity, which suggests that space itself is made of tiny indivisible pieces, so it cannot be squeezed to infinite density. The collapse simply stops and reverses.

> i: galaxies | v: galaxy zoom
Other physicists, including Paul Steinhardt and Neil Turok, proposed a cyclic universe. In their picture, our universe goes through endless cycles of expansion and contraction, or repeated collisions between giant membranes in a higher-dimensional space, each producing a new Big Bang. [pause] No beginning. No end. Just cycles.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
The physicist Lee Smolin has suggested an even stranger possibility. Perhaps every black hole gives birth to a new universe on its other side, with slightly different laws of physics. Universes that make lots of black holes would then have lots of offspring, a kind of cosmic natural selection. [pause] In that view, our Big Bang might have been the inside of a black hole in another universe.

## Penrose's Endless Aeons
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The Nobel Prize-winning physicist Roger Penrose has a different idea, called {{conformal cyclic cosmology|CONFORMAL CYCLIC COSMOLOGY}}. Penrose shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for showing that black holes are a natural consequence of Einstein's theory. His ideas about the beginning of the universe are far more controversial. In the far future, after all the stars have died and all the black holes have evaporated, the universe will contain only light and massless particles.

> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
Penrose argues that, in such a universe, there is no way to measure size or time. The enormously large future becomes indistinguishable from an incredibly small, dense beginning. And so the end of one universe becomes the Big Bang of the next. He calls each cycle an aeon.

> i: cmb map | v: cosmic microwave background
Penrose and his colleagues have claimed to find faint circular patterns and hot spots in the cosmic microwave background that could be traces of events in the previous aeon, such as the final evaporation of black holes. [pause] Most cosmologists are not convinced. They argue the patterns are consistent with random chance.

## Looking For Clues
mood: mystery
> i: cosmic inflation | v: expanding universe
Many cosmologists think the Big Bang was preceded by a period of cosmic inflation, an extraordinarily rapid expansion in the first fraction of a second. If inflation happened, it might have erased most traces of whatever came before. But it should also have left its own fingerprint.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Inflation should have shaken space itself, creating primordial gravitational waves. Those waves would leave a faint swirling pattern in the polarisation of the cosmic microwave background. In 2014, a team using the BICEP2 telescope at the South Pole announced they had found it. [pause] It turned out to be dust in our own galaxy. The search goes on.

> i: satellite orbit | v: satellite orbit
New experiments, including telescopes at the South Pole and in Chile, and a planned Japanese space mission called LiteBIRD, are designed to look for that signal with greater sensitivity. If they find it, it will tell us about the earliest instant we can ever hope to observe.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
So, what came before the Big Bang? Perhaps nothing, because time began there. Perhaps a collapsing universe that bounced. Perhaps endless cycles, aeon after aeon. [pause] We do not know. But for the first time in history, these are questions that science, not just philosophy, can start to answer. The beginning of everything may still have left a message in the sky.
