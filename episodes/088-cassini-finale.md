## The Long Road To Saturn
mood: wonder
> i: cassini launch | v: rocket launch
Cassini launched on the fifteenth of October 1997, on top of a powerful Titan rocket from Florida. It was a joint mission of NASA, the European Space Agency and the Italian Space Agency, and it carried a passenger: a European probe called {{Huygens|HUYGENS}}, designed to land on Saturn's giant moon Titan.

> i: cassini spacecraft | v: spacecraft
At Saturn, sunlight is about a hundred times weaker than at the Earth, far too faint for solar panels of a practical size. So Cassini was powered by the heat of decaying plutonium, in generators that would keep working for decades. The launch drew protests from people worried about an accident. [pause] It went perfectly.

> i: cassini spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
Saturn is so far away that Cassini could not fly there directly. It swung past Venus twice, the Earth once and Jupiter once, stealing a little speed from each planet's gravity. The journey took almost seven years.

> i: cassini saturn | v: saturn
On the first of July 2004, Cassini fired its main engine and slipped into orbit around Saturn, passing through a gap in the rings to do it. It was the first spacecraft ever to orbit the ringed planet. [pause] It would stay for thirteen years.

## Landing On Titan
mood: mystery
> i: titan | v: titan
Titan is wrapped in a thick orange haze, hiding its surface from view. Nobody knew what lay beneath. Solid ground? Oceans? Some scientists even wondered if the whole moon was covered in a sea of liquid methane.

> i: huygens probe | v: titan descent
On the fourteenth of January 2005, the Huygens probe parachuted down through Titan's atmosphere for about two and a half hours, photographing the ground as it came into view. River channels. Hills. Shorelines. Then it landed, with a thud, on a surface the consistency of damp sand.

> i: titan surface huygens | v: titan surface
It sent back a picture of the ground around it. Rounded pebbles, probably made of water ice, lying on a flat plain, like a dry riverbed. [pause] It is still the most distant landing ever made on another world.

> i: titan lakes | v: titan
Cassini went on to map Titan with radar on dozens of flybys. It found lakes and seas of liquid methane and ethane near the poles, some bigger than the Great Lakes of North America. Titan is the only other place in the solar system known to have stable liquid on its surface. [pause] And beneath its ice, Cassini found evidence of an ocean of water.

## The Breathing Moon
mood: mystery
> i: enceladus | v: enceladus
Cassini's biggest surprise came from a tiny moon called {{Enceladus|ENCELADUS}}, only about five hundred kilometres across. In 2005, the spacecraft found jets of water vapour and ice shooting out of cracks near its south pole.

> i: enceladus plumes | v: enceladus plumes
Over the next decade, Cassini flew through those plumes again and again, tasting them. It found salt, organic molecules and molecular hydrogen, signs of a warm, salty ocean beneath the ice, where hot water may be reacting with rock on the sea floor. [pause] A small, frozen moon had suddenly become one of the best places to look for alien life.

> i: saturn hexagon | v: saturn
Cassini also studied Saturn itself. It watched a giant hexagon-shaped jet stream circling the north pole, each side wider than the Earth. It saw a colossal storm erupt and wrap around the whole planet in 2010 and 2011. It caught lightning, auroras and seasons changing over more than a decade.

> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
And it studied the rings in extraordinary detail, finding waves, spokes, propeller-shaped disturbances made by tiny hidden moonlets, and vertical structures towering kilometres above the ring plane. [pause] The rings turned out to be one of the most dynamic places in the solar system.

> i: iapetus | v: saturn moons
And Cassini revealed strange moons wherever it looked. Iapetus, with one side as dark as coal and the other as bright as snow, and a ridge of mountains running around its equator like a seam. Hyperion, porous and full of holes, like a giant sponge. Pan and Atlas, shaped like ravioli. [pause] Every flyby seemed to deliver a new surprise.

## The Day The Earth Smiled
mood: wonder
> i: day the earth smiled | v: saturn
On the nineteenth of July 2013, Cassini turned its camera back towards the inner solar system, while Saturn blocked the glare of the Sun. People on Earth had been told in advance, and invited to look up and wave at Saturn at the exact moment.

> i: day the earth smiled | v: saturn rings
The resulting image showed Saturn glowing from behind, its rings lit up like a halo. And far below them, a tiny blue dot. The Earth, one and a half billion kilometres away, with everyone on it. [pause] It became known as {{The Day the Earth Smiled|THE DAY THE EARTH SMILED}}.

> i: pale blue dot | v: earth from space
It echoed Voyager's famous Pale Blue Dot, taken more than twenty years earlier. Two spacecraft, decades apart, looking back from the outer solar system and showing us how small, and how precious, our home really is.

## The Grand Finale
mood: dread
> i: cassini grand finale | v: cassini saturn
By 2017, Cassini was running out of fuel. Without fuel, it could not steer. And if it were left to drift, it might one day crash into Enceladus or Titan, contaminating them with microbes from the Earth that might have survived the journey.

> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
So mission planners designed a daring ending. Starting in April 2017, Cassini would make {{22 dives|22 DIVES}} through the narrow, unexplored gap between Saturn and its innermost ring, a gap about two thousand kilometres wide. No spacecraft had ever gone there.

> i: cassini grand finale | v: saturn
On each dive, Cassini used its big dish antenna as a shield against ring particles. It measured Saturn's gravity and magnetic field, and weighed the rings. The data suggested the rings are lighter than many expected, which some scientists think means they may be relatively young.

> i: saturn | v: saturn
Then, on the fifteenth of September 2017, it made its final plunge. It entered Saturn's atmosphere, firing its thrusters to keep its antenna pointed home, sampling the air until the last possible moment. About a minute after entering the atmosphere, its signal was lost. But Saturn was so far away that the final signal took around eighty three minutes to reach the Earth. By the time the engineers watched it fade, Cassini had already been gone for well over an hour. [pause] Cassini became part of Saturn.

> i: cassini spacecraft | v: cassini saturn
Twenty years in space. Hundreds of thousands of images. Thousands of scientific papers. A landing on Titan. An ocean on Enceladus. [pause] And a final act of care for the worlds it discovered. Cassini did not just explore Saturn. It protected it.
