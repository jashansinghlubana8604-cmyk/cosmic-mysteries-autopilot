Mars has two moons. They are nothing like ours. Small, dark and lumpy, shaped more like potatoes than spheres. The bigger one, Phobos, is only about twenty seven kilometres long. You could walk across it in a day.

> i: phobos mars moon | v: mars orbit
It orbits closer to its planet than any other moon in the solar system orbits its own. Just six thousand kilometres above the Martian surface. So close that it races around Mars three times every Martian day.

> i: phobos | v: phobos
And it is doomed. Every century, Phobos drops a couple of metres closer to Mars. Its surface is scarred with long grooves that may be the first signs of it being pulled apart. [pause] In a few tens of millions of years, it will either crash into Mars, or be shredded into a ring.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
This is the story of a moon that is falling. [pause] And the mission that is going to fetch a piece of it before it is gone.

## Fear And Panic
mood: mystery
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
In August 1877, the American astronomer Asaph Hall was searching for moons around Mars with the United States Naval Observatory's great telescope in Washington. Many astronomers believed Mars had none. Hall was about to give up when his wife, Angeline, urged him to keep looking.

> i: mars moons | v: mars orbit
Within days, he found two. He named them after the sons of Ares, the Greek god of war: {{Phobos and Deimos|PHOBOS & DEIMOS}}. Fear and Panic. Fitting companions for the red planet named after the god of war.

> i: phobos | v: phobos
Phobos turned out to be strange in almost every way. It is small, dark and lumpy. It is not dense enough to be solid rock. Studies suggest it may be a loose pile of rubble, held together by its own weak gravity, with a lot of empty space inside.

> i: phobos | v: phobos rotation
In the 1950s, measurements of its orbit led a Soviet astrophysicist, Iosif Shklovsky, to wonder whether Phobos might be hollow. Some even took that to mean it was artificial. [pause] Later, more accurate measurements showed the idea was based on faulty data. Phobos is not a spaceship. But it really is quite empty inside.

## A Moon In A Hurry
mood: wonder
> i: phobos mars moon | v: mars orbit
Phobos orbits Mars once every seven and a half hours, faster than Mars itself turns. So from the surface of Mars, something remarkable happens. Phobos rises in the {{west|RISES IN THE WEST}}, crosses the sky in about four and a half hours, and sets in the east. Then, a few hours later, it rises again.

> i: phobos eclipse | v: solar eclipse
Rovers on Mars have watched it pass in front of the Sun. Because it is so small and irregular, it never covers the Sun completely. Instead, it looks like a dark potato sliding across the Sun's disc, in an eclipse that lasts less than a minute. [pause] The strangest solar eclipses in the solar system.

> i: phobos mars moon | v: night sky
Standing on Mars near the equator, Phobos would look like a small, dim, irregular moon, about a third as wide as our full Moon appears from Earth. It would race visibly against the stars, crossing the sky in a few hours, changing phase as it went. [pause] And from near the poles, you would never see it at all. It orbits so low that the curve of Mars hides it.

> i: deimos | v: mars moons
Its sibling, Deimos, is even smaller, about twelve kilometres across, and orbits much further out. From the surface of Mars, it looks like a bright star, barely bigger than a point. Deimos is slowly drifting away. Phobos is slowly falling in.

## Stretch Marks
mood: dread
> i: phobos grooves | v: phobos
Phobos's surface is covered in long, straight grooves, up to tens of metres deep and hundreds of metres wide, running across much of the moon. They have puzzled scientists since the first spacecraft photographed them in the 1970s.

> i: stickney crater | v: phobos
At one end of the moon sits a huge crater called {{Stickney|STICKNEY}}, about nine kilometres wide. That is a third of the width of Phobos itself. Whatever made it nearly destroyed the moon. For a long time, scientists thought the grooves were cracks from that impact.

> i: phobos grooves | v: phobos rotation
But in 2015, NASA scientists proposed a more ominous explanation. Phobos is so close to Mars that the planet's gravity pulls harder on the near side than on the far side. That difference stretches the moon. [pause] The grooves may be stretch marks. The first signs of Phobos being slowly pulled apart.

> i: phobos | v: phobos
Some scientists still argue the grooves have other causes, perhaps debris from the Stickney impact rolling and bouncing across the surface. The debate is not settled. [pause] But either way, the fate of Phobos is not in doubt.

## The End Of Phobos
mood: dread
> i: mars orbit | v: mars orbit
Phobos orbits faster than Mars spins. So the tidal bulge it raises on Mars lags behind it, and pulls it backwards, slowly draining its orbital energy. Phobos is spiralling inward, by around {{two metres every century|2 M / CENTURY}}.

> i: phobos | v: phobos
That sounds slow. But Phobos is already very close. In roughly thirty to fifty million years, it will reach a point where Mars's tides are stronger than the forces holding it together. Then it will break apart.

> i: mars ring | v: saturn rings
Some models suggest the pieces would spread into a ring around Mars, which could last for millions of years before slowly raining down onto the surface. For a while, Mars could be a ringed planet, like Saturn. [pause] Other models suggest the largest chunks might simply crash into the ground.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
And here is an intriguing twist. Some scientists think this may have happened before. Mars might have had earlier moons that broke into rings, rained down, and then gathered back together into new, smaller moons. Phobos itself might be the latest in a long cycle. [pause] A moon reborn from the wreckage of older moons.

## Fetching A Piece Of Fear
mood: wonder
> i: phobos | v: spacecraft
Where did Phobos come from? One idea is that it is a captured asteroid. Another is that it formed from debris thrown up when a huge object slammed into Mars long ago, the same way our own Moon formed from a giant impact on the Earth.

> i: spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
Several missions have tried to find out and failed. Two Soviet probes, both named Phobos, were lost in 1988 and 1989. A Russian mission called Phobos-Grunt, launched in 2011, never left Earth orbit. [pause] Phobos has a reputation as a hard target.

> i: mmx spacecraft | v: spacecraft
Japan is now preparing the Martian Moons eXploration mission, known as {{MMX|MMX}}. Its plan is to travel to Mars, study both moons up close, land on Phobos, collect samples of its surface, and bring them back to the Earth.

> i: rover | v: lunar rover
It will also carry a small rover, built by the French and German space agencies, designed to drive across Phobos's surface in gravity so weak that a single jump could leave you floating above the ground for minutes. [pause] The first wheeled vehicle ever to explore a moon of Mars.

> i: phobos mars moon | v: mars orbit
If it succeeds, we will hold pieces of a moon that is falling to its doom, and finally learn where it came from. [pause] A tiny, battered moon, racing across the Martian sky three times a day, slowly spiralling down. One day, Mars will lose its Fear. And gain a ring.
