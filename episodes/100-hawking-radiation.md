For decades after they were first imagined, black holes were the ultimate prisons. Anything that crossed the edge, the event horizon, could never come back out. Not matter. Not light. Nothing. And black holes would last forever, only ever growing.

> i: stephen hawking | v: black hole
Then, in 1974, a young physicist in Cambridge, already confined to a wheelchair by motor neurone disease, published a result so surprising that even he did not believe it at first.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
Black holes are not completely black. They glow. Very, very faintly. They give off radiation, lose energy, and slowly shrink. [pause] And in the end, every black hole in the universe will disappear in a final burst of light.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
The physicist was Stephen Hawking. The glow is called {{Hawking radiation|HAWKING RADIATION}}. [pause] And it may be the most important clue we have to how the universe really works.

## A Black Hole With A Temperature
mood: mystery
> i: black hole | v: black hole
The story begins with an argument. In the early 1970s, a young physicist named Jacob Bekenstein suggested that black holes must have entropy, a measure of disorder, and that it should be proportional to the area of their event horizons.

> i: stephen hawking | v: black hole
Hawking thought this was nonsense. If a black hole had entropy, it would have to have a temperature. And anything with a temperature must glow, the way a hot coal glows. But black holes, by definition, cannot emit anything. [pause] So Hawking set out to prove Bekenstein wrong.

> i: quantum vacuum | v: quantum
He combined Einstein's theory of gravity with quantum physics, the rules that govern the very small. And to his astonishment, the calculation showed that Bekenstein was right. Near the event horizon, quantum effects cause a black hole to emit a faint, steady stream of radiation. [pause] Black holes have a temperature after all.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
A popular way to picture it goes like this. Empty space is not truly empty. Pairs of particles constantly flicker into existence and vanish again. Near the edge of a black hole, one of a pair can fall in while the other escapes. The escaping particle is the radiation. [pause] It is a simplified picture, but it captures the strange idea at the heart of it.

## The Coldest Things In The Universe
mood: wonder
> i: black hole | v: black hole
Here is the twist. The bigger the black hole, the colder it is. A black hole with the mass of the Sun would have a temperature of around sixty billionths of a degree above absolute zero. Far colder than anything in nature.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
That is so cold that it is actually colder than the faint afterglow of the Big Bang, which fills all of space at about two point seven degrees above absolute zero. So today, real black holes absorb more energy from that background glow than they give off. [pause] For now, they are still growing.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
To glow as hot as the surface of the Sun, a black hole would have to be tiny. About as heavy as a large asteroid, but squeezed into a space smaller than a virus. [pause] The smaller the black hole, the fiercer it shines. A strange reversal of everything we expect.

> i: supermassive black hole | v: black hole accretion disk
But the universe is expanding and cooling. In the far future, the background glow will become colder than even the biggest black holes. Then they will finally start to lose more than they gain. [pause] And slowly, inevitably, they will begin to evaporate.

## How Long Does It Take?
mood: dread
> i: black hole | v: black hole
The answer is almost impossible to imagine. A black hole with the mass of the Sun would take around {{10⁶⁷ years|10⁶⁷ YEARS}} to evaporate. A one followed by sixty seven zeros. The universe is only about thirteen point eight billion years old, a one followed by ten zeros.

> i: supermassive black hole | v: black hole
Supermassive black holes, like the ones at the centres of galaxies, would take even longer, around ten to the power of one hundred years. Long after every star has died. Long after every planet has frozen. Long after the last galaxy has gone dark.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Physicists even have a name for that far-future era. Once the last stars have burned out, the universe will enter what some call the Black Hole Era, when black holes are the only significant objects left, slowly evaporating one by one in an endless night. [pause] It will last unimaginably longer than everything that came before it.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
As a black hole shrinks, it gets hotter, and evaporates faster. In its final moments, it would blaze with radiation, releasing its last energy in a sudden, brilliant flash. [pause] In the very distant future, the last lights in the universe may be black holes, popping one by one in the dark.

## Tiny Black Holes Exploding Now?
mood: mystery
> i: primordial black holes | v: early universe
Is any black hole evaporating today? Possibly. Some theories suggest that the extreme conditions of the early universe could have created small black holes, called {{primordial black holes|PRIMORDIAL BLACK HOLES}}, much lighter than any star.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
A primordial black hole with the mass of a large mountain, formed in the Big Bang, would be finishing its evaporation right about now. In its final moments, it would release a burst of high-energy gamma rays. Astronomers have searched for such bursts with telescopes like NASA's Fermi satellite.

> i: particle accelerator | v: particle accelerator
Hawking radiation also answered a famous fear. When the Large Hadron Collider was switched on, some people worried it might create tiny black holes that could swallow the Earth. Physicists pointed out that cosmic rays have been smashing into the Earth, and the Moon, with even greater energies for billions of years. [pause] If tiny black holes were made, Hawking radiation would make them evaporate almost instantly.

> i: gamma ray telescope | v: satellite orbit
So far, nothing has been found. That does not mean primordial black holes do not exist, only that the evaporating ones must be rare. [pause] If one were ever caught, it would be direct proof of Hawking radiation, and almost certainly a Nobel Prize.

## Echoes In The Lab
mood: wonder
> i: physics laboratory | v: physics laboratory
Real black holes are far too cold and far away for us to measure their glow. So physicists have tried something clever. They create analogues of black holes in the lab. Not real ones, but systems that behave in similar ways.

> i: physics laboratory | v: laser
In one experiment, the physicist Jeff Steinhauer and his colleagues in Israel made a flowing cloud of ultracold atoms, where sound waves could become trapped, like light at a black hole's edge. In 2016 and 2019, they reported signs of a sound version of Hawking radiation escaping from it. [pause] Not proof that real black holes evaporate, but a powerful hint that the mathematics is right.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
Hawking's discovery also created one of the greatest puzzles in physics. If a black hole evaporates, what happens to everything that fell into it? Is all that information destroyed, which quantum physics forbids? Or is it somehow encoded in the radiation? [pause] Physicists have been arguing about this for half a century.

> i: westminster abbey | v: church
Stephen Hawking died in 2018. His ashes were placed in Westminster Abbey in London, near Isaac Newton and Charles Darwin. His memorial stone carries a single equation: the formula for the temperature of a black hole. [pause] The discovery that even the darkest things in the universe glow, and that nothing, not even a black hole, lasts forever.
