## The Star Nobody Was Watching
mood: wonder
> v: kepler telescope | i: kepler telescope artist impression
The Kepler Space Telescope launched in 2009 with one purpose: to find out how common planets are. It pointed at a single field of stars in the constellations Cygnus and Lyra, and measured their brightness with astonishing precision.

> i: exoplanet transit | v: exoplanet transit animation
When a planet passes in front of its star, the star dims very slightly, for a few hours, and then returns to normal. When the same dip repeats, again and again, at exact intervals, you have found a planet. Kepler found thousands this way.

> v: star field | i: star cluster
But a hundred and fifty thousand stars produce a mountain of data. Computers handled most of it, searching for those neat, repeating dips. Anything strange or irregular could slip through. So astronomers invited the public to help, in a project called Planet Hunters, where volunteers looked at the light curves of stars with their own eyes.

> i: star field cygnus | v: zoom to star
In 2011, some of those volunteers flagged a star with a catalogue name, {{KIC 8462852|KIC 8462852}}. They described its light curve with words like bizarre and interesting. The star itself was not special. It was a little larger and hotter than our Sun, about 1,500 light years away. But its light was doing something extraordinary.

> v: star dimming | i: star with debris
Most of the time, it shone steadily. Then, without warning, it would fade. Sometimes by a few percent. Once by fifteen. Once by twenty-two. The dips lasted days, sometimes weeks, and they were jagged and lopsided, as if a collection of objects of different shapes and sizes was passing in front of it.

## Where's The Flux?
mood: mystery
> i: telescope observatory night | v: telescope dome opening
The astronomer who led the investigation was Tabetha Boyajian, and the star soon became known by her nickname, {{Tabby's Star|TABBY'S STAR}}. In 2015, her team published their analysis. Its title asked a simple question. Where's the flux? In other words, where is the light going?

> v: planet formation disc | i: protoplanetary disc
The obvious answer was dust. Young stars are often surrounded by discs of gas and dust that can block their light. But Tabby's Star is not young. And a dusty disc around a star glows with warm infrared light. When astronomers looked, they found no such glow.

> v: planet collision | i: planetary collision
Perhaps two planets had collided, filling the system with debris? That would also produce warm dust, and infrared light. Again, nothing. Whatever was blocking the light was not behaving like any dust cloud anyone had studied.

> v: comet swarm | i: comet
The team's favourite explanation was a family of comets, broken fragments of a large comet on a long orbit, passing in front of the star in a swarm. It could explain the irregular shapes. But it needed a very large number of comets, very precisely arranged. [pause] The paper ended with the problem unsolved.

## The Megastructure
mood: dread
> i: dyson sphere | v: dyson sphere animation
Then another astronomer, Jason Wright of Penn State University, raised a possibility that would put Tabby's Star on the front pages of newspapers around the world.

> v: alien megastructure | i: dyson swarm
In 1960, the physicist Freeman Dyson had imagined that an advanced civilisation might eventually need more energy than any planet can supply. Its solution would be to surround its star with enormous solar collectors, a swarm of structures harvesting starlight.

> i: dyson swarm | v: star dimming
From far away, a swarm like that would block starlight in an irregular, unpredictable way. Exactly like Tabby's Star. Wright was careful to say this was almost certainly not the answer. But it was one of the few ideas that fit the strange shape of the dips. [pause] The phrase {{alien megastructure|ALIEN MEGASTRUCTURE?}} was born.

> v: radio telescope array | i: allen telescope array
Scientists did what they could to test it. The Allen Telescope Array in California listened for radio signals from the star. Other teams searched for flashes of laser light. If something out there was talking, it was not talking to us. Every search came back empty.

## A Century Of Fading
mood: mystery
> i: photographic plate archive | v: old telescope
Then the mystery deepened. An astronomer named Bradley Schaefer went looking for the star in old photographs of the sky, taken on glass plates at Harvard over more than a century.

> v: star dimming slow | i: star fading
He reported that Tabby's Star appeared to have dimmed steadily since the late 1800s, by around fourteen percent. That result was debated, because old plates are tricky to measure. But then astronomers went back to Kepler's own data, and found that during its four years of observation, the star had also faded slowly, by about three percent, on top of the sudden dips.

> v: star field | i: star field cygnus
For a star like this, that is very strange. Stars of its type are supposed to stay remarkably stable for billions of years. Now it seemed this one was not only flickering, but slowly dimming over decades. Something enormous seemed to be gathering in front of it.

> i: comet tail | v: comet
That was difficult for the comet idea. A swarm of comets might explain a few dips. It struggles to explain a century of fading. [pause] Every new observation seemed to make the star harder to understand, not easier.

## The Colour Of The Shadow
mood: wonder
> v: telescope observatory | i: las cumbres observatory
In 2016, Boyajian and her colleagues did something unusual. They raised more than a hundred thousand dollars from the public on Kickstarter to buy telescope time, so that a global network of telescopes could watch the star every night, waiting for the next dip.

> v: star dimming | i: star with dust
In May 2017, it came. The star began to fade, and astronomers around the world rushed to observe it in different colours of light. Over the next year, there were four major dips. The people who had funded the project got to name them. The first was called Elsie.

> i: dust disc star | v: dust around star
The crucial measurement was colour. A solid object, like a giant panel or a planet, blocks all colours of light equally. But fine dust is different. It blocks blue light more strongly than red. And that is exactly what the telescopes saw. [pause] The dips were deeper in blue light.

> v: dust disc star | i: debris disc
The shadow was not a wall of metal. It was a cloud of extremely fine dust, made of particles smaller than a micron. That result, published in 2018, effectively ruled out an opaque megastructure. Tabby's Star was not hiding a Dyson swarm.

## Still Unexplained
mood: mystery
> v: moon destruction | i: exomoon
But knowing that it is dust does not tell us where that dust comes from, or why it appears in such strange patterns. And that question is still open.

> v: planet destroyed | i: planet disintegrating
One idea is that the star swallowed a planet, perhaps thousands of years ago, and is now slowly settling down, while the wreckage drifts in its orbit. Another suggests that a moon was torn away from its planet, pulled close to the star, and is slowly evaporating into a long trail of dust.

> i: comet swarm | v: comet breakup
Others still favour shattered comets, or clouds of debris from collisions we cannot see. None of these explanations is complete. Each one fits some of the observations, and struggles with others.

> v: star field zoom | i: star field
And Tabby's Star may not be alone. Astronomers have since found other stars that dim in strange, irregular ways, suggesting that whatever is happening there might be a rare stage in the life of a planetary system, one we are only now learning to see.

> v: dyson sphere | i: star with debris
So the aliens are almost certainly gone from this story. But the mystery is not. Somewhere, 1,500 light years away, a perfectly ordinary star is being veiled, again and again, by something we still cannot name. [pause] And it is still happening.
