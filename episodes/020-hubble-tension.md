Every galaxy you can see is moving away from us. The further away it is, the faster it is going. The universe is expanding, and it has been ever since the Big Bang.

> v: expanding universe | i: galaxies moving apart
How fast? It sounds like the simplest question in cosmology. It is the number that tells us the age of the universe, its size, and its fate.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
There are two ways to measure it. One looks at the oldest light in the universe. The other looks at stars and galaxies around us. Both have been checked, and checked again, by the best telescopes ever built.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster zoom
And they do not agree. [pause] Something about our understanding of the universe may be {{fundamentally wrong|SOMETHING IS WRONG}}.

## The Day The Universe Got Bigger
mood: wonder
> i: andromeda galaxy | v: andromeda galaxy zoom
A hundred years ago, many astronomers believed the Milky Way was the entire universe. The faint spiral smudges in their telescopes, they thought, were clouds of gas inside our own galaxy.

> i: variable star | v: pulsating star
The key that unlocked the truth came from a woman named Henrietta Leavitt, working at Harvard in the early 1900s. She studied a type of star called a {{Cepheid|CEPHEID}}, which pulses steadily brighter and dimmer. She discovered that the slower a Cepheid pulses, the brighter it truly is.

> v: zoom to galaxy | i: spiral galaxy
That made Cepheids cosmic measuring sticks. If you know how bright a star really is, and you measure how bright it looks, you can work out how far away it must be. In the 1920s, Edwin Hubble used Cepheids to show that the Andromeda nebula was far beyond the Milky Way. [pause] It was another galaxy. The universe had just become unimaginably larger.

> v: expanding universe | i: redshift galaxies
Then Hubble noticed something even stranger. Almost every galaxy was moving away from us, and the further away they were, the faster they receded. Space itself was stretching. The rate of that stretching became known as the {{Hubble constant|HUBBLE CONSTANT}}.

> v: accelerating universe | i: supernova distant galaxy
In 1998, two teams measuring distant supernovae made an even bigger discovery. The expansion of the universe is not slowing down, as everyone expected. It is speeding up. Something is pushing space apart, harder and harder. We still do not know what it is. We simply call it dark energy, and it makes up around two thirds of everything in the cosmos.

## The Number From The Beginning
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic microwave background | v: planck satellite
The first way to measure it today starts at the very beginning. About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled enough for light to travel freely for the first time. That light is still all around us, stretched into microwaves. We call it the cosmic microwave background.

> v: cosmic microwave background | i: planck map
The European Space Agency's Planck satellite mapped it in extraordinary detail. Hidden in its tiny ripples is a precise record of what the young universe was made of and how it behaved. Feed that into our best model of the cosmos, and it predicts how fast the universe should be expanding today.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy flight
The answer is about {{67 kilometres per second per megaparsec|67}}. In plain language: for every three and a quarter million light years of distance, galaxies move away about sixty seven kilometres per second faster.

> v: cosmic web simulation | i: dark matter simulation
That prediction comes from the standard model of cosmology, which describes a universe made mostly of dark energy and dark matter, with ordinary matter as a small minority. It has been astonishingly successful at explaining almost everything we observe. [pause] Almost.

## The Number From The Stars
mood: mystery
> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova
The second method builds a ladder, rung by rung, out into the universe. Nearby, astronomers measure distances to stars directly by watching them shift slightly as the Earth orbits the Sun. Those distances calibrate Cepheids. And Cepheids, in turn, calibrate the brightest measuring sticks of all: a type of exploding star called a Type Ia supernova.

> i: supernova galaxy | v: supernova in galaxy
These explosions all reach nearly the same peak brightness, and they can be seen across billions of light years. Measure enough of them, and you can measure the expansion of the universe directly, in our own cosmic neighbourhood.

> i: spiral galaxy hubble | v: zoom spiral galaxy
A team led by Nobel laureate Adam Riess has spent years refining this ladder with the Hubble Space Telescope. Their answer is about {{73|73}}. [pause] Not sixty seven. Seventy three.

> v: galaxy cluster | i: galaxy cluster
It may not sound like much. But both measurements are now so precise that the gap cannot easily be blamed on chance. The difference is about nine percent, and the odds of it being a statistical fluke are now tiny. Cosmologists have a name for it. [pause] {{The Hubble Tension|THE HUBBLE TENSION}}.

## Checking The Ladder
mood: mystery
> i: james webb space telescope | v: webb telescope
For years, many suspected the stars were the problem. Cepheids in distant galaxies sit in crowded fields, surrounded by other stars and dust. Perhaps their light was being blended with neighbours, making them seem brighter and closer than they really are.

> i: spiral galaxy webb | v: zoom galaxy webb
Then the James Webb Space Telescope arrived. With its sharp infrared vision, it can separate individual stars far more cleanly than Hubble, and see through the dust. When astronomers pointed it at the same Cepheids, the measurements held up. The crowding was not to blame.

> i: red giant stars | v: red giant
Other teams use different stars entirely, such as the brightest old red giants in a galaxy, and some of them find values closer to seventy, in between the two. The debate is lively, and sometimes heated. Each side is checking the other's work with extraordinary care.

> v: gravitational waves | i: neutron star merger
There are even completely new methods. In 2017, astronomers detected gravitational waves from two neutron stars colliding, and used them to measure the expansion rate in an entirely independent way. One day, enough of these cosmic sirens could settle the question by themselves.

> i: galaxy | v: zoom galaxy
The stakes are not just a number. The expansion rate sets the age of the universe. At the slower value, the cosmos is about 13.8 billion years old. At the faster value, if nothing else in our model changed, it would be noticeably younger. Which universe do we actually live in?

## A Crack In The Cosmos
mood: wonder
> v: dark energy | i: expanding universe
So what if both measurements are right? Then the problem is not in the data. It is in our picture of the universe.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: early universe
One idea is that a new kind of energy briefly existed in the early universe, giving the expansion an extra push before fading away. Another is that dark matter or dark energy behaves differently than we assume. Some even suggest we live inside a vast, emptier than average region of space, which would make the nearby universe seem to expand faster.

> v: galaxies moving apart | i: galaxy survey
And there are new hints that dark energy itself may be changing. In 2024 and 2025, a huge survey called DESI, which has mapped millions of galaxies, reported early signs that dark energy might have been stronger in the past and weakening over time. If that holds up, it would overturn one of the central assumptions of modern cosmology.

> i: deep field | v: zoom deep field
None of this is settled. The Hubble Tension may yet turn out to be a subtle error, hidden somewhere in a long chain of measurements. Or it may be the first visible crack in our understanding of the cosmos, the place where new physics finally breaks through.

> v: zoom out universe | i: galaxy cluster
Either way, something remarkable is happening. A hundred years after Hubble discovered that the universe is expanding, we still cannot agree on how fast. [pause] And the answer could change everything we think we know about where the universe came from, and where it is going.
