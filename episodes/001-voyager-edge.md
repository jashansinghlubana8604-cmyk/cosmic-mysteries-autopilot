## A Once In 175 Years Journey
mood: wonder
> v: planets alignment | i: solar system planets
In 1965, a young engineer at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory was plotting the paths of the outer planets when he noticed something strange. In the late 1970s, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune would all swing onto the same side of the Sun.

> v: gravity assist animation | i: gravity assist
That meant a spacecraft could use each giant like a slingshot, stealing a little of its speed and leaping to the next. A journey to Neptune that should take thirty years could be done in twelve. This alignment comes around about once every {{175 years|175 YEARS}}. [pause] The last time, Thomas Jefferson was President of the United States.

> v: rocket launch | i: titan centaur launch
NASA did not waste it. In 1977, two spacecraft lifted off from Florida. Voyager 2 went first, in August. Voyager 1 followed in September, on a faster path that would let it overtake its twin. Each was the size of a small car, with a radio dish almost four metres wide.

> v: jupiter flyby | i: jupiter great red spot
What they sent back rewrote the textbooks. On Jupiter's moon Io, Voyager 1 caught a volcano erupting, the first ever seen anywhere beyond Earth. Saturn's rings turned out to be braided, twisted and full of strange dark spokes. Voyager 2 went on to become the only spacecraft ever to visit Uranus and Neptune.

> v: saturn rings | i: saturn voyager
But Voyager 1 made a sacrifice. To get a close look at Saturn's giant moon Titan, it flew a path that hurled it up and out of the plane of the planets. It would never visit another world. [pause] From that day on, its only destination was the dark between the stars.

## The Last Look Back
mood: wonder
> i: pale blue dot | v: earth from space
In February 1990, six billion kilometres from home, Voyager 1 did something it was never designed to do. The astronomer Carl Sagan persuaded NASA to turn its camera around, one last time, and photograph the planets from the outside.

> i: pale blue dot | v: earth tiny
In one of those frames, the Earth is smaller than a single pixel, a speck caught in a beam of scattered sunlight. Sagan called it the {{Pale Blue Dot|THE PALE BLUE DOT}}. Look at it, and you are looking at everyone you have ever loved. Every war. Every song. Every person who ever lived. All of it, on a mote of dust.

> i: golden record | v: golden record
Both Voyagers also carry a message, in case someone, someday, finds them. It is a gold-plated copper record, with greetings in fifty five languages, the sound of rain, of thunder, of a heartbeat and a kiss, and music from Bach to Chuck Berry.

> i: golden record cover | v: voyager golden record
Its cover shows how to play it, and a map uses fourteen pulsars, spinning dead stars, to point back to our Sun. It is a message in a bottle, thrown into the largest ocean there is. [pause] Minutes after that last photograph, the cameras were switched off to save power. They have never been turned on again.

## The Bubble Around The Sun
mood: mystery
> v: solar wind | i: solar wind
Here is something most people never learn. The Sun does not just shine. It blows. Every second, it fires a stream of charged particles out into space at hundreds of kilometres per second. We call it the {{solar wind|SOLAR WIND}}.

> i: heliosphere | v: heliosphere animation
That wind inflates a gigantic bubble around the whole solar system, the heliosphere. Every planet, every moon, every one of us lives inside it. But the space between the stars is not empty. It is filled with a thin gas of its own, and that gas pushes back.

> v: heliosphere boundary | i: heliopause
So somewhere out there, the Sun's wind must stall, and finally lose. There had to be an edge. But where? Estimates were all over the place, and no telescope on Earth could see it. [pause] There was only one way to find out. Fly there.

> v: voyager deep space | i: voyager spacecraft artist
So the Voyagers kept flying. Year after year. Decade after decade. In December 2004, Voyager 1 crossed the first warning sign, the termination shock, where the solar wind suddenly slams from supersonic to subsonic. Beyond it lay a churning, turbulent zone where the Sun's wind piles up against the galaxy.

> v: deep space flight | i: star field
And then, for eight more years, nothing. The particles kept coming from the Sun. The edge was always just a little further out. Some scientists began to wonder if Voyager would run out of power before it ever got there.

## Crossing The Line
mood: mystery
> v: interstellar space | i: interstellar medium
On the twenty fifth of August 2012, the readings changed. In a matter of days, the particles from the Sun almost vanished. And cosmic rays from distant exploding stars, from the rest of the galaxy, suddenly surged.

> i: heliopause | v: voyager crossing heliopause
Voyager 1 had crossed the {{heliopause|THE HELIOPAUSE}}, the true edge of the Sun's bubble, about 121 times further out than the Earth. For the first time in history, a human-made object was in interstellar space. Voyager 2 followed six years later.

> v: plasma waves | i: interstellar plasma
But the other side was not what anyone expected. The gas just beyond the boundary was around forty times denser than inside. And the region near the edge was hotter than the models said, a thin plasma at tens of thousands of degrees. Scientists started calling it {{a wall of fire|A WALL OF FIRE}}.

> v: magnetic field lines | i: magnetic field space
Then came the part that really did not make sense. Scientists were sure the galaxy's magnetic field would point in a completely different direction from the Sun's. Voyager flew straight through the boundary, and the field barely changed direction at all. [pause] As if the two had been draped together at the edge. The models had been wrong.

> v: sound waves space | i: plasma waves
And in 2021, scientists announced something almost eerie. Voyager's plasma instrument had picked up a faint, steady vibration in the gas of interstellar space. A constant, low background tone, now and then rippled by shock waves from eruptions on our Sun. They called it {{the hum|THE HUM}}. [pause] Interstellar space is not silent. It is humming.

## One Light-Day From Home
mood: wonder
> v: deep space network antenna | i: deep space network
Keeping Voyager alive has become one of the greatest feats of engineering ever attempted. Its power comes from the slow decay of plutonium. Every year it loses about four watts. Today it has less than half the power it launched with.

> i: voyager spacecraft | v: voyager spacecraft
In November 2023, Voyager 1 suddenly began sending back gibberish. A single memory chip had failed. Engineers, some of them younger than the spacecraft itself, dug through documents from the 1970s, rewrote its software, and sent the fix across more than twenty four billion kilometres. Months later, Voyager 1 started making sense again.

> v: deep space flight | i: star field
Then in 2026, after a routine manoeuvre, its power dipped lower than expected. In April, NASA switched off another instrument to keep it alive. Only two of its science instruments are still working. [pause] And it keeps going.

> v: deep space network antenna | i: radio telescope night
On the eighteenth of November 2026, Voyager 1 will reach about twenty six billion kilometres from Earth. A command sent from California will take a full day to arrive. The answer will take another day to come back. {{Two days|2 DAYS}} for a single conversation with the farthest thing we have ever made.

> v: oort cloud | i: oort cloud
And yet, it has barely left home. The Sun's gravity reaches far beyond the bubble, out to a vast shell of icy bodies called the Oort Cloud. Voyager will reach its inner edge in about three hundred years. Crossing it will take tens of thousands more.

> v: milky way galaxy | i: milky way
Within a few years, the power will run out and Voyager will fall silent. But it will not stop. It will drift around the centre of the Milky Way for billions of years, long after the Sun has died, carrying a golden record and a map to a world that may no longer exist.

> i: pale blue dot | v: earth from space
The edge of the solar system was supposed to be a line on a map. Voyager showed us it is alive, shifting, and stranger than we imagined. [pause] And somewhere out there, one light-day from home, the most distant thing we have ever made is still listening. Still sending its quiet signal back from the dark.
