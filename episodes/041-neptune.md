## Found With Mathematics
mood: wonder
> i: uranus | v: uranus
In the early 1800s, astronomers had a problem with Uranus. It was not moving the way it should. Sometimes it ran slightly ahead of where the calculations said it would be. Sometimes slightly behind. As if something invisible was tugging on it.

> i: night sky | v: telescope dome
A French mathematician, {{Urbain Le Verrier|URBAIN LE VERRIER}}, worked out where an unseen planet would have to be to explain it. He sent his prediction to the Berlin Observatory. On the night of the twenty third of September 1846, astronomer Johann Galle pointed his telescope at that spot. [pause] Within an hour, he found it. Less than one degree from where the maths said it would be.

> i: neptune | v: neptune rotation
For the first time in history, a planet had been discovered by calculation. Someone had sat at a desk, worked through pages of equations, and pointed at the sky: there.

> i: jupiter moons | v: jupiter
There is a twist. More than two hundred years earlier, Galileo had already seen it. In his notes from the winter of 1612, while sketching the moons of Jupiter, he drew a faint star nearby. He even noted that it seemed to have moved. [pause] It was Neptune. Galileo had discovered a planet, and never knew it.

> i: solar system | v: solar system
Neptune is about four and a half billion kilometres from the Sun, thirty times further than the Earth. It is so far that one Neptune year lasts {{165 Earth years|165 YEARS}}. It only completed its first full orbit since its discovery in 2011. [pause] Nobody who saw it found has lived to see it come back.

## The Wildest Winds
mood: dread
> i: neptune voyager | v: voyager 2
On the twenty fifth of August 1989, Voyager 2 swept past Neptune, the last stop on its grand tour of the giant planets. It is still the only spacecraft ever to visit.

> i: neptune storm | v: neptune storm
What it saw shocked scientists. Neptune gets about nine hundred times less sunlight than the Earth. It should be a sleepy, frozen world. Instead, it has the strongest winds in the solar system, racing at up to {{2,000 km/h|2,000 KM/H}}. Faster than the speed of sound.

> i: great dark spot | v: neptune storm
And it had a giant storm, a dark oval the size of the Earth, which Voyager's team called the Great Dark Spot. But when Hubble looked again in 1994, it had vanished. Since then, new dark spots have appeared and faded away in just a few years. [pause] On Neptune, even hurricanes the size of planets do not last.

> i: neptune clouds | v: neptune rotation
In 2019, something else happened. Neptune's bright white clouds almost completely disappeared. For a while, the planet looked strangely bare. When scientists compared nearly thirty years of observations, they found the clouds rise and fall in step with the Sun's eleven-year cycle of activity. [pause] A planet four and a half billion kilometres away, still dancing to the rhythm of our star.

> i: neptune | v: neptune rotation
So where does all that energy come from? Neptune gives off more than twice as much heat as it receives from the Sun. Something deep inside is still warm, still churning, and nobody is entirely sure why it is so much hotter than its near-twin, Uranus.

## Diamond Rain
mood: mystery
> i: neptune interior | v: neptune interior
Neptune is called an ice giant. Not because it is made of ice cubes, but because most of it is thought to be a hot, dense fluid of water, ammonia and methane, squeezed under enormous pressure, around a rocky core.

> i: neptune interior | v: diamond
Deep down, the pressure is millions of times greater than at the Earth's surface. And scientists think that, under those conditions, methane can be torn apart. The carbon atoms come loose and cluster together. And they form {{diamonds|DIAMONDS}}.

> i: diamond | v: diamond
In 2017, researchers in California recreated those conditions for a tiny fraction of a second, firing powerful lasers at plastic, and watched minuscule diamonds form. If the same thing happens inside Neptune, the diamonds would sink slowly through the planet. [pause] A rain of diamonds, falling for thousands of kilometres into the dark.

> i: neptune interior | v: neptune interior
Some scientists even think there could be a layer of liquid carbon far below, with diamond icebergs floating in it. That part is much less certain. [pause] But diamond rain inside the ice giants is now taken seriously by planetary scientists.

## The True Colour
mood: mystery
> i: neptune voyager | v: neptune
For thirty five years, the world thought Neptune was a vivid royal blue, much deeper than its pale greenish neighbour, Uranus. Textbooks said so. Posters said so. Every image we saw said so.

> i: neptune uranus | v: uranus
But the Voyager images had been processed to bring out the faint clouds and storms, and that processing pushed the colour towards a deeper blue. In 2024, a team at the University of Oxford carefully recalibrated the data, using measurements from modern telescopes.

> i: neptune true colour | v: neptune
The result: Neptune is {{a pale greenish blue|PALE GREENISH BLUE}}, only very slightly bluer than Uranus. The two planets are almost twins in colour. [pause] The famous blue marble of the outer solar system had been, in a sense, an artist's touch.

> i: neptune webb rings | v: neptune
Webb has revealed something else. In infrared, Neptune's faint, dusty rings glow clearly, along with several of its small moons. Rings that Voyager could only glimpse in a few frames now shine in a single image. [pause] Neptune has been wearing rings all along, too dark for our eyes.

## The Moon Going Backwards
mood: dread
> i: triton | v: triton
And then there is Triton, Neptune's giant moon. It is the only large moon in the solar system that orbits backwards, against its planet's spin. That should not happen to a moon that formed alongside its planet.

> i: triton surface | v: triton
Which suggests it did not. Triton was probably born far away, in the Kuiper Belt, a world like Pluto. At some point, it strayed too close to Neptune and was captured. Its arrival may have wrecked Neptune's original family of moons.

> i: triton geysers | v: triton
Voyager found Triton's surface at around minus two hundred and thirty five degrees, one of the coldest places ever measured in the solar system. And yet, it saw dark plumes rising kilometres high. {{Nitrogen geysers|NITROGEN GEYSERS}}, on a moon colder than almost anywhere else.

> i: neptune triton | v: neptune
Because it orbits backwards, Triton is slowly spiralling inwards. In billions of years, Neptune's gravity will tear it apart. Its pieces may form a ring so vast it could rival Saturn's. [pause] One day, Neptune might be the ringed jewel of the solar system.

> i: neptune | v: neptune rotation
A planet found with a pen. Winds faster than sound. Storms that come and go. Diamonds in the dark. A false colour we believed for decades, and a captured moon falling to its doom. [pause] We visited once, for a few hours. Imagine what we will find when we go back.
