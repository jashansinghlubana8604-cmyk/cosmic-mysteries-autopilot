In early 2015, a spacecraft approaching the largest object in the asteroid belt noticed something odd. On the dark, cratered surface, there was a single point of light. Brighter than anything around it. Shining like a lamp left on in a dark house.

> i: ceres bright spots | v: ceres
As the spacecraft got closer, the light split into several spots, clustered in the bottom of a crater. They were so bright they looked like headlights. People online had theories. Ice. Volcanoes. Even, inevitably, alien cities.

> i: occator crater | v: ceres
The truth turned out to be almost as interesting. Those spots were salt. Left behind by water that had seeped up from deep underground, perhaps very recently. [pause] Water, inside a world most people had never heard of.

> i: ceres | v: ceres rotation
This is Ceres. Once called a planet, then an asteroid, now a dwarf planet. [pause] And it may still have a hidden reservoir of salty water deep below its surface.

## The Lost Planet
mood: wonder
> i: telescope night | v: night sky
On the first night of the nineteenth century, the first of January 1801, the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi was mapping stars from his observatory in Palermo, Sicily. He noticed a faint point of light that moved slightly from one night to the next.

> i: ceres | v: ceres
He tracked it for weeks, until it moved too close to the Sun to see. Then it was lost. Nobody could predict where it would reappear. The young mathematician {{Carl Friedrich Gauss|CARL FRIEDRICH GAUSS}} invented new methods to calculate its orbit from Piazzi's few observations. At the end of 1801, astronomers looked exactly where Gauss said. [pause] It was there.

> i: asteroid belt | v: asteroid belt
Piazzi named it {{Ceres|CERES}}, after the Roman goddess of agriculture. For about half a century, it was counted as a planet. But astronomers kept finding more objects in the same region between Mars and Jupiter. Hundreds. Then thousands.

> i: dwarf planet | v: ceres
So Ceres was reclassified as an asteroid, the largest of all. Then, in 2006, when Pluto was reclassified, Ceres was given a new title. Dwarf planet. [pause] The only one in the inner solar system.

## A Tiny Giant
mood: wonder
> i: ceres | v: ceres rotation
Ceres is about nine hundred and forty kilometres across. Small for a planet, but huge for the asteroid belt. On its own, it contains around a quarter to a third of the entire mass of the belt. It is big enough that its gravity has pulled it into a round shape.

> i: dawn spacecraft | v: dawn spacecraft
In 2007, NASA launched a spacecraft called {{Dawn|DAWN}} to visit the two largest objects in the asteroid belt. It used ion engines, which push a gentle stream of charged gas out the back. The thrust was tiny, about the weight of a sheet of paper resting on your hand. But it never stopped.

> i: vesta | v: vesta
Dawn first orbited the giant asteroid Vesta, then left it and travelled on to Ceres, arriving in March 2015. It became the first spacecraft ever to orbit two different worlds beyond the Earth. [pause] And Ceres turned out to be the stranger of the two.

> i: herschel telescope | v: telescope
Even before Dawn arrived, there had been a clue. In 2014, the European Space Agency's Herschel Space Observatory detected water vapour around Ceres, a few kilograms per second, coming from specific regions on its surface. [pause] Something on this small world was releasing water into space.

## The Lights In Occator
mood: mystery
> i: occator crater | v: ceres
The bright spots sit inside a crater called {{Occator|OCCATOR}}, about ninety kilometres wide. The largest spot, in the centre, is a dome of bright material called Cerealia Facula. Smaller patches are scattered nearby.

> i: ceres bright spots | v: ceres
Dawn's instruments revealed what they are made of. Mostly sodium carbonate, a kind of salt similar to washing soda. On the Earth, deposits like this form when salty water evaporates, leaving the salt behind. Like the white crust that forms around the edge of a drying salt lake.

> i: ceres bright spots | v: ceres
So the spots are the remains of briny water that welled up from below, onto the surface, where it boiled away into space and left the salt behind. And Dawn even found a form of salt that holds water inside its crystals, and should break down quickly when exposed. [pause] Its presence suggests that some of the brine reached the surface within the last couple of million years. Very recent, for a world four and a half billion years old.

> i: occator crater | v: ceres surface
Imagine standing at the edge of Cerealia Facula. Under a black sky, a dome of brilliant white salt rises from the crater floor, glittering in weak sunlight. Gravity is only about three percent of the Earth's, so a gentle jump would carry you metres into the air and take several seconds to bring you down. [pause] A frozen salt flat, on a dwarf planet between Mars and Jupiter.

## A Buried Sea
mood: mystery
> i: ceres interior | v: ceres
Where did the water come from? In its final year, Dawn swooped down to just thirty five kilometres above Occator, mapping the crater in astonishing detail and measuring Ceres's gravity. The data suggested a large reservoir of salty water, perhaps hundreds of kilometres wide, about forty kilometres beneath the crater.

> i: ceres interior | v: ceres
Scientists think the impact that created Occator, about twenty million years ago, may have melted ice and created cracks that let the brine move upward. The reservoir may be the remains of a much larger ocean that once filled the inside of Ceres, now mostly frozen. [pause] Some of it may still be liquid today.

> i: kuiper belt | v: kuiper belt
Dawn also found clays containing ammonia on Ceres's surface. Ammonia ice is more common in the cold outer solar system. Some scientists think Ceres may have formed far beyond its current home, perhaps out among the giant planets, and later drifted inward to the asteroid belt. [pause] A wanderer that settled down.

> i: ahuna mons | v: ceres
Elsewhere, Dawn found a strange, lonely mountain called {{Ahuna Mons|AHUNA MONS}}, about four kilometres high, with steep sides streaked with bright material. It appears to be a cryovolcano, a volcano that erupted not lava, but a slushy mix of salty water and mud. [pause] An icy volcano, standing alone on a dwarf planet.

> i: ceres | v: ceres rotation
Dawn also detected organic molecules, the carbon-based compounds that are building blocks of life, in one region of the surface. Not life itself. But a sign that Ceres has the chemistry to make complex molecules, along with water and heat in its past.

## The Watcher In Orbit
mood: wonder
> i: dawn spacecraft | v: dawn spacecraft
On the thirty first of October 2018, Dawn ran out of the fuel it used to keep its antenna pointed at the Earth. The mission ended. But the spacecraft did not crash. It was left in a stable orbit around Ceres, where it is expected to stay for decades, so as not to contaminate a world that might one day be studied for signs of life.

> i: ceres | v: ceres
Today, Ceres is considered an ocean world candidate, alongside the icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn. Scientists have proposed missions to return, land near Occator and even bring samples of the bright salt back to the Earth.

> i: asteroid belt | v: asteroid belt
Once a lost planet. Then just an asteroid. Now a dwarf planet with salty water, an icy volcano and organic molecules. [pause] The brightest spots in the asteroid belt were not lights at all. They were the dried-up traces of a hidden sea, glinting in the sunshine.
