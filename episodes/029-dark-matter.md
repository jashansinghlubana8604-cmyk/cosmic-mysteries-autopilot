## The Missing Mass
mood: mystery
> i: coma cluster | v: galaxy cluster
In 1933, a Swiss astronomer named Fritz Zwicky studied a great cluster of galaxies called the Coma Cluster. He measured how fast its galaxies were moving, and found something strange.

> v: galaxy cluster zoom | i: coma cluster
They were moving far too fast. The visible matter in the cluster did not have nearly enough gravity to hold it together. By his estimate, there had to be much more mass there than could be seen. He called it dunkle Materie, {{dark matter|DARK MATTER}}.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxies
For decades, few people took it seriously. Astronomical measurements were uncertain, and Zwicky's idea seemed too strange. Perhaps the numbers were simply wrong.

> v: galaxy cluster | i: galaxy cluster hubble
Zwicky was a famously difficult man, known for insulting his colleagues, and that may have made it easier to ignore him. But his numbers were not wrong. Later measurements showed that the Coma Cluster really does contain far more mass than its galaxies and gas can account for. He had been decades ahead of his time.

## The Galaxies That Spin Too Fast
mood: wonder
> v: spiral galaxy rotation | i: andromeda galaxy
Then, in the 1970s, the astronomer Vera Rubin and her colleague Kent Ford studied how fast stars orbit within spiral galaxies, including Andromeda.

> i: spiral galaxy | v: galaxy rotation simulation
In our solar system, distant planets move more slowly than close ones. Neptune crawls around the Sun, while Mercury races. Rubin expected galaxies to behave the same way, with stars at their outer edges orbiting slowly.

> v: spiral galaxy | i: spiral galaxy hubble
They did not. Stars far from the centre were moving just as fast as stars close in. With only the visible matter present, those outer stars should have been flung off into space. Rubin found the same pattern in galaxy after galaxy.

> i: galaxy halo | v: dark matter halo
The simplest explanation was that each galaxy sits inside an enormous invisible halo of matter, far larger and heavier than the visible galaxy itself. Our own Milky Way appears to be embedded in one, many times more massive than all its stars.

> i: galaxy halo | v: milky way galaxy
The numbers are staggering. Ordinary matter, everything made of atoms, from stars to people, makes up only about five percent of the universe. Dark matter makes up about twenty seven percent. The rest, around sixty eight percent, is something different again: dark energy, the mysterious force accelerating the expansion of space. In other words, about eighty five percent of all the matter in the universe is invisible.

## Seeing The Invisible
mood: wonder
> v: gravitational lensing | i: gravitational lens
How do you map something you cannot see? You watch how it bends light. Einstein showed that mass curves space, and light follows those curves. Huge concentrations of mass act like lenses, distorting the images of galaxies behind them.

> i: einstein ring | v: gravitational lensing animation
By measuring these distortions, astronomers can draw maps of where the mass is, whether it shines or not. And those maps show the same thing: far more mass than we can see.

> v: bullet cluster | i: bullet cluster
The most striking evidence comes from the {{Bullet Cluster|THE BULLET CLUSTER}}, two clusters of galaxies that smashed into each other. The hot gas from each cluster collided and slowed down, glowing in X-rays. But most of the mass, mapped by gravitational lensing, passed straight through, as if it barely interacted at all.

> i: bullet cluster | v: galaxy cluster collision
The mass and the visible gas were in different places. It was as if a ghost had walked through a wall while the people beside it bumped into each other. For many astronomers, this was the clearest sign that dark matter is real, a substance, not just a quirk of gravity.

> v: ultra diffuse galaxy | i: ultra diffuse galaxy
And then there are the galaxies that break the pattern. In 2018, astronomers found a faint, wispy galaxy that seemed to have almost no dark matter at all. At first it seemed impossible. But it turned out to be useful evidence. If dark matter were just a mistake in our theory of gravity, every galaxy should show the same effect. A galaxy without dark matter suggests that dark matter is a real substance, which can sometimes be stripped away.

## What Could It Be?
mood: mystery
> v: particle collisions | i: particle physics
Dark matter is not ordinary matter hiding in the dark, like dim stars or rogue planets. Measurements of the early universe show that there is far too much of it to be made of atoms. It must be something else, something new.

> i: particle detector underground | v: dark matter detector
One leading idea is a heavy particle that barely interacts with anything, called a WIMP. Physicists have built enormous detectors deep underground, filled with tonnes of liquid xenon, waiting for one to bump into an atom. After decades of searching, they have found nothing, which rules out many of the simplest versions of the idea.

> v: particle physics | i: particle accelerator
Another candidate is the {{axion|AXION}}, an extremely light particle that could fill space like a faint wave. Experiments are searching for axions by trying to convert them into tiny flashes of radio signal inside powerful magnets.

> i: black hole | v: black hole simulation
Others suggest dark matter could be primordial black holes formed in the Big Bang. And a minority of physicists argue that there is no dark matter at all, and that our theory of gravity needs to change on large scales. Each idea faces serious challenges.

> i: particle detector | v: dark matter detector
The search goes on in every possible direction: in particle colliders, in detectors buried under mountains, in telescopes watching for faint signals from the centre of our galaxy, where dark matter should be densest. Some hints have come and gone. None has yet survived careful scrutiny.

## The Invisible Scaffolding
mood: wonder
> v: cosmic web simulation | i: dark matter simulation
Without dark matter, we would not exist. In the early universe, its gravity pulled together the first clumps of matter, long before ordinary gas could collapse on its own. Galaxies formed inside those clumps, like water collecting in hollows.

> i: cosmic web | v: dark matter simulation zoom
On the largest scales, dark matter forms a vast cosmic web of filaments and knots. Galaxies are the glowing lights strung along its threads. The visible universe is a thin coating on an invisible skeleton.

> v: euclid telescope | i: euclid
New telescopes are now mapping that skeleton in unprecedented detail. Europe's Euclid space telescope, launched in 2023, is charting billions of galaxies to measure how dark matter is distributed across the sky. The Vera Rubin Observatory, named in honour of the astronomer who revealed the spinning galaxies, is surveying the entire southern sky.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy rotation
Right now, trillions of dark matter particles may be passing through your body every second, without a trace. They have shaped every galaxy in the universe. [pause] And we still cannot say what they are.
