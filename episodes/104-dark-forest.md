Imagine walking through a dark forest at night. You cannot see more than a few metres ahead. You know there are others in the forest with you. Some are hunters. Some may be harmless. You cannot tell which.

> i: forest night | v: forest
Would you shout, to let everyone know where you are? Or would you move silently, listening, ready to defend yourself the moment you heard something? [pause] What if everyone in the forest made the same choice?

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
That is the idea behind one of the most chilling answers ever given to a simple question. Our galaxy is ancient and enormous, with billions of planets where life could arise. So why is it so quiet? Why have we never heard anyone else?

> i: star field | v: deep space flight

## The Great Silence
mood: mystery
> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
For more than sixty years, scientists have been listening for signals from other civilisations. Radio telescopes have scanned millions of stars. They have found nothing that could not be explained by natural sources or human interference.

> i: milky way galaxy | v: milky way galaxy
That silence is a puzzle known as the Fermi paradox. Given how old and big the galaxy is, even a few civilisations that spread out from their home worlds should have filled it by now. So where is everybody?

> i: ocean | v: ocean waves
To be fair, we have not searched much. One estimate in 2018 compared everything SETI has examined to a hot tub of water scooped from all the Earth's oceans. The silence may partly be because we have barely started looking. [pause] But the deeper we look and still hear nothing, the more the question bites.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
There are many possible answers. Perhaps life is rare. Perhaps civilisations destroy themselves. Perhaps they are too far apart. Or perhaps they are out there, and have very good reasons not to make a sound. [pause] The Dark Forest is the darkest version of that last idea.

## The Two Axioms
mood: dread
> i: book | v: library
The idea was made famous by the Chinese science fiction writer {{Liu Cixin|LIU CIXIN}}, in his novel The Dark Forest, published in 2008, the second book of the trilogy that began with The Three-Body Problem. The books became international bestsellers and were adapted for television.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
In the story, a character proposes a cosmic sociology based on two simple axioms. First: survival is the primary need of every civilisation. Second: civilisations continually grow and expand, but the total matter in the universe stays the same. [pause] Resources are limited. Everyone wants to survive.

> i: star field | v: star field
From there, two more ideas make everything worse. The first is the {{chain of suspicion|CHAIN OF SUSPICION}}. Even if you are peaceful, you cannot know whether another civilisation is. And they cannot know whether you are. Across light-years, there is no way to build trust. Every message takes decades or centuries to arrive.

> i: deep space flight | v: deep space flight
The second is the {{technological explosion|TECHNOLOGICAL EXPLOSION}}. A civilisation that looks primitive today could advance enormously in a few centuries. Humanity went from horses to spaceflight in about a hundred years. So even a weak civilisation you discover now might become dangerous by the time anything can be done about it.

> i: dark forest | v: forest night
Put those together, and the logic becomes grim. If you detect another civilisation, the safest thing to do is to destroy it, before it can destroy you. And if everyone reasons this way, then the best strategy for survival is to stay hidden. [pause] The universe becomes a dark forest, full of silent hunters.

> i: star | v: star
In the novel, this logic leads to a terrifying weapon. Not a fleet. Not a bomb. Just information. Simply broadcasting the location of a star to the rest of the galaxy is enough to doom it, because somewhere, someone will strike. [pause] In the dark forest, being seen is the same as being killed.

## Before The Novel
mood: mystery
> i: star field | v: star field
Liu Cixin was not the first to imagine a dangerous galaxy. In 1983, the scientist and author David Brin wrote a review of possible solutions to the great silence, including the idea that civilisations might hide from aggressive ones, or be wiped out by them.

> i: spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
Science fiction had explored versions of this for decades. Stories imagined self-replicating machines, built by long-dead civilisations, roaming the galaxy and destroying any life they find. Others imagined planet-killing weapons quietly sent towards any world that made too much noise. In the 1960s, the writer Fred Saberhagen called his killing machines Berserkers, and the name stuck.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
And some real scientists have made similar warnings. Stephen Hawking cautioned that answering a message from aliens might be dangerous, comparing it to what happened when more technologically advanced societies on Earth encountered less advanced ones. [pause] The Dark Forest gave those fears a single, memorable shape.

## Is It True?
mood: mystery
> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
Many scientists think the Dark Forest is unlikely as a real explanation. One reason is that hiding may be impossible. The Earth has been broadcasting radio and television into space for about a century. But more importantly, our planet's atmosphere has contained oxygen for more than two billion years.

> i: exoplanet atmosphere | v: exoplanet atmosphere
Any advanced civilisation with good telescopes could detect the oxygen in our air and know that something here is alive, from very far away. If the forest is full of hunters, they have had billions of years to notice us. [pause] We are not hiding. We never were.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
Others argue that destroying another civilisation across light-years would be enormously difficult and expensive, and might reveal your own position in the process. Cooperation, or simply ignoring each other, may be far more sensible strategies. The galaxy is vast enough for everyone.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
And the theory makes a lot of assumptions about how aliens would think. That they would value survival above everything. That they would reason like us. That fear would win over curiosity. [pause] We simply do not know if any of that is true.

## The Forest Around Us
mood: dread
> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
Still, the idea has real consequences. Scientists debate whether humanity should deliberately send powerful messages to the stars. Some think we should, to make contact. Others think we should not, until we know more about what is out there.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
The Dark Forest is a story, not a scientific theory. There is no evidence for it, and good reasons to doubt it. But it captures something unsettling about our situation. We are a young civilisation, in an old galaxy, shouting into the dark, with no idea who might be listening.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
So the next time you look up at a sky full of stars, and wonder why none of them speak to us, remember the forest. [pause] Perhaps the silence means we are alone. Perhaps it means everyone else is very far away. Or perhaps it means that somewhere out there, someone has heard us, and is deciding what to do.
