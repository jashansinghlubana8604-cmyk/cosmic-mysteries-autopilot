On the twenty seventh of December 2004, something hit the Earth. It came from the other side of the galaxy, from a star about fifty thousand light-years away. And for a fraction of a second, it was brighter than the full Moon.

> v: satellites orbit | i: satellite
Not in visible light. In gamma rays. Satellites across the solar system were blinded. Instruments built to watch distant explosions were overwhelmed. And high above our heads, the upper atmosphere of the Earth was briefly knocked out of shape.

> i: magnetar | v: magnetar
All of it from an object about twenty kilometres across. Smaller than a city. [pause] It had released, in a tenth of a second, more energy than our Sun gives off in a hundred thousand years.

> i: magnetar artist impression | v: neutron star magnetic field
That object was a {{magnetar|MAGNETAR}}. The strongest magnet known to exist in the universe. And what happened that night was not an explosion. [pause] It was a starquake.

## What Is Left Behind
mood: dread
> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova
To understand a magnetar, you have to start with the death of a giant star. A star at least ten times heavier than the Sun burns through its fuel in a few million years. When it runs out, its core collapses in less than a second.

> v: neutron star collapse | i: supernova remnant
The outer layers are blasted into space in a supernova. But the core keeps falling inward until the atoms themselves are crushed. Electrons are forced into protons. What is left is a ball made almost entirely of neutrons. A {{neutron star|NEUTRON STAR}}.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
It holds more mass than the Sun, squeezed into a sphere the size of a city. A single teaspoon of it would weigh about a billion tonnes, more than every car on Earth put together. Its gravity is so strong that light leaving its surface is bent around it.

> v: pulsar | i: pulsar
Most neutron stars spin fast and have strong magnetic fields. But about one in ten is born different. As the core collapses, it spins and churns so violently that it winds its magnetic field up, tighter and tighter, like a coil. [pause] Until the field is a thousand times stronger than a normal neutron star's.

> v: pulsar | i: neutron star
Strangely, magnetars are slow. Ordinary young neutron stars can spin many times a second, some of them hundreds of times. A magnetar turns only once every few seconds. Its own colossal magnetic field acts like a brake, dragging on everything around it until the star has almost been stopped in its tracks.

## A Magnet That Could Kill You
mood: dread
> i: earth magnetic field | v: earth magnetic field
Let's put that into numbers we can feel. The magnetic field of the Earth, the one that turns your compass needle, is weak. A fridge magnet is about a hundred times stronger. The most powerful magnets humans have ever built are around a million times stronger than the Earth.

> i: magnetar | v: magnetar
A magnetar's field can be around a {{thousand trillion times|1,000 TRILLION ×}} stronger than the Earth's. There is nothing in our experience that comes close. Nothing.

> v: magnetic field lines | i: magnetic field
If one were placed halfway between the Earth and the Moon, it would wipe every credit card on the planet. Get closer, within about a thousand kilometres, and something much worse happens. The field would distort the atoms in your body, stretching them into thin needles.

> i: magnetar artist impression | v: neutron star
Chemistry would stop working. The bonds that hold your molecules together would fail. You would not burn. You would not be crushed. [pause] You would simply stop being able to exist as matter.

## Starquakes
mood: mystery
> i: neutron star crust | v: neutron star
Here is where it gets stranger. A neutron star has a crust. A shell of iron and other elements, maybe a kilometre thick, so dense and so rigid that it is billions of times stronger than steel.

> v: magnetic field lines | i: magnetar
But the magnetic field inside a magnetar is always shifting, always twisting. It pushes against that crust from below, building up stress, the way tectonic plates build up stress before an earthquake.

> i: magnetar | v: magnetar flare
And sometimes, the crust cracks. A {{starquake|STARQUAKE}}. The magnetic field snaps into a new shape and releases a burst of energy unlike anything else in the universe. Scientists think the tallest mountains on a neutron star are only millimetres high. But a crack in that crust can shake a galaxy.

> v: magnetar flare | i: magnetar artist impression
Most magnetars flare quietly, now and then, with small bursts of X-rays. But a few times a century, somewhere in the galaxy, one lets go completely. A giant flare. [pause] On the twenty seventh of December 2004, it was the turn of a magnetar called {{SGR 1806-20|SGR 1806-20}}.

> i: large magellanic cloud | v: large magellanic cloud
It was not the first time. On the fifth of March 1979, two Soviet probes on their way to Venus were suddenly hit by a burst of gamma rays so strong it pushed their detectors off the scale. Seconds later, the same flash swept past other spacecraft across the inner solar system. Astronomers traced it to the remains of an exploded star in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy next to ours. [pause] It was the first giant flare ever recorded, and for years nobody understood what could possibly have made it.

## The Night It Reached Us
mood: dread
> i: milky way center | v: milky way
SGR 1806-20 sits on the far side of our galaxy, buried behind clouds of gas and dust. Nobody could see it with an ordinary telescope. And yet, when it flared, the blast of radiation crossed tens of thousands of light-years and swept over the Earth.

> v: satellites orbit | i: gamma ray satellite
It saturated detectors on spacecraft orbiting the Earth, the Moon and even Mars. Several instruments that were not even pointed at it still recorded it, because the gamma rays punched straight through their shielding.

> i: earth atmosphere | v: earth from space
The flash was strong enough to ionise the upper layers of the Earth's atmosphere on the side facing it, the way the Sun does every day. For a moment, the night sky over half the planet behaved as if it was daytime. [pause] And we never saw a thing with our eyes.

> i: magnetar | v: magnetar flare
Now imagine that flare had come from a magnetar just a few light-years away. It could have stripped away a large part of the ozone layer, leaving life on the surface exposed to the Sun's ultraviolet. The good news is, there is no known magnetar anywhere near us. [pause] The nearest ones are thousands of light-years away.

## Voices In The Dark
mood: mystery
> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
For years, astronomers had been picking up something else they could not explain. Fast radio bursts. Flashes of radio waves lasting a few thousandths of a second, coming from galaxies billions of light-years away. Nobody knew what made them.

> i: magnetar | v: magnetar
Then, in April 2020, a magnetar inside our own galaxy, called {{SGR 1935+2154|SGR 1935+2154}}, gave off a burst of X-rays. And at the exact same moment, radio telescopes caught a fast radio burst coming from the same spot. It was the first time one had been traced to a known object.

> i: galaxy m82 | v: galaxy
Since then, telescopes have even caught a giant flare from a magnetar in another galaxy, the Cigar Galaxy, about twelve million light-years away. These dead stars are not rare curiosities. They seem to be speaking across the whole universe.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
In our own galaxy, astronomers know of only about thirty magnetars. A tiny family, scattered across a hundred billion stars. Most were found only because they flared. [pause] Which means there may be many more out there, quiet for now, waiting.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
But they do not last. A magnetar's field is so strong it tears itself down. After maybe ten thousand years, the field weakens, the flares stop, and the magnetar fades into an ordinary neutron star. On the scale of the cosmos, that is a heartbeat. [pause] Which means every magnetar we see is young, furious, and already dying.

> i: magnetar artist impression | v: star field
Somewhere out there, right now, a ball of neutrons the size of a city is twisting its own magnetic field to breaking point. When it snaps, the light will race out across the galaxy. [pause] And one day, perhaps thousands of years from now, it will reach us too.
