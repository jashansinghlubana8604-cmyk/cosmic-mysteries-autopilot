But it is not alone. It is being pulled in a circle, once every five and a half days, by something invisible. Something with about twenty times the mass of the Sun, that gives off no visible light at all, yet blazes in X-rays.

And at the time, even Stephen Hawking was not sure. He made a famous bet that it was not a black hole. [pause] He lost. And this is how the first black hole was found.

## X-Rays From The Swan
mood: mystery
> i: rocket launch | v: rocket launch
X-rays from space are blocked by the Earth's atmosphere. To see them, you have to get above the air. In 1964, scientists launched small rockets carrying X-ray detectors, which rose above the atmosphere for a few minutes before falling back.

> i: x-ray sky | v: chandra xray
During those brief flights, they found several bright sources of X-rays in the sky. One of them, in the constellation Cygnus, was among the strongest. They named it Cygnus X-1, the first X-ray source found in that constellation.

> i: x-ray sky | v: rocket launch
These early experiments were part of the birth of X-ray astronomy, led by scientists such as Riccardo Giacconi, who would later win a Nobel Prize for opening this new window on the universe. Before them, nobody knew the sky was full of objects blazing in X-rays. [pause] Cygnus X-1 was one of the first, and it would become the most famous.

> i: uhuru satellite | v: satellite orbit
In 1970, NASA launched {{Uhuru|UHURU}}, the first satellite dedicated to X-ray astronomy, from a platform off the coast of Kenya. It found that Cygnus X-1's X-rays flickered rapidly, changing in less than a second. [pause] That meant the source had to be tiny. Light cannot coordinate a flicker across something bigger than the distance it travels in that time.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
Radio telescopes then helped pin down the exact position. And at that spot sat a hot, blue supergiant star with a long catalogue name, HDE 226868. [pause] But a normal blue star does not make X-rays like that. Something else had to be there.

## The Invisible Partner
mood: mystery
> i: blue supergiant | v: star
In 1971 and 1972, astronomers Tom Bolton in Canada, and Louise Webster and Paul Murdin in England, independently measured the blue star's motion. Its light shifted back and forth, regularly, every {{5.6 days|5.6 DAYS}}. It was orbiting something.

> i: binary star | v: binary star
From the size of that wobble, they could estimate the mass of the unseen partner. It was too heavy to be a white dwarf or a neutron star, the other kinds of dead stars. Those cannot exist above a certain mass without collapsing further.

> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
The picture that emerged was striking. The black hole was pulling gas off the blue supergiant. That gas swirled into a flat disc around it, getting hotter and hotter as it spiralled inwards, until it glowed in X-rays at millions of degrees, just before vanishing.

> i: cygnus x-1 | v: black hole
The black hole itself was invisible. But the gas falling into it was screaming. [pause] For the first time, a black hole had been caught in the act of feeding.

> i: black hole | v: black hole falling
If you could fly towards it, you would see the blue star's gas stretched into a stream, spiralling down into a glowing whirlpool. And unlike the giant black holes in the centres of galaxies, a black hole this small would be deadly long before the horizon. The difference in its pull between your head and your feet would stretch you apart, like dough through a pasta machine. [pause] Astronomers really do call it spaghettification.

## Hawking's Bet
mood: wonder
> i: stephen hawking | v: black hole
In the 1970s, Stephen Hawking had spent years working on the theory of black holes. If they did not exist, much of his work would be wasted. So, in 1974, he made a playful bet with his friend, the physicist Kip Thorne.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
Hawking bet that Cygnus X-1 was {{not a black hole|NOT A BLACK HOLE}}. He called it an insurance policy. If black holes turned out to be real, he would be thrilled and lose the bet. If they did not exist, at least he would win something. The prize was a magazine subscription.

> i: cygnus x-1 | v: black hole accretion disk
Over the following years, the evidence grew stronger and stronger. More measurements, better telescopes, more detailed models. By 1990, the case was overwhelming. Hawking broke into Thorne's office, found the framed bet on the wall, and signed his concession. [pause] He had lost, and he was delighted.

## A Heavier Black Hole
mood: dread
> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope
For decades, the black hole in Cygnus X-1 was thought to weigh around fifteen times the mass of the Sun. Then, in 2021, a team used a network of radio telescopes spanning the Earth to measure its distance more precisely. It was further away than thought, about seven thousand two hundred light-years.

> i: cygnus x-1 | v: black hole
That meant everything about the system was bigger and heavier. The black hole turned out to weigh about {{21 times the Sun|21 SUNS}}. At the time, that was surprisingly heavy for a black hole in our galaxy. It raised questions about how much mass massive stars lose before they collapse.

> i: black hole spinning | v: black hole
The black hole also appears to spin almost as fast as the laws of physics allow. Its event horizon would be roughly a hundred and twenty five kilometres across. [pause] Twenty one Suns, spinning, squeezed into a space you could drive across in an hour.

> i: blue supergiant | v: star
And its companion star is a monster too, around forty times the mass of the Sun. One day, it will also collapse. It might become another black hole, orbiting the first. [pause] And eventually, the two could spiral together and merge, sending gravitational waves across the universe.

## The Black Hole Next Door
mood: wonder
> i: black hole jet | v: black hole jet
Cygnus X-1 also fires jets of particles out of its poles. In 2005, astronomers found that those jets have inflated a vast ring-shaped bubble in the surrounding gas, several light-years across. [pause] The invisible object is shaping the space around it.

> i: chandra xray | v: chandra xray
Its X-ray glow is not steady either. Cygnus X-1 switches between different states, sometimes dominated by very high-energy X-rays from a hot cloud near the black hole, sometimes by softer X-rays from the disc itself. Astronomers still study these changes to understand how matter behaves in the last moments before it disappears.

> i: gaia bh1 | v: star field
Today, astronomers know of many black holes in our galaxy, some feeding on companions and blazing in X-rays, and others completely quiet, found only by the way they tug on nearby stars. Some are just over a thousand light-years from the Earth.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
Since 2015, gravitational-wave detectors have heard hundreds of black holes colliding across the universe. Black holes are no longer exotic theories. They are everywhere. [pause] But it all began with a bright blue star in the swan, dancing around an empty point in space. And a bet that was a pleasure to lose.
