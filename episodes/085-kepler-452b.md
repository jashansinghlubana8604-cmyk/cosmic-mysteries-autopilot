In July 2015, NASA held a press conference with an exciting announcement. They had found a planet a little bigger than the Earth, orbiting a star very much like our Sun, in the zone where liquid water could exist on its surface.

> i: earth like exoplanet | v: exoplanet

> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
It was called Kepler-452b. For years, it appeared in documentaries, books and lists of the most Earth-like planets ever found. [pause] And then, in 2018, a team of astronomers looked at the data again and asked an uncomfortable question.

> i: kepler-452b | v: star field

## The Planet Hunter
mood: wonder
> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
NASA's Kepler Space Telescope launched in 2009 with one job. It stared at a single patch of sky, in the constellations Cygnus and Lyra, watching around one hundred and fifty thousand stars at the same time, for years.

> i: exoplanet transit | v: exoplanet transit
It was looking for tiny dips in their brightness. When a planet passes in front of its star, it blocks a minuscule fraction of the light. For an Earth-sized planet crossing a Sun-like star, the dip is about {{one hundredth of one percent|0.01%}}. Like a moth crossing in front of a car headlight, seen from kilometres away.

> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
Kepler found thousands of planets. Most were bigger than the Earth or orbited far too close to their stars. What astronomers really wanted was the rarest prize of all. An Earth-sized planet, around a Sun-like star, in a year-long orbit. [pause] Those are the hardest of all to find, because they cross their stars only once a year.

> i: kepler telescope | v: spacecraft
In 2013, Kepler suffered a crippling failure. A second of its four reaction wheels, the spinning devices that kept it pointed precisely, broke down. It could no longer hold its gaze on its original patch of sky. Engineers found a clever fix, using the gentle pressure of sunlight to help balance it, and the telescope continued in a new mission called K2 until it ran out of fuel in 2018. [pause] But the original, four-year stare that found Kepler-452b was over.

## Earth 2.0
mood: wonder
> i: kepler-452b artist impression | v: exoplanet
Kepler-452b seemed to be the closest match yet. Its star, Kepler-452, is the same type as our Sun, a yellow star, slightly larger and about twenty percent brighter. And it is older, around six billion years, compared with our Sun's four and a half.

> i: kepler-452b | v: exoplanet orbit
The planet itself was estimated to be about one and a half times the width of the Earth. It orbits at roughly the same distance from its star as the Earth does from the Sun, completing one lap every {{385 days|385 DAYS}}.

> i: earth like exoplanet | v: exoplanet
If it is rocky, and that is a big if, it could be several times the mass of the Earth, with stronger gravity, a thicker atmosphere, and perhaps active volcanoes. Planets of that size sit on a dividing line. Some are rocky super-Earths. Others are small gas worlds with thick envelopes of hydrogen. [pause] Kepler could not tell which.

> i: exoplanet surface | v: exoplanet surface
If it is rocky, imagine standing there. A yellow sun, much like ours, a little larger and brighter in the sky. Gravity perhaps around twice what you feel now, making every step heavy. Clouds thicker, the air denser, the horizon hazy. [pause] Familiar, and yet utterly alien.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
And it is very far away. Estimates of its distance have ranged from around fourteen hundred to eighteen hundred light-years. Even at the speed of light, we would never get there in any lifetime. [pause] But the idea was intoxicating. Somewhere out there, perhaps, a world like ours, around a sun like ours, had been circling for longer than the Earth has existed.

## A Glimpse Of Our Future
mood: dread
> i: sun | v: sun
Because Kepler-452 is older than the Sun, it is also a preview of what is coming for us. Stars like ours get slowly brighter as they age. In about a billion years, the Sun will be bright enough to start boiling away the Earth's oceans.

> i: kepler-452b artist impression | v: exoplanet atmosphere
Kepler-452b may be facing that fate right now. Its star is already brighter than ours, and getting brighter. If the planet once had oceans, they might be warming, evaporating into a thick, steamy atmosphere that traps even more heat. [pause] It might be entering a runaway greenhouse, the same path that turned Venus into an inferno.

> i: venus | v: venus
NASA scientists said at the time that it gives us a chance to think about Earth's evolving environment. If anyone ever lived there, they may already be facing the slow, certain end of their world. [pause] An older cousin, showing us what old age looks like.

## Is It Real?
mood: mystery
> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
Here is the twist. Kepler-452b was never confirmed in the usual way. Normally, astronomers measure the planet's tug on its star, making the star wobble. For a small planet far from its star, that wobble is far too tiny to detect with today's instruments.

> i: computer data | v: data visualization
So instead, the planet was validated statistically. Astronomers calculated that the dips in Kepler's data were much more likely to be caused by a real planet than by anything else. And Kepler saw only a few of those dips, because the planet's year is so long.

> i: gaia spacecraft | v: gaia
In 2018, a team took another look. New data from the Gaia mission had revised the properties of many Kepler stars. And they found that, for planets with signals as faint as Kepler-452b's, the statistical method was not as certain as it seemed. [pause] The planet might be real. Or the dips might come from something else entirely, like a faint, distant star mixed into the light, or random noise in the detectors.

> i: kepler-452b | v: star field
They did not say it definitely is not there. They said we cannot be sure. And with the Kepler telescope now retired, no instrument can easily check it. [pause] Earth 2.0 became Earth 2.0, maybe.

## The Search Continues
mood: wonder
> i: plato telescope | v: spacecraft
The hunt for a true Earth twin goes on. The European Space Agency is preparing a mission called {{PLATO|PLATO}}, designed to watch large numbers of bright, Sun-like stars for years and find Earth-sized planets in Earth-like orbits around them.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
Because PLATO's stars will be brighter and closer than Kepler's, astronomers will be able to follow up its planets from the ground, measuring their masses directly. [pause] That means its Earth twins, if it finds any, should be confirmed for real.

> i: exoplanet atmosphere | v: exoplanet atmosphere
And NASA is planning a future space telescope, the Habitable Worlds Observatory, designed to block out the glare of nearby stars and directly photograph Earth-like planets, then analyse their air for signs of life.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
Maybe Kepler-452b is out there, warming under its ageing sun. Maybe it never was. But the question it raised is more alive than ever. [pause] Somewhere in our galaxy, there may be another world that looks like ours. We have not truly found it yet. But for the first time in history, we have the tools to look.
