## The Island Universe
mood: wonder
> i: andromeda galaxy | v: andromeda galaxy
For most of history, nobody knew what the Andromeda smudge was. Some astronomers thought it was a cloud of gas inside our own galaxy, perhaps a solar system being born. Others suspected it was something far grander, an island universe of its own.

> i: hubble telescope mount wilson | v: telescope dome
In 1923, at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California, astronomer {{Edwin Hubble|EDWIN HUBBLE}} found a special kind of star in Andromeda, a Cepheid, whose brightness pulses in a rhythm that reveals its true power. By comparing how bright it looked with how bright it really was, he could work out its distance.

> i: andromeda galaxy | v: andromeda zoom
The answer was so large that Andromeda had to lie far outside the Milky Way. In one stroke, the universe grew from a single galaxy to countless galaxies. [pause] Andromeda was the first proof that we are not alone in the cosmos. Not as a planet. As a galaxy.

> i: andromeda galaxy | v: andromeda galaxy
Today we know it is about two and a half million light-years away, perhaps two hundred and twenty thousand light-years across, with a supermassive black hole at its heart many times heavier than ours. It is the largest member of our local group of galaxies. [pause] And it is falling towards us.

> i: andromeda halo | v: galaxy merger
Andromeda has done this before. Its outer halo is crossed by streams and shells of stars, the wreckage of smaller galaxies it has torn apart. In 2018, astronomers argued that about two billion years ago it swallowed a large galaxy, perhaps the third biggest in our local group at the time. [pause] Andromeda is not a calm spiral. It is a survivor of collisions.

## The Approach
mood: mystery
> i: andromeda galaxy | v: andromeda galaxy
Almost every galaxy in the universe is moving away from us, carried by the expansion of space. Andromeda is one of the few exceptions. Its light is shifted slightly towards the blue, which means it is getting closer.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
The Milky Way and Andromeda are bound together by gravity. Over billions of years, that pull has been drawing them in. The speed of approach is around one hundred and ten kilometres per second, roughly {{400,000 km/h|400,000 KM/H}}.

> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
But measuring how fast Andromeda moves towards us is the easy part. To know whether it will hit us, you also need its sideways motion, how fast it is drifting across our sky. And at two and a half million light-years, that drift is incredibly small.

> i: andromeda milky way collision | v: galaxy collision
In 2012, astronomers used years of Hubble images to measure it for the first time. They found that the sideways motion was small. Andromeda was coming almost straight at us. Their conclusion: a head-on collision in about four billion years. [pause] It became one of the most famous predictions in astronomy.

## A Coin Toss
mood: mystery
> i: gaia spacecraft | v: gaia
In 2025, a team of astronomers ran the calculation again, using the latest data from Hubble and from the European Space Agency's {{Gaia|GAIA}} mission, which has measured the positions and motions of billions of stars with astonishing precision.

> i: large magellanic cloud | v: large magellanic cloud
And this time, they included the pull of other galaxies too. The Triangulum Galaxy, a smaller spiral close to Andromeda. And the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy orbiting the Milky Way, which turns out to be much heavier than once thought.

> i: andromeda galaxy | v: galaxy merger
They ran thousands of simulations, each with slightly different starting values within the uncertainties. In about half of them, the Milky Way and Andromeda merge within the next ten billion years. In the other half, they swing past each other and keep going, at least for that long. [pause] The chance of a merger within five billion years? Only about {{2%|2%}}.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
The Large Magellanic Cloud tugs the Milky Way slightly out of Andromeda's path, making a direct hit less likely. The Triangulum Galaxy pulls in the other direction. [pause] Our galaxy's fate, it turns out, is being decided by the little galaxies around it.

## If They Do Collide
mood: wonder
> i: galaxy collision | v: galaxy collision
So imagine the collision does happen. What would it be like? Surprisingly, almost nothing would hit anything. Stars are so far apart, compared with their size, that when two galaxies pass through each other, stars almost never collide.

> i: night sky andromeda | v: night sky
But the sky would change beyond recognition. Over hundreds of millions of years, Andromeda would grow from a faint smudge to an enormous spiral filling the night sky. Then, as the galaxies passed through each other, the sky would blaze with new star clusters, twisted streams of stars and glowing clouds of gas.

> i: galaxy merger | v: galaxy merger
The two galaxies would swing apart, fall back, and merge, perhaps over a couple of billion years. Their spiral arms would be torn up. Their gas clouds would slam together and collapse, lighting up in a burst of new stars, as bright blue clusters formed by the thousand. The result would most likely be one giant, rounded elliptical galaxy. Astronomers have already given it a name. {{Milkomeda|MILKOMEDA}}.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
At the centre, the two supermassive black holes would sink towards each other, orbit, and eventually merge, shaking space with gravitational waves. [pause] And our Sun? It would probably survive, but be flung into a new orbit, perhaps far out in the halo of the new galaxy.

## A Future Without Us
mood: wonder
> i: sun | v: sun
Here is the honest truth. Whatever Andromeda does, we will not be here to see it in the way we are now. In about a billion years, the Sun will have grown bright enough to boil away the Earth's oceans. Life as we know it will be gone long before the galaxies meet.

> i: sun red giant | v: sun
In about five billion years, the Sun will swell into a red giant. Galaxy collision or not, the Earth's story will be ending. Only by leaving this planet could anyone born of the Earth ever watch Andromeda fill the sky.

> i: andromeda galaxy | v: andromeda zoom
And yet, there is something wonderful in this. The light you see from Andromeda tonight left it when our ancestors were first learning to make stone tools. It has crossed two and a half million years to reach your eye. [pause] And the galaxy it came from may, one day, become part of our own home.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
Two vast spirals of stars, falling towards each other through the dark. Maybe they will collide. Maybe they will pass by. It is, quite literally, a coin toss on the scale of the universe. [pause] Either way, nothing in the cosmos stays still. Not even the galaxy we live in.
