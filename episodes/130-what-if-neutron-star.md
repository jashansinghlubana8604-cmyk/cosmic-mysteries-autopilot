> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
A neutron star is the crushed core of a giant star that exploded. It holds more mass than the Sun, packed into a ball about twenty kilometres across. A teaspoon of it would weigh about a billion tonnes.

> i: neutron star artist impression | v: neutron star
It is the closest thing to a solid surface you could ever find on a dead star. Unlike a black hole, it has a real surface, a crust you could, in theory, touch. [pause] So let's try. Let's take a ship and attempt to land on one.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
We will approach slowly, from far away, and see what happens at each stage. The radiation. The magnetic field. The gravity. The light. [pause] It will not end well. But the journey shows just how extreme the universe can be.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Imagine your ship is a few billion kilometres away, about the distance of Neptune from the Sun. Ahead of you, a tiny point of light is spinning. [pause] Let's go.

## The Lighthouse
mood: wonder
> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
From far away, the neutron star looks like nothing. It is so small that even from a million kilometres, it would be an almost invisible point. But if it is a {{pulsar|PULSAR}}, you would notice something else. Beams of radiation sweeping past you, again and again.

> i: pulsar beam | v: pulsar
A pulsar spins rapidly, sending beams out from its magnetic poles. If you are in the path of a beam, you get hit by a pulse of radio waves, and often X-rays and gamma rays too, every time it comes around. Some pulsars do this hundreds of times a second.

> i: pulsar | v: neutron star
For the fastest spinners, the surface at the equator races around at a noticeable fraction of the speed of light. A ball heavier than the Sun, turning hundreds of times every second, without flying apart, held together by its own ferocious gravity. [pause] Approaching it would be like flying towards a spinning lighthouse made of collapsed star.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
The surface itself is incredibly hot, often around a million degrees, glowing mostly in X-rays. Your ship would need serious radiation shielding just to approach. [pause] And we have not even reached the dangerous part yet.

## The Magnetic Field
mood: dread
> i: magnetic field lines | v: magnetic field
As you get within a few thousand kilometres, the magnetic field becomes overwhelming. A typical neutron star's field is roughly a trillion times stronger than the Earth's. A magnetar's can be a thousand times stronger still.

> i: magnetar | v: neutron star
Fields like that do strange things to matter. Every credit card and hard drive on your ship would have been wiped long ago. Any metal object would feel enormous forces. And closer in, the field would even begin to distort the atoms in your body.

> i: magnetic field | v: magnetic field
Atoms are normally round clouds of electrons around a nucleus. In fields this strong, the electrons are forced into tight spirals around the field lines, and atoms are squeezed into long, thin, needle-like shapes. [pause] Ordinary chemistry stops working. The molecules that make you, you, would no longer hold together the same way.

> i: pulsar wind nebula | v: pulsar
The spinning field also acts like a colossal electric generator, accelerating charged particles to nearly the speed of light and flinging them outward in a powerful wind. Around some pulsars, that wind lights up glowing clouds, like the Crab Nebula. [pause] Flying in, you would be sailing against a storm of particles more energetic than anything made on Earth.

## The Tides
mood: dread
> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
Let's imagine your ship is magically shielded from both radiation and magnetism. You keep falling. Now the real killer arrives: gravity. But not in the way you might expect.

> i: black hole falling | v: black hole falling
It is not the pull itself that gets you first. It is the difference in pull between your feet and your head. Close to a neutron star, the gravity at your feet, slightly nearer the star, would be far stronger than at your head. That difference is called a {{tidal force|TIDAL FORCE}}.

> i: neutron star artist impression | v: neutron star
Long before you reach the surface, perhaps thousands of kilometres out, the tidal force would become strong enough to stretch you apart, top to bottom, while squeezing you from the sides. Astronomers really do call it spaghettification. [pause] Your body would become a long, thin stream of atoms, falling towards the star.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
Strangely, a giant black hole would be gentler. Near a supermassive black hole, the difference in pull across your body is small enough that you could cross its edge without noticing. But a neutron star packs its mass into a ball just kilometres wide. [pause] Small, dense objects are the most violent places to approach.

## The Surface
mood: dread
> i: neutron star surface | v: neutron star
What reaches the surface would be travelling at a huge fraction of the speed of light. Gravity at the surface of a neutron star is around a hundred billion times stronger than on Earth. Even dropping something from a metre above it would make it hit the ground at well over a thousand kilometres per second.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
The impact would release an enormous burst of energy, and whatever was left of you would be flattened into a layer thinner than an atom, spread across the surface. [pause] There would be no landing. Only arrival.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
And the neutron star would barely notice. Your mass would simply be added to its own, an amount far too small to measure. Its spin would not change. Its glow would not flicker. [pause] Everything you are would become a few atoms of crust on a dead star, among the countless others that have fallen there before.

> i: neutron star surface | v: neutron star
And the surface itself would be strange. A thin atmosphere, perhaps only centimetres deep. Beneath it, a crust of iron nuclei packed in a crystal lattice, billions of times stronger than steel. Its tallest mountains would be only millimetres high, flattened by gravity. [pause] It would be smoother than the finest mirror ever made.

## What You Would See
mood: wonder
> i: gravitational lensing | v: light bending
Now imagine, impossibly, that you could stand there and look around. Gravity bends light so strongly near a neutron star that light from the far side of the star curves around to reach you. You would be able to see more than half of the star's surface at once.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
The horizon would not curve down away from you, as it does on Earth. It would seem to curve up around you, as if you were standing at the bottom of a giant bowl. [pause] The whole world would look turned inside out.

> i: clock | v: time lapse
Time itself would run slower. On the surface of a typical neutron star, clocks tick roughly twenty to thirty percent more slowly than they would far away. Spend an hour there, and slightly less than an hour would pass for you than for someone watching from a safe distance.

> i: pulsar | v: star field
Of course, none of this is survivable. No ship, no material, no living thing could ever land on a neutron star. [pause] But it is not science fiction. Every one of these effects follows from physics we have tested. And there are hundreds of millions of these objects in our galaxy, spinning silently in the dark, waiting for nothing at all.
