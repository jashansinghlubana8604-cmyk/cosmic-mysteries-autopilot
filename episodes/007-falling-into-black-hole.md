## A Solution Nobody Believed
mood: wonder
> i: einstein general relativity | v: spacetime curvature
In 1915, Albert Einstein published his theory of general relativity. It described gravity not as a force, but as the curving of space and time by mass. A heavy object bends the fabric of the universe around it, and everything else follows those curves.

> v: spacetime curvature | i: star field
Just weeks later, a German physicist named Karl Schwarzschild found the first exact solution to Einstein's equations, while serving as a soldier on the Russian front. It described the space around a single, perfectly round mass.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole simulation
Hidden in his mathematics was something disturbing. If you squeezed any mass into a small enough space, the curve of space would become so steep that nothing could climb out of it. Not even light. That boundary is now called the {{event horizon|EVENT HORIZON}}.

> v: dying star | i: supernova
For decades, most physicists, including Einstein, assumed nature would never actually do this. Then astronomers learned how massive stars die. When a giant star runs out of fuel, its core can collapse under its own weight, and nothing in physics can stop it. [pause] Black holes were not a mathematical curiosity. They were inevitable.

> i: event horizon telescope m87 | v: m87 black hole
In 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope, a network of radio dishes spanning the whole Earth, released the first ever image of one. A glowing ring around a dark shadow, at the centre of the galaxy M87, fifty five million light years away. It has the mass of {{six and a half billion Suns|6,500,000,000 SUNS}}.

## The Approach
mood: wonder
> v: black hole accretion disc | i: accretion disc
So let us choose our black hole carefully. A small one, a few times the mass of the Sun, would kill you long before you reached it. We will come back to why. Instead, let us travel to the giant at the heart of our own galaxy, Sagittarius A star, four million times the mass of the Sun.

> i: sagittarius a star event horizon telescope | v: milky way centre zoom
From far away, it is mostly invisible. Its event horizon is about twenty four million kilometres across, smaller than the orbit of Mercury, sitting in the middle of a galaxy a hundred thousand light years wide.

> v: accretion disc simulation | i: black hole disc
As you approach, you see the light of gas swirling around it, heated to millions of degrees as it spirals inward. And you start to see something impossible. The ring of light that wraps around the black hole is not just in front of it. You can see the far side as well, bent up and over the top, as if the disc were folded around the darkness.

> v: gravitational lensing | i: gravitational lens galaxy
That is gravity bending light. The black hole acts like an enormous lens, wrapping images of the universe around itself. Stars behind it are smeared into arcs and rings. In the film Interstellar, the black hole called Gargantua was created from real equations, and this is exactly what it showed.

> i: photon sphere | v: black hole simulation
Closer still, at one and a half times the size of the horizon, you reach a place where light itself can orbit the black hole in a circle. If you could stand there and look sideways, you might see the back of your own head, by light that has travelled all the way around.

## The Line You Cannot Feel
mood: mystery
> v: falling into black hole | i: black hole artist impression
Then you cross the event horizon. [pause] And here is the strangest part of all. You feel nothing.

> v: star field slow | i: star field
There is no barrier, no flash, no warning. For a black hole this large, the horizon is just an ordinary-looking region of space. If no one had told you, you would not know that you had passed the point of no return.

> i: black hole | v: black hole simulation
But everything has changed. Inside the horizon, space is falling inward faster than light can travel. Every direction you could possibly move now leads toward the centre. Firing your rockets will not help. Turning around will not help. The centre is no longer a place in front of you. [pause] It is your future, as certain as tomorrow.

> v: zoom out galaxy | i: deep field
Looking back, the outside universe does not vanish. Light from the stars keeps falling in behind you, and the whole sky shrinks into a bright circle above, growing smaller and brighter as you fall.

## Two Different Endings
mood: mystery
> v: time dilation clock | i: spacetime
Now imagine a friend watching you from a safe distance. What they see is completely different.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole simulation
As you approach the horizon, your friend sees you moving more and more slowly. Your watch appears to tick slower. Your light stretches from white to red to infrared. You never quite seem to reach the edge. You simply fade, frozen on the brink, until you disappear. [pause] To your friend, you never fall in at all.

> v: tidal disruption | i: tidal disruption event
Both stories are true. That is the extraordinary lesson of relativity: time is not the same for everyone. And now we can return to the smaller black holes.

> v: tidal disruption event | i: star torn apart black hole
Near a small black hole, gravity changes so violently over short distances that the pull on your feet is far stronger than the pull on your head. You would be stretched into a long, thin stream of atoms before you ever reached the horizon. Physicists really do call this {{spaghettification|SPAGHETTIFICATION}}. Astronomers have even watched it happen to whole stars that strayed too close to giant black holes.

## Where Physics Breaks
mood: wonder
> i: black hole | v: black hole simulation
So what happens at the centre? Here, our knowledge runs out.

> v: falling into black hole | i: black hole artist impression
Einstein's equations predict a singularity, a point where density becomes infinite and space and time come to an end. Inside Sagittarius A star, you would reach it in about a minute after crossing the horizon. Inside the giant in M87, you might have more than a day.

> i: quantum universe | v: cosmic web simulation
But almost every physicist believes the singularity is not real. It is the place where Einstein's theory stops working, where the physics of the very large meets the physics of the very small, and we do not yet have a theory that unites them.

> v: hawking radiation | i: black hole evaporating
In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that black holes are not completely black. They should glow very faintly, losing energy, and over unimaginable stretches of time, evaporate entirely. That raised a question that is still argued about today. When a black hole disappears, what happens to everything that fell in? Is the information about it lost forever, or written somehow into that faint glow?

> v: zoom milky way centre | i: milky way centre
Somewhere at the heart of our galaxy, twenty six thousand light years away, a black hole of four million Suns is quietly turning. Stars orbit it at thousands of kilometres per second. Gas falls in, and is never seen again.

> i: event horizon telescope sagittarius a star | v: black hole simulation
It is the darkest place in our galaxy, and one of the most beautiful. And it holds an answer we have not been able to reach. [pause] What lies on the other side of the horizon may be the deepest secret in the universe.
