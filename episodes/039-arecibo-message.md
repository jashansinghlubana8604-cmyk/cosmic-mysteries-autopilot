## The Giant Ear
mood: wonder
> i: arecibo observatory | v: arecibo telescope
The Arecibo Observatory was built in the 1960s inside a natural sinkhole in the hills of Puerto Rico. Its dish was {{305 metres|305 METRES}} across, wide enough to hold three football pitches end to end, made of nearly forty thousand aluminium panels.

> i: arecibo telescope | v: arecibo telescope
Above it, suspended on cables from three tall towers, hung a nine-hundred-tonne platform holding the receivers. For more than fifty years, it was one of the most important telescopes ever built.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
It found the first planets outside our solar system, around a dead star. It helped discover the first pulsar in a binary system, a discovery that won a Nobel Prize. It mapped the surfaces of Venus and Mercury with radar, and tracked asteroids that might one day threaten the Earth.

> i: arecibo observatory | v: arecibo telescope
But it could also do something no other telescope could. It could transmit. Its radar was powerful enough to bounce signals off other planets. [pause] And in 1974, a group of scientists realised it could send a message much, much further.

## A Picture Made Of Numbers
mood: mystery
> i: frank drake | v: radio telescope
The message was designed by astronomer Frank Drake, with help from Carl Sagan and others. It had a tricky problem to solve. How do you write a letter to someone who does not share a single word, symbol or idea with you?

> i: arecibo message | v: arecibo message
Their answer was mathematics. The message was {{1,679 bits|1,679 BITS}} long. That number was chosen on purpose. It is the product of only two prime numbers, twenty three and seventy three. A curious mind would try arranging the bits in a grid of twenty three columns by seventy three rows. [pause] And when you do, a picture appears.

> i: arecibo message | v: dna helix
At the top, the numbers one to ten, in binary. Then the atomic numbers of hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and phosphorus, the elements that build our DNA. Then formulas for the pieces of DNA itself, and the twisting double helix.

> i: arecibo message | v: arecibo message
Below that, a crude stick figure of a human, with our average height beside it, and the number of people alive at the time, about four billion. Then a row of dots showing our solar system, with the third one raised up, pointing at the figure. [pause] This is where we live.

> i: arecibo telescope | v: arecibo telescope
And at the very bottom, a picture of the telescope that sent it. As if to say: this is how we called you. If you can read this, you can answer.

## Aimed At A Moving Target
mood: wonder
> i: m13 globular cluster | v: globular cluster
The message was aimed at {{M13|M13}}, the Great Globular Cluster in the constellation Hercules. A ball of several hundred thousand stars, packed so tightly that some of them are only a fraction of a light-year apart.

> i: globular cluster | v: globular cluster
The idea was simple. Aim at a crowd, and you raise the odds that somebody is listening. But M13 is about twenty five thousand light-years away. The message will take twenty five thousand years to arrive. Any answer would take another twenty five thousand to come back.

> i: m13 globular cluster | v: star field
And by the time the signal gets there, the cluster will have drifted. The stars will no longer be quite where we aimed. [pause] The truth is that the message was never really meant to be answered. It was a demonstration of what our technology could do.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
But a signal does not care what you meant. It is out there. It is passing star after star as it goes. Anyone, anywhere along its path, with a big enough antenna and a reason to listen, could catch it.

> i: crop circle | v: field aerial
In 2001, a strange pattern appeared in a wheat field in England, next to a radio telescope. It looked like a twisted copy of the Arecibo message, with a different figure, a different DNA and a different planet raised. Some people claimed it was a reply. [pause] It was a hoax, made by people with planks and rope. But it showed how badly we want someone to answer.

## Should We Be Shouting?
mood: dread
> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Not everyone thought this was a good idea. Shortly after the message was sent, the British astronomer Sir Martin Ryle reportedly protested that it was reckless to announce our existence to the galaxy without knowing who was out there.

> i: dark forest | v: star field
Decades later, Stephen Hawking made a similar warning. If aliens ever visited us, he said, the outcome might be much like when Columbus landed in the Americas, which did not turn out well for the people who already lived there. [pause] A civilisation advanced enough to travel between stars would be advanced enough to do whatever it wanted.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
The debate even has a name. Listening for signals is called SETI. Deliberately sending them is called {{METI|METI}}, messaging extraterrestrial intelligence. And scientists are still sharply divided about whether we should do it at all.

> i: radio telescope dish | v: radio telescope
Arecibo was not the last. In 1999 and 2003, a radio dish in Ukraine sent longer messages towards several nearby stars. In 2008, NASA beamed a Beatles song, Across the Universe, towards the North Star. Small groups have sent poems, photographs and adverts into space. [pause] Most of these signals were far weaker than Arecibo's. But they are all out there, spreading.

> i: earth from space | v: earth night
Others argue the question is already settled. Since the 1930s, our television and radio broadcasts, and especially our powerful radar, have been leaking into space. A bubble of human noise, about a hundred light-years wide, is already expanding around the Earth. [pause] If someone nearby is listening carefully, they may have noticed us long ago.

## The Silent Telescope
mood: mystery
> i: arecibo observatory | v: arecibo telescope
In August 2020, one of the cables holding Arecibo's platform slipped out of its socket and tore a gash in the dish. A few months later, a second cable failed. Engineers decided the telescope could not be safely repaired.

> i: arecibo telescope collapse | v: arecibo telescope
On the first of December 2020, before it could be dismantled, the platform fell. Nine hundred tonnes of steel crashed through the dish it had hung over for more than half a century. {{Arecibo was gone|ARECIBO IS GONE}}.

> i: star field | v: milky way time-lapse
But its message is not. Right now, it is still racing outward at the speed of light, more than fifty light-years from home, passing stars we have never named. It has already outlived the machine that sent it, and it will almost certainly outlive us.

> i: m13 globular cluster | v: globular cluster
In about twenty five thousand years, that faint stream of pulses will finally sweep through the stars of Hercules. Maybe nobody will be there to hear it. Maybe someone will. [pause] Either way, it carries a simple picture of who we were in 1974. Our numbers, our chemistry, a little figure of a person, and a dot that says: here. We were here.
