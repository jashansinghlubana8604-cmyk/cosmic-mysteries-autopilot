On the fourth of July 2016, a spacecraft the size of a basketball court fired its engine for thirty five minutes, deep inside the most dangerous radiation belts in the solar system. If the burn had failed, it would have flown past Jupiter and been lost.

> i: juno spacecraft | v: juno spacecraft

> i: jupiter south pole juno | v: jupiter poles
What it saw there had never been seen before. Giant storms locked together in geometric patterns. Lightning where there should be none. Cloud bands rooted thousands of kilometres deep.

## The Dive
mood: epic
> i: juno spacecraft | v: juno launch
Juno launched in August 2011. It took five years to reach Jupiter, including a swing back past the Earth to gain speed. Its three huge solar panels made it the most distant solar-powered spacecraft ever at that time, because sunlight at Jupiter is about twenty five times weaker than at Earth.

> i: jupiter radiation belts | v: jupiter magnetosphere
Jupiter is surrounded by radiation strong enough to destroy unshielded electronics in days. So Juno's computers sit inside a {{titanium vault|TITANIUM VAULT}}, with walls about a centimetre thick. Its orbit was designed to slip under the worst of the belts, dive close over the planet, and swing far away again.

> i: jupiter juno | v: jupiter flyby
A problem with valves in its engine meant Juno never tightened its long loops as planned. Each orbit took fifty three days instead of fourteen. [pause] The mission adapted, and the long orbits let it survive far longer than first hoped, year after year.

> i: junocam jupiter | v: jupiter clouds
It also carried a camera mostly meant for the public. Called {{JunoCam|JUNOCAM}}, its raw pictures are posted online, and people around the world process them into images. [pause] Many of the most beautiful pictures of Jupiter ever made came from amateurs at home.

> i: juno spacecraft | v: juno spacecraft
Juno also carried three small passengers: aluminium figurines of the Roman god Jupiter, his wife Juno, and the astronomer Galileo, who discovered Jupiter's four largest moons in 1610. Galileo holds a tiny telescope. [pause] They are the most distant toys ever sent into space.

## Storms At The Poles
mood: wonder
> i: jupiter south pole juno | v: jupiter poles
Nobody had ever had a good look at Jupiter's poles. From Earth, and from earlier spacecraft, we only saw them at a steep angle. Juno flew right over them.

> i: jupiter north pole cyclones | v: jupiter poles
At the north pole, it found {{eight cyclones|8 CYCLONES}} arranged in a ring around a central one, like a giant octagon. At the south pole, five surrounded another central storm, in a pentagon. Each cyclone is thousands of kilometres wide, some larger than the United States.

> i: jupiter cyclones | v: jupiter storm
On Earth, hurricanes drift towards the poles and fade. On Jupiter, these storms are packed together and stay in place, year after year, without merging. In 2019, Juno watched a new small storm try to join the southern group. [pause] The pattern shifted into a hexagon, with six storms around the centre.

> i: saturn hexagon | v: saturn north pole
Scientists are still working out why the storms remain so orderly. Saturn has its own strange polar pattern, a six-sided jet stream. [pause] Giant planets, it seems, build shapes at their poles that our planet never could.

## Deeper Than We Thought
mood: mystery
> i: jupiter clouds | v: jupiter clouds
Jupiter's famous stripes, the light zones and dark belts, were long thought to be a thin layer of weather on top of the planet. Juno showed otherwise.

> i: jupiter bands | v: jupiter rotating
By measuring tiny changes in Jupiter's gravity, Juno discovered that the winds in the bands reach about {{three thousand kilometres|3,000 KM}} deep. That is more than a quarter of the Earth's diameter, all moving as a vast weather system.

> i: great red spot | v: great red spot
Juno also flew over the Great Red Spot, the giant storm bigger than the Earth. Its instruments looked into it with microwaves and found that it extends several hundred kilometres down, far deeper than any storm on Earth, yet still shallow compared to the planet.

> i: jupiter lightning | v: lightning storm
Then came lightning. Earlier spacecraft had seen flashes deep in Jupiter's clouds, where water condenses. Juno found smaller flashes high up, where it should be far too cold for liquid water. Scientists think ammonia acts like antifreeze there, and forms slushy hailstones they nicknamed {{mushballs|MUSHBALLS}}. [pause] The mushballs fall deep into the planet, carrying ammonia with them, which explains why it seemed to be missing from parts of the atmosphere.

## A Fuzzy Heart
mood: mystery
> i: jupiter interior | v: jupiter interior
The biggest surprise came from the planet's centre. Most scientists expected Jupiter to have a small, compact core of rock and ice, around which the gas had gathered.

> i: jupiter core | v: jupiter interior
Juno's gravity measurements suggested something stranger. The core seems to be {{fuzzy|FUZZY}}, spread out over a large part of the planet's interior, with heavy elements mixed into the surrounding hydrogen. [pause] There is no sharp boundary where the core begins.

> i: planet collision | v: planet collision
One idea is that, early in its history, a young planet many times the mass of the Earth slammed into Jupiter and stirred up its core. Others argue that a dilute core could simply form this way as the planet grew. [pause] Either way, Juno has rewritten the story of how the biggest planet came to be.

> i: jupiter magnetic field | v: jupiter aurora
Juno also mapped Jupiter's magnetic field. It is lumpy and uneven, unlike the Earth's. Near the equator, there is an intense patch that scientists named the Great Blue Spot, and the field has changed slightly during Juno's years in orbit. [pause] For the first time, we have seen the magnetic field of another planet change over time.

> i: jupiter aurora | v: jupiter aurora
Above the poles, Juno flew straight through the regions that feed Jupiter's auroras, the most powerful in the solar system. Each one is bigger than the Earth. Juno found that they are driven partly by processes that work differently from the northern lights on our own planet, and that the volcanic moon Io leaves a glowing footprint in them as it orbits. [pause] Even Jupiter's light shows are shaped by its moons.

## Visiting The Moons
mood: epic
> i: ganymede | v: ganymede
As its orbit slowly shifted, Juno began flying close past Jupiter's moons. In 2021, it passed Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system. In 2022, it skimmed Europa, the icy moon thought to hide an ocean.

> i: io volcano | v: io
Then, at the end of 2023 and in early 2024, Juno swooped within about fifteen hundred kilometres of {{Io|IO}}, the most volcanic world we know. Its images showed lava lakes, a mountain shaped like a sharp peak and fresh volcanic plumes. [pause] Juno even measured that Io does not seem to have a single global ocean of magma under its crust.

> i: jupiter juno | v: jupiter flyby
Juno was supposed to finish its work after about two years. Instead, it kept going for years longer, orbit after orbit, through radiation that slowly damaged its camera and instruments. [pause] Its engineers kept finding ways to keep it alive.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotating
When its mission ends, Juno is planned to dive into Jupiter's atmosphere and burn up, so it can never crash into and contaminate the moons that might hold life. [pause] It will become part of the planet it spent so many years studying. And thanks to it, Jupiter is no longer just a striped ball in a telescope. It is a world with a fuzzy heart, deep storms and poles no one had ever seen.
