## Why Black Holes Hide
mood: mystery
> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
Most of the black holes we knew about before were noisy. They orbit close to a living star and pull gas off it. That gas swirls into a disc and heats up, glowing brightly in X-rays. That glow is what gives them away.

> i: chandra xray | v: chandra xray
Even so, only a few dozen black holes in our galaxy had their masses reliably measured this way. A tiny sample, out of an estimated hundred million. [pause] It was like trying to understand the population of a country by counting only the people who were shouting.

> i: binary star | v: binary star
But if a black hole's companion orbits further out, no gas flows between them. The black hole stays quiet. It does not glow. It does not flicker. It simply sits there, its gravity pulling its partner in a slow, steady circle. [pause] Astronomers call these dormant black holes.

> i: star field | v: star field
For years, astronomers suspected there must be huge numbers of them, but finding them was extremely difficult. Several claimed discoveries turned out to be mistakes, ordinary stars misinterpreted. [pause] To find a sleeping black hole, you need to measure a star's motion with almost unbelievable accuracy.

## The Most Precise Map Ever Made
mood: wonder
> i: gaia spacecraft | v: gaia
In 2013, the European Space Agency launched {{Gaia|GAIA}}. Its job was to measure the positions, distances and motions of more than a billion stars, with precision never achieved before.

> i: gaia | v: gaia
Gaia could measure positions in the sky so precisely that it was like measuring the width of a coin on the Moon from the Earth. By observing each star many times over years, it could see tiny motions, including the small loops a star traces as it orbits an unseen companion.

> i: gaia spacecraft | v: spacecraft
Gaia was switched off in March 2025, after more than a decade of observations. But astronomers will be analysing its data for many years, and its largest data releases are still to come. It has already given us the most detailed three-dimensional map of our galaxy ever made, revealed the remains of galaxies the Milky Way swallowed long ago, and tracked hundreds of thousands of asteroids in our own solar system. [pause] It may turn out to be one of the most productive missions in the history of astronomy.

## Gaia BH1 And BH2
mood: wonder
> i: gaia bh1 | v: binary star
In 2022, a team led by astronomer Kareem El-Badry searched Gaia's data for stars wobbling around something heavy and dark. They found one: a Sun-like star in Ophiuchus, orbiting an invisible companion every one hundred and eighty six days.

> i: black hole companion star | v: binary star
They confirmed it with ground-based telescopes, measuring the star's speed as it moved towards and away from us. The companion weighed about {{ten times the Sun|10 SOLAR MASSES}}. Too heavy and too dark to be anything but a black hole. It was named Gaia BH1, at about fifteen hundred and sixty light-years away.

> i: sunset | v: sunset
Imagine a planet orbiting that Sun-like star. Its sky might look much like ours, with a warm yellow sun rising and setting. But every six months, the sun would be tugged around an invisible point by a black hole as heavy as ten Suns, orbiting at roughly the distance of the Earth from our own star. [pause] A quiet monster, hidden in plain sight.

> i: red giant star | v: red giant
In 2023, the same team announced Gaia BH2, in the southern constellation Centaurus, about three thousand eight hundred light-years away. It is orbited by a red giant star, on a wider path that takes about three and a half years to complete.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
What makes these two systems so strange is their size. The companion stars orbit at distances similar to the planets in our solar system. But the black holes formed from giant stars, which would have swelled to enormous sizes before dying. [pause] How did these companions survive so close to such giants? Nobody is quite sure.

## The Monster In Aquila
mood: dread
> i: gaia bh3 | v: black hole
In April 2024, while checking early data for an upcoming release, the Gaia team noticed a star that was wobbling unusually strongly. It was an old giant star in the constellation Aquila, about two thousand light-years from the Earth.

> i: gaia bh3 | v: binary star
Its invisible companion weighed about {{33 times the Sun|33 SOLAR MASSES}}. The heaviest black hole known in the Milky Way that formed from a dying star. It was named Gaia BH3. The discovery was so important that the team released it early, before the official data release.

> i: star | v: star
The companion star is very old and poor in heavy elements like iron. That fits a theory: stars made mostly of hydrogen and helium lose less mass in their winds as they age, so they can leave behind heavier black holes when they die. [pause] Gaia BH3 may be a relic from an earlier era of our galaxy.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
It is also moving with a stream of stars that seems to be the remains of an ancient star cluster, torn apart by the Milky Way's gravity long ago. [pause] A giant black hole, drifting with the wreckage of its birthplace.

## How Many Are Out There?
mood: mystery
> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
Astronomers estimate that our galaxy contains around a hundred million black holes formed from stars. We have found only a tiny fraction. The ones Gaia found are among the closest known to the Earth.

> i: gaia spacecraft | v: gaia
With future Gaia data releases, scientists expect to find many more dormant black holes, hiding beside stars that look completely normal. [pause] Some may be closer still.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
Gaia BH3 also connects to another discovery. Gravitational-wave detectors on Earth have recorded many collisions between black holes of thirty Suns or more in distant galaxies. Astronomers wondered where such heavy black holes come from. [pause] Gaia BH3 shows that black holes this big exist in our own galaxy too, quietly waiting.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
So when you look up at the night sky, remember that some of those ordinary-looking stars may be dancing with something invisible. A dark partner, heavier than the Sun, silent and hidden. [pause] The galaxy is full of sleeping giants. We are only just learning how to see them.
