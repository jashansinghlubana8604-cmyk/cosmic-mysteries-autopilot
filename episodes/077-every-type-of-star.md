On a clear night, the stars look almost identical. Tiny white points, a little brighter or fainter, perhaps a hint of colour here and there. It is easy to think they are all basically the same.

> i: star cluster | v: star cluster
They are not. Some stars are barely bigger than Jupiter and will glow for trillions of years. Others are so enormous they would swallow the orbit of Saturn, and so violent they blow themselves apart in a few million years.

> i: star size comparison | v: star size comparison
Some are dead, still glowing with leftover heat. Some spin hundreds of times a second. Some have collapsed so completely that not even light escapes. [pause] The universe is a zoo of stars.

> i: blue supergiant | v: star
Today, we are going to meet all of them. From the tiniest embers, to stars like our Sun, to the giants, the monsters and the corpses. [pause] Every type of star, from tiny to terrifying.

## The Alphabet Of Stars
mood: wonder
> i: harvard computers | v: telescope dome
In the early 1900s, at the Harvard College Observatory, a team of women known as the Harvard Computers sorted hundreds of thousands of stars by their light. One of them, {{Annie Jump Cannon|ANNIE JUMP CANNON}}, created the system astronomers still use today.

> i: star spectrum | v: star
She arranged stars into classes using letters: O, B, A, F, G, K and M. From the hottest, bluest stars to the coolest, reddest ones. Generations of students have memorised them with a simple phrase: Oh Be A Fine Girl, or Guy, Kiss Me.

> i: cepheid variable | v: star
Another of the Harvard Computers, Henrietta Leavitt, studied stars that pulse in brightness, called Cepheids. She found that the slower a Cepheid pulses, the brighter it truly is. That simple rule turned these stars into cosmic measuring sticks. [pause] Years later, Edwin Hubble used them to prove that the universe is full of other galaxies.

> i: sun | v: sun
In 1925, another Harvard astronomer, {{Cecilia Payne|CECILIA PAYNE}}, made a revolutionary discovery from those spectra. Stars are made overwhelmingly of hydrogen and helium. At the time, everyone believed they were made of the same elements as the Earth. [pause] Her finding was so shocking that she was persuaded to call it probably not real. It was.

## The Small Ones
mood: wonder
> i: brown dwarf | v: brown dwarf
At the very bottom are objects that never quite became stars. {{Brown dwarfs|BROWN DWARFS}}. Heavier than Jupiter, but too light to fuse ordinary hydrogen. They glow dimly for a while and then slowly cool. The coldest known ones are about as cool as a cup of tea.

> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
Just above them are {{red dwarfs|RED DWARFS}}, class M. The smallest true stars, sometimes only a tenth of the Sun's mass. They are dim, cool and red, and they are by far the most common stars in the galaxy, around three quarters of all stars. Not one of them is visible to the naked eye.

> i: proxima centauri | v: red dwarf
They burn their fuel so slowly that the smallest of them will shine for trillions of years, far longer than the universe has existed so far. Every red dwarf ever born is still alive. [pause] When every other star has died, red dwarfs will still be glowing.

> i: orange dwarf star | v: star
Then come the {{orange dwarfs|ORANGE DWARFS}}, class K. A little smaller and cooler than the Sun, living for tens of billions of years, and much calmer than red dwarfs. Some astronomers think they might be the best stars of all for life: stable, long-lived and gentle. [pause] Goldilocks stars.

## Stars Like Ours
mood: wonder
> i: sun solar dynamics observatory | v: sun
Our Sun is a {{yellow dwarf|YELLOW DWARF}}, class G. The name is a little misleading. It is not really yellow, it is white, and it is not small. It is bigger and brighter than about nine in ten stars in the galaxy. It will live for about ten billion years in total.

> i: sirius | v: star
Hotter and heavier are the white stars of class A, like {{Sirius|SIRIUS}}, the brightest star in our night sky. It is about twice the mass of the Sun and around twenty five times brighter. Stars like this burn faster and live only a billion years or so.

> i: blue star | v: star
And at the top of the alphabet are the blue stars, classes B and O. Tens of times heavier than the Sun, thousands to hundreds of thousands of times brighter, with surfaces hotter than thirty thousand degrees. They are incredibly rare. [pause] And they live fast. The biggest burn out in just a few million years.

> i: red giant star | v: red giant
When stars like the Sun run out of hydrogen in their cores, they swell into {{red giants|RED GIANTS}}. The bright orange star Aldebaran, in Taurus, is one of them, about forty times wider than the Sun. One day, the Sun will become one too.

## The Monsters
mood: dread
> i: betelgeuse | v: red supergiant
The heaviest stars become something far grander. {{Supergiants|SUPERGIANTS}}. Betelgeuse, in Orion, is a red supergiant so vast that if it replaced our Sun, it would reach out beyond the orbit of Mars. Rigel, Orion's other bright star, is a blue supergiant, tens of thousands of times brighter than the Sun.

> i: hypergiant | v: star
Bigger still are the {{hypergiants|HYPERGIANTS}}. The largest known stars, unstable and swollen, shedding their outer layers in huge eruptions. Some would reach out towards the orbit of Saturn if placed where our Sun is. They are so big that their own light pushes them apart.

> i: wolf rayet star | v: star
And then there are {{Wolf-Rayet stars|WOLF-RAYET STARS}}. Massive stars that have lost their outer layers in ferocious winds, exposing their blazing hot interiors. They blow gas off at thousands of kilometres per second. They are close to the end, and many may die in spectacular supernovae.

> i: r136 | v: star cluster
In the Tarantula Nebula, in a neighbouring galaxy, lies a star called R136a1, one of the heaviest ever found, perhaps around two hundred times the mass of the Sun, and millions of times brighter. [pause] Stars like this push right up against the limit of how big a star can be.

## The Dead
mood: dread
> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
Every star dies. Stars like our Sun end as {{white dwarfs|WHITE DWARFS}}. The leftover core, about the size of the Earth, but with about half the mass of the Sun. Sirius has one, a tiny companion called Sirius B. A teaspoon of it would weigh tonnes.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
Heavier stars collapse further, into {{neutron stars|NEUTRON STARS}}. A ball of neutrons heavier than the Sun, squeezed to the size of a city. Some spin hundreds of times a second and sweep beams across space as pulsars. Some have magnetic fields a thousand trillion times the Earth's, as magnetars.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
And the heaviest of all collapse completely, into {{black holes|BLACK HOLES}}. Regions where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. The final form of the greatest stars.

> i: white dwarf | v: star field
And one type of star does not exist yet. When a white dwarf finally loses all its heat, it will become a cold, dark black dwarf. But white dwarfs cool so slowly that this takes far longer than the thirteen point eight billion years the universe has existed. [pause] Not a single black dwarf has ever formed. They are the stars of the far future.

> i: star cluster | v: star cluster
Brown dwarfs and red dwarfs. Orange, yellow, white and blue. Giants, supergiants and hypergiants. White dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes. [pause] Next time you look at the night sky and see all those identical white points, remember. You are looking at a zoo. And every animal in it is a different kind of fire.
