## Burning Ice And Wild Swings
mood: mystery
> i: exoplanet transit | v: exoplanet transit
How can we know the weather on a planet we have never seen as more than a dot? Most of these worlds pass in front of their stars as we watch. For a few hours, a tiny fraction of the starlight shines through the edge of the planet's atmosphere. Each gas in that atmosphere leaves its own fingerprint in the light. [pause] We are reading alien skies, one sliver of starlight at a time.

> i: gliese 436b | v: exoplanet
Number eight. {{Gliese 436 b|GLIESE 436 b}}. A world about the size of Neptune, roughly thirty light-years away, orbiting a small red star. Its surface temperature may be over four hundred degrees.

> i: exoplanet artist impression | v: exoplanet atmosphere
And yet, scientists think much of it could be water ice. Not normal ice. Ice squeezed so hard by the planet's gravity that it stays solid even while it is scorching hot. Burning ice. It is also trailing a giant cloud of hydrogen behind it, like the tail of a comet, as its star slowly boils away its air.

> i: hd 80606b | v: exoplanet orbit
Number seven. {{HD 80606 b|HD 80606 b}}. A giant planet on one of the most stretched orbits ever found. For most of its year, it drifts far from its star. Then it plunges in, much closer than Mercury is to our Sun.

> i: hot jupiter | v: exoplanet
When Spitzer watched it swing past, its temperature shot up by hundreds of degrees in just a few hours. That sudden heating must drive storms of unimaginable violence, with shock waves racing around the planet. [pause] Imagine a world where summer arrives in an afternoon and feels like a furnace.

## The Coldest And The Darkest
mood: dread
> i: ogle 2005 blg 390lb | v: icy planet
Number six. A planet with a name only an astronomer could love: {{OGLE-2005-BLG-390Lb|OGLE-2005-BLG-390Lb}}. It sits around twenty thousand light-years away, near the centre of our galaxy, circling a dim red star at a great distance.

> i: icy planet | v: frozen planet
It was found in 2005 through a strange trick of gravity, as its star briefly magnified the light of another star behind it. It is about five times the mass of the Earth, and its estimated surface temperature is around minus two hundred and twenty degrees. Cold enough to freeze the air you breathe solid. [pause] A dark, frozen ball, lit by a sun that gives it almost nothing.

> i: tres-2b | v: dark planet
Number five. {{TrES-2b|TrES-2b}}. A gas giant about the size of Jupiter, some seven hundred and fifty light-years away. It is the darkest planet ever found.

> i: dark planet | v: hot jupiter
TrES-2b reflects less than one percent of the light that hits it. That is darker than black acrylic paint. Darker than coal. Something in its scorching atmosphere, perhaps vaporised sodium and potassium, swallows almost every photon of light. [pause] If you could see it, it would look like a hole in space, glowing a faint, sullen red.

## Rain Made Of Rock And Metal
mood: dread
> i: corot-7b | v: lava planet
Number four. {{CoRoT-7b|CoRoT-7b}}. One of the first rocky planets ever found outside the solar system. It is only a little bigger than the Earth, but it orbits so close to its star that one year lasts less than a day.

> i: exoplanet lava | v: lava
The side facing the star may be covered in an ocean of lava, at well over two thousand degrees. Some models suggest the rock there is so hot it evaporates. The vapour rises, drifts to the cooler side, and condenses. [pause] And then it rains. Not water. Pebbles of rock, falling from a sky made of stone.

> i: wasp-76b | v: exoplanet atmosphere
Number three. {{WASP-76b|WASP-76b}}. A giant planet about six hundred light-years away, so close to its star that its day side reaches around two thousand four hundred degrees. That is hot enough to boil iron.

> i: hot jupiter | v: hot jupiter
And that is exactly what happens. Iron evaporates on the day side. Fierce winds carry it around to the night side, where it is cooler. And there, the iron condenses into droplets. [pause] On WASP-76b, scientists think it rains liquid iron.

> i: wasp-76b | v: exoplanet atmosphere
In 2024, astronomers reported something even stranger there. A hint of a glory, a rainbow-like ring of light, the kind you sometimes see around the shadow of a plane on clouds below. On Earth, a glory needs perfectly round droplets of water. [pause] On WASP-76b, they may be droplets of something far more dangerous.

## The Blue Marble Of Death
mood: dread
> i: hd 189733b | v: exoplanet
Number two. From a distance, {{HD 189733 b|HD 189733 b}} looks beautiful. Hubble measured its colour, and it is a deep, rich cobalt blue. A blue world, about sixty four light-years away. It could almost be the Earth.

> i: hd 189733b artist impression | v: exoplanet atmosphere
It is not. That blue comes from clouds full of tiny particles of silicate, the stuff glass is made of. The planet is so hot that these particles may fall as glass. And the winds there have been measured at around eight thousand seven hundred kilometres per hour, seven times the speed of sound.

> i: hot jupiter | v: storm
So on HD 189733 b, it may rain molten glass. Sideways. At supersonic speed. [pause] And in 2024, Webb found hydrogen sulphide in its air, the gas that makes rotten eggs smell. A shining blue jewel that would shred you with glass and smell of decay.

## Planets Around A Corpse
mood: mystery
> i: pulsar planets | v: pulsar
And number one. The first planets ever found around another star were not where anyone expected. In 1992, two astronomers discovered planets circling {{PSR B1257+12|PSR B1257+12}}. A pulsar. The spinning, collapsed core of a star that had exploded.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
A pulsar spins hundreds of times a second and sweeps beams of radiation across space like a lighthouse. Around this one, there are at least three planets. Today they have names. {{Draugr, Poltergeist and Phobetor|DRAUGR · POLTERGEIST · PHOBETOR}}. An undead creature, a ghost, and a god of nightmares.

> i: supernova remnant | v: supernova explosion
These worlds should not be there at all. A supernova should have destroyed any planets that existed before it. So these may be second-generation planets, built afterwards from the debris of the dead star. [pause] Worlds born from a grave.

> i: pulsar planets | v: neutron star
There is no sunlight there. Just a dead star the size of a city, spinning furiously, blasting its planets with radiation and a wind of charged particles. One of them is barely twice the mass of our Moon, among the smallest planets ever detected.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
They were found because the pulsar's ticking was not perfectly steady. Its pulses arrived a few thousandths of a second early, then late, then early again, as the planets tugged it back and forth. A clock so precise that a world smaller than Mercury could be detected from thousands of light-years away. [pause] Humanity's very first alien planets were heard, not seen.

> i: exoplanet artist impression | v: star field
Glass rain. Iron storms. Burning ice. A sky of stone. Worlds orbiting the dead. [pause] We used to think the Earth was ordinary. Every new planet we find tells us how rare, and how fragile, a calm blue world really is.
