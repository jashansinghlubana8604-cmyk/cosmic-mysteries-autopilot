## Stars That Were Not Stars
mood: mystery
> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
In the 1950s, radio astronomers began mapping the sky and found hundreds of sources pouring out radio waves. Most of them matched galaxies. But a few matched something that looked, through ordinary telescopes, like a single faint star.

> i: 3c 273 | v: quasar
Stars do not normally shine brightly in radio waves. These objects were labelled quasi-stellar radio sources. {{Quasars|QUASARS}}. And their light was strange. When astronomers split it into a spectrum, it showed bright lines in places that made no sense.

> i: 3c 273 | v: galaxy zoom
In 1963, astronomer Maarten Schmidt looked again at one of them, called {{3C 273|3C 273}}, and suddenly saw the pattern. The lines were perfectly normal. Hydrogen, glowing as expected. But they had all been shifted towards the red by a huge amount. [pause] The object was not a star in our galaxy. It was about two and a half billion light-years away.

> i: quasar | v: quasar
And that meant something staggering. To look like a faint star from that distance, 3C 273 had to be hundreds of times brighter than the whole Milky Way. And its brightness flickered over weeks or months. [pause] Something smaller than a solar system was outshining a hundred billion stars.

> i: virgo constellation | v: night sky
And here is something wonderful. 3C 273 is the brightest quasar in our sky. With a good backyard telescope, on a dark night, you can see it yourself, a faint bluish point in the constellation Virgo. [pause] The photons hitting your eye would have left that black hole before there was any complex life on Earth.

## The Engine
mood: dread
> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
What could do it? The answer, worked out over the following decades, is a supermassive black hole, feeding. Not the black hole itself. Black holes give off no light. It is the matter falling into it.

> i: accretion disk | v: accretion disk
As gas, dust and torn-apart stars spiral towards the black hole, they form a flat, swirling disc, the {{accretion disk|ACCRETION DISK}}. The material near the inner edge whips around at a large fraction of the speed of light. Friction heats it to hundreds of thousands of degrees.

> i: quasar artist impression | v: black hole accretion disk
And that disc blazes. Falling into a black hole is one of the most efficient ways to turn matter into energy in the whole universe. Far more efficient than the nuclear fusion that powers the Sun. [pause] A black hole eating gas can shine brighter than all the stars around it put together.

> i: quasar jet | v: black hole jet
Some quasars also fire jets of particles out of their poles, at almost the speed of light, stretching for hundreds of thousands of light-years. When one of these jets points straight at us, we see an even more extreme object, a blazar, flickering wildly. [pause] All of it powered by a hole you could never see directly.

## The Record Breaker
mood: dread
> i: quasar | v: quasar
Which brings us back to the brightest object ever found. Its name is {{J0529-4351|J0529-4351}}. Strangely, it had been sitting in astronomical surveys for decades, mistaken for an ordinary star in our galaxy because it was so bright.

> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
In 2023, a team in Australia used the Very Large Telescope in Chile to measure its light. Its true distance revealed its true power. The disc swirling around its black hole is estimated to be about seven light-years across, roughly fifteen thousand times the distance from the Sun to Neptune.

> i: quasar black hole | v: black hole accretion disk
Inside that disc, the temperature reaches around ten thousand degrees, with winds strong enough to rip gas away at thousands of kilometres per second. One of the astronomers described it as the most violent place we know in the universe. [pause] And the black hole at its heart is still eating, about one Sun's worth of matter every day.

## The Age Of Quasars
mood: mystery
> i: early universe | v: early universe
Here is a strange thing. Almost all quasars are far away, which means we are seeing them as they were long ago. Quasars were most common around ten billion years ago, when the universe was busy, young and full of gas. Today, very few are left.

> i: galaxy merger | v: galaxy merger
Back then, galaxies were colliding constantly, pouring gas towards their central black holes. The black holes feasted and blazed. Then, over billions of years, the fuel ran low, and most of them went quiet. [pause] The quasar age is mostly over. We are living in its afterglow.

> i: milky way center | v: milky way center
Even our own Milky Way may once have had a much more active centre. Astronomers have found two gigantic bubbles of hot gas above and below the plane of our galaxy, possibly blown out by an outburst from our black hole a few million years ago. Today, it is quiet. [pause] But every big galaxy may have had its quasar years.

> i: quasar | v: early universe
And some quasars appeared astonishingly early. Webb and other telescopes have found quasars shining less than seven hundred million years after the Big Bang, already powered by black holes more than a billion times the mass of the Sun. How they grew that fast is one of the biggest puzzles in astronomy.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
Quasars also shape the galaxies around them. Their radiation and winds can heat and blow away the gas a galaxy needs to make new stars. Many astronomers think this is why the biggest galaxies stopped forming stars long ago. [pause] The black hole at the centre may decide the fate of a hundred billion suns.

## Lighthouses Across Time
mood: wonder
> i: quasar | v: deep space flight
Quasars are not just monsters. They are tools. Because they are so bright, their light can be seen across almost the entire universe. And on its way to us, that light passes through clouds of gas, galaxies and the thin web of matter between them.

> i: intergalactic gas | v: cosmic web
Each cloud it crosses leaves its fingerprint, a dark line in the quasar's light. By reading those lines, astronomers have mapped gas that is completely invisible otherwise. [pause] Quasars are cosmic lighthouses, shining through billions of years of history.

> i: gravitational lens quasar | v: gravitational lensing
In 1979, astronomers even found the same quasar appearing twice in the sky, its light split by a galaxy in front of it. It was the first gravitational lens ever discovered, proof that gravity really does bend light across the universe, just as Einstein said.

> i: quasar artist impression | v: black hole accretion disk
So when you hear the word black hole, remember that the most brilliant lights in the universe come from them. Not from the darkness itself, but from everything screaming as it falls in. [pause] Five hundred trillion Suns' worth of light, from matter making its final journey.
