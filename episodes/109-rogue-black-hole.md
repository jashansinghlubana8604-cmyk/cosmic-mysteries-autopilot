> i: rogue black hole | v: black hole
Somewhere between us and the centre of our galaxy, about five thousand light-years away, there is a black hole moving through space. It weighs about seven times as much as the Sun. It is completely alone.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
It has no companion star to feed on. No glowing disc of gas around it. It gives off no light at all. You could point every telescope on Earth directly at it and see nothing.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: gravitational lensing
And yet, astronomers found it. Not by seeing it, but by watching what it did to a star far behind it. In 2011, it drifted in front of that star, and for a few months, its gravity bent and magnified the star's light. [pause] The star brightened. And then, ever so slightly, it moved.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
It took six years of Hubble observations and years of analysis. But in 2022, astronomers announced it: the first isolated black hole ever confirmed, wandering through the Milky Way. [pause] And there could be a hundred million more like it.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
A black hole of seven Suns is not big. Its event horizon, the point of no return, would be only around forty kilometres across. Smaller than many cities. [pause] Seven times the mass of our star, packed into a dark sphere you could drive around in an hour.

## The Invisible Population
mood: mystery
> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
When a star much heavier than the Sun dies, its core can collapse into a black hole. Our galaxy has been making stars for more than ten billion years. So astronomers estimate there should be something like a {{hundred million|100 MILLION}} stellar black holes in the Milky Way.

> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
But almost all the black holes we know about are found in pairs, orbiting a living star. They pull gas off their companion, which heats up and glows in X-rays, revealing them. Others have been found by the way they tug a companion star back and forth.

> i: star field | v: star field
A black hole on its own does almost nothing that we can see. It drifts through the galaxy's thin gas, swallowing a tiny trickle. [pause] For a long time, the vast majority of the galaxy's black holes were completely hidden from us.

> i: star field | v: star field
Even its tiny trickle of feeding may be almost invisible. Gas falling into a lone black hole might give off a faint glow of X-rays or radio waves, but so weak that, at thousands of light-years, it disappears among countless other faint sources. [pause] The perfect hiding place is simply to do nothing.

## A Lens Made Of Darkness
mood: wonder
> i: gravitational lensing | v: gravitational lensing
There is one way to catch them. Einstein showed that gravity bends light. If a heavy object passes almost exactly in front of a more distant star, its gravity acts like a lens, focusing the star's light. The star appears to brighten for a while, then fade back to normal.

> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
This is called {{microlensing|MICROLENSING}}. Surveys like OGLE, run from Chile, and MOA, run from New Zealand, watch hundreds of millions of stars towards the crowded centre of the galaxy, waiting for these brightenings. They find thousands of events every year, mostly caused by ordinary dim stars passing in front. The same technique has also revealed planets drifting through space without any star at all, rogue worlds betrayed only by a brief flicker of magnified light.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: star field
On the second of June 2011, both surveys spotted an event in the constellation Sagittarius. A star about nineteen thousand light-years away began to brighten. And it kept brightening, for months, in an event lasting around two hundred and seventy days. [pause] Long events hint at heavy lenses. And nothing could be seen at the lens position.

## Watching A Star Move
mood: mystery
> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
The brightening alone could not tell astronomers how heavy the lens was. For that, they needed something much harder to measure. When gravity bends the light of a background star, it does not just brighten it. It also shifts the star's apparent position in the sky, by a tiny amount.

> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
So a team led by astronomer Kailash Sahu used Hubble to photograph the star again and again over six years, measuring its position with extraordinary precision. The shift they were looking for was about a thousandth of an arcsecond. [pause] Like measuring the width of a coin on the Moon.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole
They found it. The star's position wobbled exactly as expected for a lens of about {{seven solar masses|7 SOLAR MASSES}}. And yet the lens gave off no light. A star that heavy would have been easily visible. Only one thing could be that heavy, that compact and that dark. [pause] A black hole.

> i: rogue black hole | v: black hole
Another team, analysing the same event, initially found a lower mass, which could have meant a neutron star instead. With more data and refined analysis, the results converged. Most astronomers now agree it is a black hole, drifting through space at about forty five kilometres per second.

## Alone In The Dark
mood: dread
> i: supernova remnant | v: supernova explosion
How did it end up alone? When massive stars collapse, the explosion can kick the newborn black hole, sending it flying. Or it may once have had a companion that was flung away. Its fairly modest speed suggests it did not receive a huge kick at birth.

> i: rogue black hole | v: black hole
Now it travels the galaxy unseen. If it passes through a cloud of gas, it may briefly feed and glow faintly. Otherwise, it simply moves on, its presence revealed only when it happens to line up with a distant star. [pause] A ghost, visible only by its shadow on starlight.

> i: solar system | v: solar system
If it ever drifted close to another star system, its gravity could wreak havoc, scattering comets, disturbing planetary orbits, perhaps even flinging worlds out into space. Thankfully, space is so vast that such close encounters are extremely rare. [pause] But over the billions of years it will wander, it may pass through many star systems' outskirts.

> i: star field | v: star field
And there are many more. Statistics suggest that some isolated black holes could be surprisingly close to us, perhaps within a hundred light-years or so. We simply have not found them. [pause] They could be drifting through our stellar neighbourhood right now.

## Hunting More Ghosts
mood: wonder
> i: roman space telescope | v: spacecraft
The next great hunter is NASA's {{Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope|ROMAN SPACE TELESCOPE}}. It will watch hundreds of millions of stars towards the galactic centre, repeatedly, from space. Scientists expect it to catch many microlensing events, including some caused by isolated black holes.

> i: gaia spacecraft | v: gaia
The European Space Agency's Gaia mission has also found black holes, not alone, but quietly orbiting stars without feeding on them. Some of them are only a couple of thousand light-years away. [pause] Little by little, the hidden population of black holes is being revealed.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
So the next time you look up at the Milky Way, remember. Among those hundreds of billions of stars, there are perhaps a hundred million black holes, drifting silently between them. [pause] We have found one of the lonely ones. The rest are still out there, waiting to pass in front of a star.
