## The Puzzles
mood: mystery
> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
By the 1970s, the Big Bang theory was well established. The universe was expanding from a hot, dense beginning nearly fourteen billion years ago. But the theory left some deep puzzles unexplained.

> i: universe | v: universe expanding
The first was the one we began with, the {{horizon problem|HORIZON PROBLEM}}. Distant parts of the universe have never been in contact, yet they share the same temperature, as if they had once been mixed together like milk stirred into coffee.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy
The second was the {{flatness problem|FLATNESS PROBLEM}}. The amount of matter and energy in the universe is balanced almost exactly on a knife edge. Too much, and the universe would have collapsed back on itself long ago. Too little, and it would have flown apart before any stars could form. [pause] Something seemed to have tuned it with incredible precision.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
And there was a third puzzle. Theories of particle physics predicted that the early universe should have made huge numbers of strange particles called magnetic monopoles. [pause] Nobody has ever found one.

> i: big bang | v: big bang
The standard Big Bang theory could not explain any of this. It simply had to assume the universe began that way. [pause] Physicists wanted a reason.

## The Great Stretch
mood: epic
> i: universe expanding | v: universe expanding
Guth's idea was that, for a brief moment, the young universe was filled with a strange kind of energy that made space itself expand exponentially, doubling in size again and again.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
In a tiny fraction of a second, less than a trillionth of a trillionth of a trillionth of a second, a region far smaller than a proton may have been stretched to something like the size of a grapefruit or larger. That is a growth of at least {{a hundred trillion trillion times|100 TRILLION TRILLION}}.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: universe expanding
That is faster than light, but it does not break Einstein's rule. Nothing was moving through space faster than light. [pause] Space itself was growing, carrying everything along with it.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
Physicists often picture the energy behind inflation as a ball resting on a high, nearly flat plateau. While the ball sits there, space inflates. Slowly it rolls towards the edge, and when it finally drops into the valley below, inflation stops. [pause] Guth's first version had a flaw: it ended too unevenly. Within a couple of years, Andrei Linde in Moscow, and Andreas Albrecht and Paul Steinhardt in the United States, found ways to let it end smoothly. Alexei Starobinsky in the Soviet Union had already proposed a related model.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Inflation solves the puzzles. Regions that look far apart today were once close enough to mix and settle to the same temperature, before inflation flung them apart. Any curvature was stretched flat, like a balloon blown up so large its surface looks flat to an ant. [pause] And any monopoles would have been spread so thin that we would never expect to find one.

> i: big bang | v: big bang
When inflation ended, the energy driving it turned into a hot soup of particles and light. [pause] That is the moment many cosmologists would call the hot Big Bang.

## Seeds Of Galaxies
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
There was an even bigger payoff. In the quantum world, nothing is perfectly still. Even empty space constantly fizzes with tiny random fluctuations.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
During inflation, these microscopic quantum ripples were stretched to enormous sizes, leaving behind slight differences in density from place to place. [pause] Some regions ended up with just a tiny bit more matter than others.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Over billions of years, gravity pulled more matter into those slightly denser regions. They grew into clouds, then into galaxies, clusters and the vast cosmic web. [pause] If inflation is right, every galaxy in the universe began as a quantum flicker, smaller than an atom.

> i: planck cmb | v: cosmic microwave background
And we can see those seeds. They are the faint hot and cold spots in the afterglow of the Big Bang, mapped by the satellites {{COBE|COBE}}, {{WMAP|WMAP}} and {{Planck|PLANCK}}. Inflation made specific predictions about their pattern and sizes. [pause] The measurements match them remarkably well.

## The Announcement
mood: mystery
> i: south pole telescope | v: antarctica
But there was one prediction still missing. Inflation should also have shaken space itself, creating gravitational waves that would leave a faint twisting pattern in the polarisation of the Big Bang's afterglow.

> i: bicep2 telescope | v: antarctica telescope
In March 2014, a team using a telescope at the South Pole called {{BICEP2|BICEP2}} announced that they had found it. It was front-page news around the world. A video showed one of inflation's pioneers, Andrei Linde, opening his door and being told the news. [pause] It looked like the discovery of the century.

> i: milky way dust | v: interstellar dust
Then came the doubts. The same twisting pattern can be produced by dust in our own galaxy. When the team combined their data with the Planck satellite's measurements of that dust, the signal could be fully explained by dust. [pause] The discovery evaporated.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The signal from inflation may still be there, only fainter. New telescopes in Chile and at the South Pole are searching for it. A Japanese-led satellite called LiteBIRD is planned to map the whole sky in search of it. [pause] If they find it, it would be direct evidence of the universe's first moment.

## Is It True?
mood: mystery
> i: universe | v: universe expanding
Most cosmologists today accept inflation as the best explanation we have. But not everyone. Some critics, including one of the theory's early contributors, Paul Steinhardt, argue that it can be adjusted to fit almost any observation, which makes it hard to test.

> i: multiverse | v: multiverse
There is another twist. Many versions of inflation suggest that once it starts, it never fully stops. Across the wider cosmos, it might keep going forever, with bubble universes forming where it ends. [pause] Our observable universe might be just one bubble in an endless sea.

> i: big bang | v: big bang
And inflation does not explain where the universe itself came from. It describes what happened in the first fraction of a second, but not what, if anything, came before.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
Still, it remains a remarkable idea. In a fraction of a second shorter than any clock could ever measure, the universe grew from smaller than an atom to larger than we can see. [pause] And somewhere in that instant, a few random quantum ripples were frozen into the shape of everything that would ever exist. Including the galaxy you live in.
