## From Our Sun To The Giants
mood: wonder
> v: sun | i: sun
The Sun is a fairly ordinary star, about one point four million kilometres across. Most stars in our galaxy are actually smaller, dim red dwarfs that would be almost invisible from here.

> i: sirius | v: sirius
The brightest star in the night sky, Sirius, is about one point seven times wider than the Sun, and twenty five times brighter. It looks so bright mainly because it is close, just eight and a half light years away.

> v: rigel | i: rigel orion
In the constellation Orion is Rigel, a blue supergiant nearly eighty times wider than the Sun, shining with the light of over a hundred thousand Suns. Stars like this live fast. Rigel is only around eight million years old, and it is already near the end of its life.

> i: orion constellation | v: orion constellation
Across Orion from Rigel is a star that looks distinctly orange red. It is one of the most famous stars in the sky, and one of the largest we can see with our own eyes. [pause] {{Betelgeuse|BETELGEUSE}}.

## The Star That Dimmed
mood: mystery
> v: betelgeuse | i: betelgeuse
Betelgeuse is a red supergiant, around seven hundred and fifty times wider than the Sun. Placed in our solar system, its surface would extend beyond the orbit of Mars, and possibly as far as the asteroid belt.

> i: betelgeuse surface | v: betelgeuse surface
It is so close, about five hundred and fifty light years away, and so large that telescopes can actually resolve its surface, a seething, bubbling layer of enormous convection cells, each larger than our Sun.

> v: betelgeuse dimming | i: betelgeuse great dimming
In late 2019, something alarming happened. Betelgeuse began to fade. By February 2020, it had lost around two thirds of its brightness, and was visibly dimmer to anyone looking up. People wondered if it was about to explode. Astronomers called it {{the Great Dimming|THE GREAT DIMMING}}.

> i: betelgeuse dust | v: star dust ejection
It did not explode. Observations showed that the star had blasted off a huge cloud of material from its surface, which cooled into dust and temporarily blocked its light. Betelgeuse has since recovered. And in 2025, astronomers announced they had found evidence of a small companion star orbiting it, close to its surface, which may explain some of its strange behaviour.

> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova
Betelgeuse will explode as a supernova one day, probably within the next hundred thousand years. When it does, it will shine as brightly as a half Moon in our sky for weeks, and be visible during the day. At its distance, it poses no danger to us.

> i: antares | v: antares
In the summer sky, in the heart of the constellation Scorpius, is another red supergiant you can see with your own eyes. Antares, whose name means rival of Mars, because of its colour. It is around seven hundred times wider than the Sun. Like Betelgeuse, it is a dying giant, and one day it too will explode.

## The True Monsters
mood: wonder
> i: red supergiant | v: red hypergiant
But Betelgeuse is not the largest. There are stars that are larger still, so big they challenge our ability to measure them.

> v: red supergiant | i: vy canis majoris
VY Canis Majoris is a red hypergiant about three thousand eight hundred light years away. It is around fourteen hundred times wider than the Sun. It is losing mass at a staggering rate, throwing off huge arcs and clumps of gas, and is surrounded by a vast cloud of its own debris.

> i: uy scuti | v: red supergiant
UY Scuti was once thought to be the largest known star, around seventeen hundred times the Sun's width. Better distance measurements later shrank it to roughly nine hundred. This is one of the challenges: these stars are so distant, and their edges so diffuse, that their sizes are uncertain.

> v: stephenson 2-18 | i: red supergiant star cluster
Then there is {{Stephenson 2-18|STEPHENSON 2-18}}, in a cluster of red supergiants around nineteen thousand light years away. Some estimates suggest it is over two thousand times wider than the Sun. If it replaced our Sun, its surface could reach out beyond the orbit of Saturn. Light, travelling at three hundred thousand kilometres every second, would need around nine hours just to circle its surface once.

> i: wohg64 | v: red supergiant
In 2024, astronomers captured the first close-up image of a star in another galaxy, a red supergiant called WOH G64 in the Large Magellanic Cloud. It is around fifteen hundred times wider than the Sun, wrapped in an egg shaped cocoon of dust, and may be changing before our eyes, as it nears the end of its life.

## The Heaviest Star
mood: mystery
> v: tarantula nebula | i: tarantula nebula
Size is not the same as mass. The largest stars are swollen and thin, their outer layers less dense than the air around us. The heaviest stars are more compact, and far more ferocious.

> i: r136 cluster | v: r136 star cluster
In the Tarantula Nebula, a stellar nursery in the Large Magellanic Cloud, sits a dense cluster called R136. Inside it is {{R136a1|R136a1}}, the most massive star known. It may be around two hundred times the mass of the Sun, shining with millions of times its light.

> v: stellar winds | i: massive star
Stars this heavy should barely be able to form. As a star grows, its own radiation pushes back the gas falling onto it. Above a certain limit, stars may blow themselves apart before they finish forming. R136a1 sits close to that limit, and astronomers still debate exactly how such giants are born.

## The Limits Of Size
mood: wonder
> i: red supergiant | v: red supergiant surface
Is there a limit to how big a star can be? It seems there is. Stars much larger than about fifteen hundred times the Sun's width become unstable. Their outer layers are so loosely held that they drift away, and the star sheds mass until it shrinks back below the limit.

> v: supernova remnant | i: supernova remnant
That is why the largest stars we know cluster around that size, and why the very biggest estimates are so hard to confirm. These giants live for only a few million years, a blink compared to the Sun's ten billion. Then they collapse, explode, and scatter heavy elements across the galaxy.

> i: crab nebula | v: crab nebula
The iron in your blood, the calcium in your bones, and the oxygen you breathe were made inside stars like these, and blasted into space when they died. [pause] We are made of the remains of giants.

> v: zoom out milky way | i: milky way
And somewhere out there, in the dust lanes of our own galaxy or in galaxies we have barely begun to study, there may be stars larger than any we have measured, waiting to be found.
