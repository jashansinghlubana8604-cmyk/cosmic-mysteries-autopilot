Look up on a clear, dark night, and you can see a few thousand stars. But that is a tiny sample. Our galaxy, the Milky Way, holds somewhere between one and four hundred billion of them.

> i: exoplanet artist impression | v: exoplanet
We now know that most of those stars have planets. Billions of them are rocky worlds, in the zone where liquid water could exist on the surface. Many are billions of years older than the Earth.

> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
So by now, the galaxy should be humming with life. With signals. With visitors. There should be traces everywhere. [pause] And yet, when we listen, there is nothing. No signal. No probe. No ruins. Just silence.

> i: milky way galaxy | v: milky way galaxy
In 1950, one of the greatest physicists of the twentieth century looked at this problem and asked a question so simple it has haunted scientists ever since. [pause] Where is everybody?

## A Question Over Lunch
mood: mystery
> i: los alamos | v: desert
Summer, 1950. Los Alamos, New Mexico, the laboratory where the atomic bomb had been built a few years earlier. A group of physicists walked to lunch together, chatting about a recent wave of flying saucer reports and a cartoon in a magazine that joked about aliens stealing rubbish bins.

> i: enrico fermi | v: physics laboratory
Among them was {{Enrico Fermi|ENRICO FERMI}}, a Nobel Prize winner famous for working out answers to enormous questions in his head, with rough numbers on the back of an envelope. The conversation moved on. Then, in the middle of lunch, Fermi suddenly said: but where is everybody?

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
His colleagues laughed, because they knew exactly what he meant. Fermi had been running the numbers. The galaxy is enormous, and incredibly old. Even if only a tiny fraction of stars had planets, and only a tiny fraction of those had life, there should be civilisations out there. [pause] And they should have had plenty of time to reach us.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
That clash, between how likely aliens seem and how completely absent they are, became known as the {{Fermi paradox|THE FERMI PARADOX}}. More than seventy years later, nobody has solved it.

## The Numbers Do Not Lie
mood: wonder
> i: milky way galaxy | v: milky way galaxy
Here is why the silence is so strange. The Milky Way is about thirteen billion years old. The Earth is about four and a half billion. So there were stars with planets around them for billions of years before our Sun was even born.

> i: spacecraft deep space | v: spacecraft flight
Now imagine a civilisation that learns to build ships that travel at just one percent of the speed of light. Slower than science fiction. Not so far beyond what we can almost imagine doing. At that speed, it could cross the entire galaxy in about ten million years, even stopping to build new colonies along the way.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
Ten million years sounds like forever. But the galaxy is a thousand times older than that. One determined civilisation, at any point in that history, could have spread to every star system. [pause] Including ours.

> i: drake equation | v: radio telescope
In 1961, astronomer Frank Drake wrote down a famous equation to estimate how many civilisations might be out there. How many stars form, how many have planets, how many planets get life, how many get intelligence, how long they last. The trouble is that we know only the first few numbers. [pause] For the rest, the answer could be millions, or just one. Us.

## Have We Even Looked?
mood: mystery
> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
In 1960, Frank Drake made the first real attempt to listen. He pointed a radio dish in West Virginia at two nearby Sun-like stars, for a few hours each day, over several months. He heard nothing.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
Since then, searches have grown much bigger. Projects like Breakthrough Listen use some of the largest radio telescopes on Earth to scan millions of stars and many frequencies. And still, not a single confirmed signal from anyone else.

> i: ocean | v: ocean waves
But here is the catch. In 2018, astronomers tried to measure how much of the sky, and how many frequencies, we have really searched. Their answer: if the galaxy were the Earth's oceans, we have so far checked roughly {{a hot tub's worth|A HOT TUB}} of water. [pause] If you scooped a hot tub of water out of the ocean and found no fish, you would not conclude that the ocean is empty.

> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
So one answer to the paradox is simply this: we have barely started. The silence may not be silence at all. We may just not have been listening in the right place, at the right time, in the right way.

## The Answers
mood: dread
> i: earth from space | v: earth
But many scientists think the silence is too deep to be explained by that alone. So they have proposed answers. Some are hopeful. Some are deeply unsettling.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
What about the strange lights people report in the sky? In 2023, a panel of experts assembled by NASA studied unexplained sightings and found no evidence that any of them came from beyond the Earth. Most turn out to be balloons, drones, aircraft, satellites or tricks of the camera. [pause] Whatever the answer to Fermi's question is, it is not hiding in blurry videos.

> i: earth | v: earth rotation
One is that life itself is rare. The Earth may be extraordinarily lucky, with its large Moon, its magnetic field, its plate tectonics, its steady Sun. Simple microbes might be common, but the leap to complex, intelligent life might almost never happen. [pause] We might be one of very few, or even the only ones.

> i: zoo hypothesis | v: earth from space
Another is the {{zoo hypothesis|ZOO HYPOTHESIS}}. Maybe they are out there, and they know about us, but they have chosen to stay hidden. Watching. Like scientists observing wildlife without disturbing it. Or waiting to see whether we are ready.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
A darker idea is that every civilisation that has ever existed has stayed silent on purpose, because the galaxy is dangerous. That revealing yourself is the surest way to be destroyed. [pause] And the darkest idea of all is that civilisations do not last. That something, somewhere along the road from microbes to starships, almost always ends them.

## Early, Or Alone
mood: wonder
> i: early universe | v: early universe
There is one more answer, and it is strangely comforting. Maybe we are early. The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old, but stars like the Sun will keep shining for trillions of years. On that scale, the cosmos has barely woken up.

> i: milky way galaxy | v: milky way time-lapse
Perhaps civilisations are only now starting to appear, in many places at once, and none has had time to spread yet. In a few hundred million years, the galaxy might be crowded. [pause] We might be among the first voices, in a universe that is just beginning to speak.

> i: earth from space | v: earth night
Either answer is astonishing. If we are alone, then everything that thinks and feels and wonders in our part of the universe exists on one small planet, and it matters more than we can imagine. If we are not alone, then somewhere out there, someone else is looking up at their own sky, asking the same question. [pause] Where is everybody?

> i: radio telescope night | v: radio telescope
Fermi's question was asked over lunch, in a single sentence. It may take the rest of human history to answer it. Or it might be answered next year, by a faint, steady signal from a star we have never noticed. [pause] Until then, we keep listening.
