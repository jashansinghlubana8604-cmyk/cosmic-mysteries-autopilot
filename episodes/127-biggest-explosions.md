On the thirtieth of October 1961, the Soviet Union detonated the largest bomb ever built by humans. Its blast was equivalent to about fifty million tonnes of TNT. The fireball was visible a thousand kilometres away. The shock wave circled the Earth several times.

> i: nuclear explosion | v: explosion

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion

## Our Own Star
mood: dread
> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
Let's start close to home. Our Sun is not a quiet ball of light. Its surface is laced with twisted magnetic fields, and when they suddenly snap and reconnect, they release a {{solar flare|SOLAR FLARE}}.

> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
The largest solar flares release energy equivalent to tens of millions of Tsar Bombas, all in a matter of minutes. And that is still tiny compared with the Sun's ordinary glow. Every single second, just by shining, the Sun gives off the energy of about two billion Tsar Bombas. [pause] And yet the Sun is a calm, middle-aged, ordinary star.

> i: red dwarf flare | v: red dwarf flare
Some stars are far wilder. Young, active red dwarfs and other stars can produce superflares thousands of times stronger than anything seen on our Sun. [pause] And that is just the warm-up.

## Dying Stars
mood: dread
> i: nova | v: nova
Higher on the list is the nova. In a pair of stars, a white dwarf steals gas from its companion until it ignites in a runaway nuclear explosion across its whole surface. For a few days, it can shine tens of thousands of times brighter than the Sun.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Then comes the true giant: the {{supernova|SUPERNOVA}}. When a massive star runs out of fuel, its core collapses in less than a second, and the rest of the star is blasted apart. For a few weeks, a single supernova can outshine an entire galaxy of a hundred billion stars.

> i: supernova remnant | v: supernova remnant
In a typical supernova, the light is only a tiny part of the energy. Most of it pours out as neutrinos, ghostly particles that pass straight through planets. The total energy released in a few seconds can exceed what our Sun will produce in its entire ten billion year life, a hundred times over. [pause] It is so violent that it forges new elements and scatters them across space.

> i: kilonova | v: neutron star merger
And when two neutron stars, the collapsed cores of dead stars, collide, they produce a {{kilonova|KILONOVA}}, flinging out material rich in gold, platinum and other heavy elements. [pause] Some of the most precious metals on Earth were made in explosions like these.

## The Monster Deaths
mood: dread
> i: superluminous supernova | v: supernova explosion
Some stars die even more spectacularly. Superluminous supernovae can shine ten to a hundred times brighter than ordinary ones, perhaps powered by a newborn magnetar spinning furiously at the centre, or by a star so massive that it tears itself apart in a special kind of explosion.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Theory predicts that the very heaviest stars, more than about a hundred and thirty times the mass of the Sun, might die in what is called a pair-instability supernova. Their cores become so hot that they lose the pressure holding them up, collapse, and ignite in a runaway thermonuclear blast that destroys the entire star, leaving nothing behind. [pause] Not even a black hole.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
Then there are {{gamma-ray bursts|GAMMA-RAY BURSTS}}. When the core of a very massive, rapidly spinning star collapses into a black hole, it can blast two narrow jets out into space at almost the speed of light. If one points at the Earth, we see a flash of gamma rays brighter than anything else in the sky.

> i: gamma ray burst artist impression | v: black hole jet
In October 2022, a gamma-ray burst nicknamed the BOAT, the brightest of all time, reached us from about two billion light-years away. It was so intense that it disturbed the Earth's upper atmosphere. [pause] A single star's death, felt across a fifth of the observable universe.

## Colliding Black Holes
mood: mystery
> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
Now we leave light behind. In 2015, detectors on Earth felt a ripple in space itself, sent out by two black holes, each around thirty times the mass of the Sun, spiralling together and merging over a billion light-years away.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
In a fraction of a second, about three times the mass of the Sun was converted directly into energy, in the form of gravitational waves. At its peak, that merger was releasing more power than all the stars in the observable universe shining together. [pause] And it produced no light at all.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
Since then, hundreds of such collisions have been detected. Some involve black holes over a hundred times the mass of the Sun. [pause] The loudest explosions in the universe are completely silent to our eyes.

> i: black hole merger | v: gravitational waves
One signal, detected in 2019 and called GW190521, came from two black holes of roughly eighty five and sixty six times the mass of the Sun, merging into one of about a hundred and forty. Around eight Suns' worth of mass vanished into gravitational waves. [pause] It was so heavy that astronomers are still debating how such black holes could form.

## The Biggest Of All
mood: dread
> i: galaxy cluster eruption | v: galaxy cluster
And at the top of the list are the eruptions of supermassive black holes. At the centres of giant galaxy clusters, black holes weighing billions of Suns occasionally fire immense jets into the hot gas around them, carving out enormous cavities.

> i: ms 0735 cavity | v: galaxy cluster
In 2005, astronomers found a cluster called MS 0735 where a black hole had blown two giant bubbles in the gas, each larger than our galaxy. The energy needed was equivalent to hundreds of millions of gamma-ray bursts. [pause] It had been the biggest explosion ever seen.

> i: ophiuchus cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Then, in 2020, astronomers studying the {{Ophiuchus galaxy cluster|OPHIUCHUS CLUSTER}}, about three hundred and ninety million light-years away, found a curved edge in the hot gas so large they first assumed it could not be part of a cavity. Radio observations confirmed it. A black hole had carved a cavity big enough to fit fifteen Milky Way galaxies in a row. [pause] The eruption released about five times more energy than the one in MS 0735.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Tsar Bomba. A solar flare. A supernova. A gamma-ray burst. Colliding black holes. A black hole eruption that reshaped a cluster of galaxies. [pause] Every one of them dwarfed by the next. And the Big Bang itself is not on the list, because it was not an explosion in space at all, but the beginning of space itself.
