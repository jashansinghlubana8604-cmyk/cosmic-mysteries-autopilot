Of all the stars in the night sky, one is closer to us than any other. It is so faint that you cannot see it without a telescope. It sits in the southern constellation Centaurus, about four point two light-years away.

> i: proxima centauri | v: red dwarf
Its name is Proxima Centauri. Proxima, meaning nearest. And in 2016, astronomers announced that it has a planet. A rocky world, about the mass of the Earth, orbiting in the zone where water could be liquid on its surface.

> i: proxima b artist impression | v: exoplanet
It is the closest planet to us outside our solar system. If we ever send a probe to another world around another star, this is almost certainly where it will go first. [pause] Our nearest neighbour might be a second Earth.

> i: red dwarf flare | v: red dwarf flare
Or it might be a scorched rock, blasted for billions of years by one of the most violent kinds of star in the galaxy. [pause] This is Proxima b. And it may be the most important planet we know of.

## The Nearest Star
mood: wonder
> i: alpha centauri | v: star field
Proxima Centauri was discovered in 1915 by a Scottish-born astronomer named Robert Innes, working in South Africa. He noticed a faint red star moving across the sky together with the bright pair of stars called Alpha Centauri. It was part of the same family.

> i: proxima centauri | v: red dwarf
Proxima is a {{red dwarf|RED DWARF}}. It has about an eighth of the Sun's mass and gives off well under one percent of its light, mostly as invisible infrared. If you replaced our Sun with Proxima, it would look like a dim orange ember in the sky.

> i: alpha centauri | v: alpha centauri
It orbits two much brighter stars, Alpha Centauri A and B, which are similar to our Sun. But it orbits them from very far away, so far that one lap takes it hundreds of thousands of years. [pause] Our nearest stellar neighbours are a triple system, and Proxima is the little outsider.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Four point two light-years sounds close. It is about forty trillion kilometres. If you could drive there in a car at highway speed, it would take tens of millions of years. Voyager 1, our fastest outbound spacecraft, would need around seventy five thousand years.

## The Pale Red Dot
mood: mystery
> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
For years, astronomers suspected Proxima might have planets, but none showed up. In 2016, a team led by Guillem Anglada-Escudé ran a campaign they called {{the Pale Red Dot|PALE RED DOT}}, after Carl Sagan's famous photograph of the Earth.

> i: harps spectrograph | v: telescope dome
They used an instrument in Chile called HARPS to measure Proxima's light night after night, looking for a tiny back-and-forth wobble. A planet orbiting a star tugs it slightly towards us, then away. The wobble they found was about one and a half metres per second. [pause] Slower than walking pace, measured from more than four light-years away.

> i: proxima b artist impression | v: exoplanet orbit
The wobble repeated every eleven point two days. That was the year of a new planet, {{Proxima b|PROXIMA b}}. Later measurements, with an even more precise instrument called ESPRESSO, showed that it has at least about one point one times the mass of the Earth.

> i: proxima b | v: exoplanet
It orbits just seven or eight million kilometres from its star, far closer than Mercury is to our Sun. But because Proxima is so dim, that puts the planet in the habitable zone. It gets about two thirds of the light the Earth receives. [pause] On paper, it is in the right place.

## A Violent Little Star
mood: dread
> i: red dwarf flare | v: red dwarf flare
And then there is the problem. Red dwarfs like Proxima are notoriously violent, especially when young. They are covered in powerful magnetic fields, and they erupt with flares far more often, and far more fiercely for their size, than our Sun.

> i: proxima centauri flare | v: solar flare
In 2016, a sky survey caught Proxima in a superflare so bright that, for a few moments, the star would have been visible to the naked eye from a dark site. In 2019, astronomers watched another flare in which its ultraviolet light surged by more than ten thousand times in seconds.

> i: proxima b artist impression | v: exoplanet atmosphere
Proxima b sits so close that each of these flares hits it with a blast of radiation and charged particles. Over billions of years, that could have stripped away any atmosphere it once had. And without an atmosphere, there is no liquid water on the surface. [pause] Only a bare, sterilised rock.

> i: tidally locked planet | v: exoplanet rotation
The planet is also probably tidally locked, with one side facing its star forever and the other in endless night. If it does have thick air, winds could carry heat around and make that bearable. If it does not, the day side would bake and the night side would freeze. [pause] We do not know which it is.

## The View From Proxima b
mood: wonder
> i: proxima b surface | v: exoplanet surface
Imagine standing on Proxima b, on the boundary between day and night. Proxima hangs low on the horizon, never moving, appearing about three times wider than the Sun looks from the Earth. A swollen, reddish sun, flickering now and then.

> i: alpha centauri | v: night sky stars
And in the sky above, two dazzling stars sit close together. Alpha Centauri A and B, our Sun's near twins. From Proxima b, they would shine so brightly you could read by their light, brighter than any star in the Earth's sky.

> i: cassiopeia constellation | v: night sky
And somewhere in the constellation we call Cassiopeia, there would be one more bright yellow star. Ordinary looking. Easy to miss. [pause] Our Sun. With the Earth, and everyone we have ever known, too small and too faint to see.

> i: proxima d | v: exoplanet orbit
Proxima b is not alone. In 2022, astronomers confirmed a smaller planet, {{Proxima d|PROXIMA d}}, about a quarter of the Earth's mass, circling even closer to the star. A third, much more distant planet has been suggested too, but that one is still debated.

## Can We Ever Go?
mood: wonder
> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
Proxima b does not pass in front of its star from our point of view, which makes its atmosphere very hard to study. The next big hope is a giant telescope being built in Chile, the {{Extremely Large Telescope|EXTREMELY LARGE TELESCOPE}}, whose main mirror will be thirty nine metres wide. Astronomers hope it can catch the faint light reflected from the planet itself.

> i: light sail | v: light sail
And some people want to go there. A project called Breakthrough Starshot has studied the idea of sending tiny probes, each weighing about a gram, on ultra-thin sails pushed by powerful lasers from the Earth. In theory, they could reach a fifth of the speed of light, and arrive in about twenty years.

> i: light sail | v: spacecraft flight
The engineering challenges are enormous, and nobody knows if it can ever be done. But for the first time in history, people are seriously working out how to reach another star within a human lifetime. [pause] And the first target would be this one.

> i: proxima centauri | v: star field
So tonight, if you are in the southern hemisphere, find the two bright pointer stars near the Southern Cross. Just beside them, too faint to see, is the nearest star in the universe. [pause] And circling it, perhaps under a thin sky, perhaps under no sky at all, is the closest world we could ever hope to visit.
