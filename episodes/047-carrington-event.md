On the morning of the first of September 1859, an English astronomer named Richard Carrington was sketching dark spots on the Sun, projected through his telescope onto a screen. It was something he did most days. Careful. Patient. Ordinary.

> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
Then, at about quarter past eleven, two blinding points of white light suddenly burst out of the sunspots. They grew, crawled across the spots, and faded, all in about five minutes. Carrington rushed out of the room to find a witness. When he came back, it was nearly over.

> i: coronal mass ejection | v: coronal mass ejection
He had just become the first person ever to see a solar flare. And unseen, a billion-tonne cloud of charged gas had been blasted off the Sun, heading straight for the Earth. [pause] It would arrive the next day.

> i: aurora | v: aurora
That night, the sky turned red over half the planet. Telegraph lines threw sparks. Operators were shocked at their desks. Paper caught fire. [pause] And if the same storm hit us today, we may not be ready.

## The Storm Arrives
mood: dread
> i: coronal mass ejection | v: coronal mass ejection
What Carrington saw was the flash. What followed was a {{coronal mass ejection|CORONAL MASS EJECTION}}, a vast bubble of magnetised plasma torn off the Sun's atmosphere. Most of these take two to four days to reach the Earth. This one arrived in less than eighteen hours.

> i: earth magnetic field | v: earth magnetic field
When it slammed into the Earth's magnetic field, it shook it violently. The field that normally shields us was squeezed and twisted. Electric currents surged through the upper atmosphere. And energy poured down along the magnetic field lines towards the poles, and far beyond.

> i: aurora | v: aurora
The auroras that night were seen almost everywhere. Not just near the Arctic, but in Rome, in Havana, in Hawaii, close to the equator. In the Rocky Mountains, gold miners woke up in the middle of the night, saw the bright sky, and started making breakfast, thinking it was dawn.

> i: aurora red | v: aurora
In Boston, the light was so bright that people said you could read a newspaper by it at midnight. Many who had never heard of an aurora thought their city was on fire. Some thought it was the end of the world. [pause] For a night, the sky had stopped behaving like the sky.

> i: sunspots | v: sun
Carrington was not imagining things. Another English astronomer, Richard Hodgson, had been watching the Sun at the very same moment, and saw the same brilliant flash. Their two reports, published side by side, were the first scientific record of a solar flare. [pause] And the storm that followed proved that what happens on the Sun does not stay on the Sun.

## Sparks On The Wire
mood: dread
> i: telegraph | v: telegraph
In 1859, the most advanced technology in the world was the telegraph. Thousands of kilometres of copper wire, carrying messages as electric pulses. And those long wires turned out to be perfect antennas for the storm.

> i: telegraph office | v: telegraph
As the Earth's magnetic field shook, it pushed electric currents through the wires all on their own. Operators reported sparks leaping from their equipment. Some were knocked back by shocks. In a few offices, the paper used to record messages caught fire. [pause] The storm was literally running electricity through the planet's nervous system.

> i: telegraph | v: telegraph wires
And then something even stranger happened. Between Boston and Portland, operators found they could disconnect their batteries completely and keep sending messages, powered only by the current from the sky. They chatted for about two hours, using {{electricity from the Sun|POWERED BY THE SUN}}.

> i: sun | v: sun
Carrington's observation and the storm the next day were the first clue that the Sun and the Earth are electrically connected. A violent event on the Sun's surface, one hundred and fifty million kilometres away, could reach out and touch human technology. [pause] The science of space weather had begun.

## What It Would Do Today
mood: dread
> i: earth city lights | v: earth night
In 1859, the world barely depended on electricity. Today, almost everything does. Power grids, water pumping, hospitals, banking, communication, food supply chains. All of it, held together by electricity and satellites.

> i: power grid | v: power lines
A storm like Carrington's would push powerful currents through long-distance power lines. The biggest danger is to the giant transformers that keep the grid running. They are expensive, slow to build, and if many were damaged at once, replacing them could take months, or even longer.

> i: quebec blackout | v: city blackout
We have had a warning. On the thirteenth of March 1989, a much smaller solar storm hit the Canadian province of Quebec. In about ninety seconds, its power grid collapsed. {{Six million people|6 MILLION PEOPLE}} lost power for around nine hours, in the cold of a Canadian winter.

> i: satellites orbit | v: satellites orbit
Satellites would be battered too. Storms heat the upper atmosphere, making it swell and drag on spacecraft in low orbit. GPS signals could become unreliable. Radio communications would suffer. Studies have estimated that a Carrington-scale storm today could cause damage worth hundreds of billions to trillions of dollars. [pause] Nobody knows the exact number, because it has never happened to a modern world.

## The Near Miss
mood: mystery
> i: stereo spacecraft | v: stereo spacecraft
Here is a story most people never heard. On the twenty third of July 2012, the Sun launched a huge coronal mass ejection. NASA's STEREO-A spacecraft, which was orbiting the Sun ahead of the Earth, happened to be right in its path, and measured it directly.

> i: coronal mass ejection | v: coronal mass ejection
Scientists later concluded it was at least as powerful as the Carrington storm, possibly more. And it had crossed the Earth's orbit at a point where the Earth had been {{about a week earlier|ONE WEEK}}.

> i: earth orbit | v: earth orbit
If it had erupted a week sooner, it would have hit us full on. The Earth simply was not there yet. [pause] We missed the biggest solar storm of the space age by a matter of days, and most of the world never knew.

> i: aurora | v: aurora
In May 2024, a strong but much smaller storm hit the Earth. It was the most powerful in more than twenty years. Auroras were seen across Europe, North America and even near the tropics, and millions of people photographed them with their phones. [pause] It was beautiful. And it was only a fraction of what the Sun can do.

## Will It Happen Again?
mood: dread
> i: tree rings | v: forest
The answer is yes. The question is when. And the Sun has done far worse. Tree rings and ice cores record sudden spikes of radioactive carbon and beryllium, signs of colossal blasts of particles from the Sun. One, in the year {{774|774 AD}}, seems to have been many times stronger than the Carrington Event.

> i: sun solar dynamics observatory | v: sun
Estimates for how often a Carrington-scale storm happens vary a lot. Some studies have suggested a chance of roughly one in ten per decade. Others say less. But over a lifetime, the odds are not small.

> i: solar observatory | v: sun
The good news is that we are watching now. Spacecraft between the Earth and the Sun can give hours of warning before a storm arrives. Power companies can reduce the load on their grids. Satellite operators can protect their spacecraft. Many grids have been strengthened since 1989.

> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
But the Sun does not care how ready we are. Somewhere on its surface, right now, magnetic fields are twisting and storing energy. One day, a bright flash will appear above a group of sunspots, just like the one Carrington saw. [pause] And about a day later, the sky will turn red.
