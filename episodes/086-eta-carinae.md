## A Monster In Carina
mood: wonder
> i: carina nebula | v: carina nebula
Eta Carinae lies about seven and a half thousand light-years away, buried inside the Carina Nebula, one of the largest and brightest star-forming regions in the Milky Way. It is a place full of young, massive stars.

> i: carina nebula webb | v: carina nebula
The nebula itself is spectacular. Its glowing clouds stretch for hundreds of light-years, sculpted into pillars and cliffs by the fierce radiation of the stars inside. The famous Cosmic Cliffs, one of the first images ever released by the James Webb Space Telescope, are the edge of a cavity in this same vast complex. [pause] And at its bright heart sits Eta Carinae.

> i: eta carinae | v: star
Eta Carinae is actually two stars, orbiting each other every five and a half years. The bigger one is a monster. Estimates suggest it is around {{100 times|100 × THE SUN}} the mass of the Sun, and that together the pair shine with the light of around five million Suns.

> i: star size comparison | v: star size comparison
A star that heavy lives on the edge. Its own light pushes outward so strongly that it can barely hold itself together. It sheds gas in powerful winds, losing more mass every year than our Sun does in billions. Astronomers think it sits close to a theoretical ceiling called the Eddington limit, the point at which a star's own light becomes strong enough to blow it apart. Push past that line, even briefly, and the star begins to tear itself to pieces.

> i: eta carinae | v: eta carinae
When the two stars swing closest together, their fierce winds slam into each other, producing bursts of X-rays that telescopes can measure. [pause] Even in its quiet years, Eta Carinae is a violent place.

## The Great Eruption
mood: dread
> i: telescope night | v: night sky
In 1677, the astronomer Edmond Halley, of comet fame, catalogued Eta Carinae as a fairly faint star. Over the next century and a half, it flickered up and down in brightness. Then, around 1837, it began to brighten dramatically.

> i: eta carinae | v: star
The outburst lasted around twenty years. At its peak, in 1843, Eta Carinae blazed in the southern sky. Astronomers now call this event the {{Great Eruption|THE GREAT ERUPTION}}. During it, the star released almost as much light as a supernova, the explosion that marks the death of a massive star.

> i: homunculus nebula | v: nebula
But it was not a supernova. The star did not die. Instead, it blasted out an enormous amount of gas, perhaps around ten times the mass of the Sun or more, at hundreds of kilometres per second. [pause] Astronomers call events like this a supernova impostor. Something that shines like a stellar death, but leaves the star alive.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
Some historians even think the Boorong people of south-eastern Australia, who had their own detailed stories about the stars, may have woven the brightening of Eta Carinae into their oral traditions. [pause] A cosmic event, remembered in human culture, half a world away from the astronomers' notebooks.

## The Little Man
mood: wonder
> i: homunculus nebula | v: homunculus nebula
The material thrown off in the Great Eruption is still expanding today. Hubble's images show it as two huge, glowing lobes of gas and dust, bulging out from either side of the star, with a thin disc around the middle.

> i: homunculus nebula | v: eta carinae
Through early telescopes, the cloud looked a bit like a tiny human figure. So astronomers named it the {{Homunculus|THE HOMUNCULUS}}, Latin for little man. Today we know each lobe is far larger than our solar system. The dust in it is what made the star fade from view, hiding it behind its own ejected shell.

> i: carina nebula | v: carina nebula
And here is something remarkable. In 2012, astronomers found light from the Great Eruption still arriving, reflected off distant clouds of dust. Light that had taken a longer path, bouncing off clouds before coming to Earth, is reaching us more than a century and a half later. [pause] These light echoes let modern telescopes study an eruption that happened before photography was invented.

> i: eta carinae | v: star
What the echoes revealed was surprising. The light from the eruption was cooler than expected, not like a simple star blowing off a wind, but more like an explosive blast. [pause] Some scientists think the eruption may have been triggered when the star merged with a third companion, or when a violent instability shook it from inside.

## Waiting For The End
mood: dread
> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Eta Carinae survived its eruption. But it cannot survive forever. A star this massive will burn through its fuel in just a few million years and end its life in a spectacular way.

> i: hypernova | v: supernova explosion
It will probably explode as a supernova, perhaps one of the most powerful kinds, sometimes called a hypernova. When it does, it may become bright enough to see in daylight from the Earth, and may outshine the full Moon at night.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
Could it hurt us? Some extremely powerful stellar deaths produce gamma-ray bursts, narrow beams of intense radiation. If one were aimed at the Earth from close enough, it could damage the ozone layer. But Eta Carinae is seven and a half thousand light-years away, and its axis does not appear to point towards us. [pause] Most astronomers think it poses no real danger.

> i: eta carinae | v: star
When will it happen? Nobody knows. It could be tomorrow, or it could be a hundred thousand years from now. [pause] For a star like this, both are the blink of an eye.

## A Star On The Edge
mood: wonder
> i: carina nebula | v: carina nebula
Eta Carinae is one of the best laboratories in the universe for studying how the heaviest stars live and die. These stars are rare, but they matter enormously. Their winds and explosions spread heavy elements through galaxies, and their deaths can create black holes.

> i: eta carinae | v: eta carinae
Few stars like it are close enough to study in such detail. And it has already given us an eruption, a nebula shaped like a little man, and light echoes from the past. [pause] All before its real death.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
If you live in the southern hemisphere, you can see Eta Carinae today, faint but visible to the naked eye in a dark sky, glowing inside the Carina Nebula. [pause] Look at it, and remember. You are looking at a star that once nearly destroyed itself, and lived. And one day, perhaps sooner than we think, it will light up the sky one last time.
