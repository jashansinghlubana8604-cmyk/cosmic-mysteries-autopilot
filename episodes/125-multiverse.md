For most of history, the universe meant everything. All the stars, all the galaxies, all of space and time. There was nothing else, by definition.

> i: multiverse | v: cosmic web
But in the last century, some of the most successful theories in physics have started to whisper something unsettling. That our universe might not be everything. That it might be one of many. Perhaps infinitely many.

> i: bubble universes | v: expanding universe
Universes where the laws of physics are different. Universes where every possible outcome of every event actually happens. Universes so far away that there is a perfect copy of you, reading these exact words, right now. [pause] This is the idea of the multiverse.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
Some physicists think it is one of the most profound discoveries we could ever make. Others think it is not science at all, because it may be impossible to test. [pause] Let's look at what the multiverse really means, and why it divides scientists.

[title]

## Beyond The Horizon
mood: wonder
> i: observable universe | v: observable universe
The first kind of multiverse is the simplest. Light travels at a fixed speed, and the universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old. So there is a limit to how far we can see, a cosmic horizon. Everything inside it is the {{observable universe|OBSERVABLE UNIVERSE}}. Because space has been expanding while light travelled, that observable region is now about ninety three billion light-years across, a sphere with us at its centre.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
But the universe almost certainly continues beyond that horizon. Measurements suggest space is very nearly flat, and it may go on forever. If it does, then beyond our horizon there are other regions, just as big as ours, that we will never see.

> i: galaxies | v: galaxy zoom
And here is the strange consequence. If space is infinite, and the particles in any region can only be arranged in a finite number of ways, then every arrangement must repeat, again and again. Somewhere, unimaginably far away, there would be another Earth, and another you. [pause] The cosmologist Max Tegmark estimated your nearest identical copy would be around ten to the power of ten to the power of twenty nine metres away. A number far too big to write.

## Bubble Universes
mood: mystery
> i: cosmic inflation | v: expanding universe
The second kind comes from cosmic inflation, the theory that the universe expanded enormously in its first fraction of a second. Many versions of inflation suggest that it never stops completely. It ends in some regions, creating universes like ours, but continues in others forever.

> i: bubble universes | v: expanding universe
The result would be an endless foam of {{bubble universes|BUBBLE UNIVERSES}}, each one a separate region where inflation ended, expanding forever, never touching most of the others. Physicists like Andrei Linde and Alexander Vilenkin developed this idea, called eternal inflation. The space between the bubbles would keep inflating faster than light, so no traveller and no signal could ever cross from one bubble to another.

> i: multiverse | v: cosmic web
And in each bubble, the laws of physics might settle differently. Different strengths of gravity. Different masses for particles. Different numbers of dimensions. String theory, a candidate theory of everything, seems to allow an enormous number of possible arrangements, sometimes estimated at ten to the power of five hundred. [pause] Most of those universes would be lifeless. A few, like ours, might allow stars, planets and people.

## Every Possibility
mood: mystery
> i: quantum | v: quantum
The third kind comes from quantum physics. At the smallest scales, nature behaves in strange ways. A particle can exist in a combination of states at once, until it is measured, when it seems to choose just one. Why, and how, is one of the deepest puzzles in physics.

> i: quantum entanglement | v: particle collision
In 1957, a young physicist named Hugh Everett proposed a radical answer. Maybe the particle does not choose. Maybe every possible outcome happens, and the universe splits into branches, one for each result. This became known as the {{many-worlds interpretation|MANY WORLDS}}.

> i: quantum | v: quantum
The famous thought experiment of Schrödinger's cat captures the puzzle. A cat in a sealed box, its fate tied to a random quantum event, seems to be both alive and dead until someone looks. In the many-worlds view, there is no mystery about looking. [pause] In one branch, the cat lives. In another, it does not. Both are equally real.

> i: multiverse | v: deep space flight
In this picture, every time a quantum event happens, reality branches. There would be versions of you who made every different choice, lived every different life. You would only ever experience one branch. [pause] Many physicists take this idea seriously. Others think it multiplies universes for no reason.

## The Mathematical Universe
mood: wonder
> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
There is an even more radical idea. Max Tegmark has suggested that our universe is not just described by mathematics, but actually is a mathematical structure. And that every consistent mathematical structure exists as a universe of its own.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
In that picture, the multiverse would include every possible set of rules, not just different settings of our own laws. Most scientists see this as philosophy rather than physics. [pause] But it shows how far the idea can be pushed.

> i: computer data | v: data visualization
A related idea, popular in recent years, asks whether our universe could be a simulation running inside some larger reality. Like the mathematical universe, it is fascinating to think about, but no one has found a way to test it. [pause] Ideas this big sit right at the border between physics and philosophy.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
One reason the multiverse attracts serious attention is the question of fine-tuning. Some of the constants of nature seem set to values that allow stars, chemistry and life. Change them a little, and the universe might be lifeless. [pause] If there are countless universes with different settings, it would not be surprising that we find ourselves in one of the rare ones where life is possible.

## Can We Ever Know?
mood: mystery
> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The big problem is testing. By definition, other universes are beyond our reach. But some physicists have suggested ways to look for clues. If another bubble universe ever collided with ours in the distant past, it might have left a circular bruise in the cosmic microwave background, the oldest light in the sky.

> i: cmb cold spot | v: cosmic microwave background
Astronomers have searched for such patterns. Nothing convincing has been found. A large cold region in the cosmic microwave background has occasionally been mentioned as a possible sign, but most scientists think it has a more ordinary explanation.

> i: deep field | v: deep field zoom
Critics, including some of the physicists who helped develop inflation, argue that if a theory can predict anything, it predicts nothing, and that the multiverse risks leaving science behind. Supporters reply that it is a consequence of theories that have already passed other tests. [pause] The debate is still going on.

> i: multiverse | v: cosmic web
So, is our universe one of many? Nobody knows. It might be the only one. Or it might be one bubble in an endless foam. [pause] Either way, the question itself shows how far human curiosity has come. From looking up at the stars, to wondering whether all of them, and all of us, are just one small chapter in something unimaginably larger.
