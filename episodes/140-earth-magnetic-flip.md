Every second, the Sun throws a stream of charged particles across the solar system. It hits the Earth at hundreds of kilometres per second. [pause] And almost all of it misses us.

## The Engine Below
mood: wonder
> i: earth core | v: earth interior
Nearly three thousand kilometres below the surface lies Earth's outer core, an ocean of molten iron and nickel larger than the planet Mars. It is hotter than the surface of the Sun.

> i: earth interior | v: earth core
That liquid metal is constantly moving. Heat escaping from the solid inner core makes it churn, and the planet's spin twists the flows into giant spirals. Moving metal carries electric currents, and electric currents make magnetic fields. [pause] The result is a self-sustaining dynamo, the {{geodynamo|GEODYNAMO}}, that has been running for billions of years.

> i: earth magnetosphere | v: solar wind
Its field reaches tens of thousands of kilometres into space, forming a bubble called the {{magnetosphere|MAGNETOSPHERE}}. The solar wind flows around it like a river around a rock, stretching it into a long tail on the night side of the planet.

> i: mars | v: mars rotating
We know what happens to a planet without such a shield. Mars had a global magnetic field billions of years ago, but its dynamo died. Without that protection, the solar wind helped strip away much of its atmosphere. [pause] Today Mars is a cold, dry desert.

> i: sea turtle | v: birds flying
Life on Earth uses the field too. Migrating birds, sea turtles and salmon are thought to sense it to find their way across the planet. [pause] For billions of years, it has been one of the quiet conditions that made Earth livable.

## A Shield Growing Thin
mood: mystery
> i: compass | v: earth magnetic field
The first precise measurements of the field's strength were made by the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss in the 1830s. Since then, it has weakened by roughly {{nine percent|-9%}} worldwide.

> i: swarm satellites | v: satellite orbiting earth
To track these changes, the European Space Agency launched three satellites called {{Swarm|SWARM}} in 2013. They map the field from orbit in great detail. And they confirm that the weakening is not even. It is concentrated over one region.

> i: south atlantic anomaly | v: earth from space
Between South America and southern Africa lies the {{South Atlantic Anomaly|SOUTH ATLANTIC ANOMALY}}. There, the field is far weaker than it should be, and radiation trapped around the Earth dips much closer to the surface.

> i: hubble space telescope | v: international space station
Satellites flying through it are struck by more charged particles, which can flip bits in their computers and damage electronics. The Hubble Space Telescope does not take observations while crossing it. Astronauts on the space station have reported seeing flashes of light with their eyes closed, caused by particles passing through them. [pause] And the anomaly is growing, and appears to be splitting into two separate weak spots.

> i: arctic | v: arctic ice
Meanwhile, the magnetic north pole is on the move. For most of the twentieth century it wandered slowly across northern Canada. Then it sped up, at times to more than fifty kilometres a year, heading towards Siberia. [pause] In 2019, scientists had to release an early update to the model used by smartphone maps, ships and aircraft to account for the drift.

## When North Became South
mood: dread
> i: lava flow | v: lava flow
How do we know the field can flip? Because rocks remember. When lava cools, tiny magnetic minerals inside it lock into the direction of the field at that moment, like frozen compass needles.

> i: volcanic rock | v: volcano
In 1906, the French physicist Bernard Brunhes found lava rocks whose magnetism pointed the wrong way. In the 1920s, the Japanese geophysicist Motonori Matuyama found more and dated them. They had recorded a time when the north magnetic pole sat in the southern hemisphere. [pause] At first, few people believed them.

> i: ocean floor | v: ocean floor
The proof came from the ocean floor. In the 1960s, surveys found the seabed was striped with bands of normal and reversed magnetism, mirrored on either side of the mid-ocean ridges where new crust forms. [pause] The planet had been recording its own flips like a tape recorder.

> i: earth magnetic field | v: earth magnetic field
There have been hundreds of reversals in Earth's history. They come at irregular intervals. Sometimes several happen within a million years. Once, during the age of the dinosaurs, the field held steady for nearly {{forty million years|40 MILLION YEARS}}.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotating
The last full reversal happened about {{seven hundred and eighty thousand years ago|780,000 YEARS AGO}}. Our own species has never lived through one. [pause] But our ancestors may have lived through something close.

## The Near Miss
mood: mystery
> i: aurora | v: aurora borealis
About forty one thousand years ago, the field went through what geologists call an excursion. It weakened dramatically, the poles wandered far from their usual places, and then, within a few centuries, the field recovered in its original direction. It is named the {{Laschamp|LASCHAMP}} event, after the lava flows in France where it was first recorded.

> i: aurora | v: aurora night sky
At its weakest, the field may have fallen to a small fraction of its present strength. Auroras would likely have been seen far from the poles, and more cosmic radiation would have reached the atmosphere. Studies of ancient trees, ice cores and cave formations all show traces of that extra radiation.

> i: ice core | v: glacier
Some researchers have proposed that the Laschamp event changed the climate and even affected early humans. That idea is much debated. [pause] What is clear is that life on Earth carried on.

> i: earth core | v: earth interior
Full reversals seem to take thousands of years. During that time the field does not simply switch off. It becomes weaker and messier, with several north and south poles scattered around the globe at once. [pause] Then slowly, a new order takes hold, pointing the other way.

## Is A Flip Coming?
mood: epic
> i: earth magnetic field | v: earth magnetic field
So is the present weakening the start of a reversal? Most geophysicists say there is no clear sign of that. The field today is still stronger than its average over the last million years. It has risen and fallen many times without flipping.

> i: earth core | v: earth interior
The South Atlantic Anomaly may be a long-lived feature, linked to unusual structures deep down at the boundary between the core and the mantle. Rocks suggest similar weak spots have appeared in the same region before. [pause] But the core is chaotic, and nobody can predict exactly what it will do.

> i: satellite orbiting earth | v: satellite orbiting earth
If a reversal did begin, it would not be sudden. The greatest danger would be to technology rather than life. A weaker shield would leave satellites, power grids and astronauts more exposed to solar storms. [pause] Compasses would become unreliable, and navigation systems would need constant updates.

> i: fossil | v: earth from space
There is no evidence that past reversals caused mass extinctions. Life has come through hundreds of them. [pause] Animals that navigate by the field would have to adapt, but they have done it before.

> i: aurora from space | v: aurora from space
Somewhere beneath us, an ocean of liquid iron is turning in the dark, as it has for billions of years. It decides where our compasses point and how much of the Sun's fury reaches us. [pause] One day, it will flip again. And when it does, the northern lights may dance over places that have never seen them.
