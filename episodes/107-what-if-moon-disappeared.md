Every night of human history, the Moon has been there. Waxing and waning. Lighting the way for travellers. Pulling on the oceans. Inspiring calendars, myths, poems and songs.

> i: moon | v: moon orbit
Now imagine you step outside one evening, and it is simply gone. Not hidden behind a cloud. Not in its new phase. Gone, as if it had never existed.

> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
What would happen? Would the Earth fly out of its orbit? Would the oceans rise up? Would life notice? [pause] The answers are surprising. Some changes would be instant. Others would take millions of years to unfold. And the Earth would not fly away. The Moon does not hold us in place around the Sun.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
The Moon is not just a pretty light in the sky. It is part of how our planet works. [pause] Let's take it away, and see what is left.

## The First Night
mood: mystery
> i: night sky | v: night sky
The first thing you would notice is the darkness. Moonlight is just reflected sunlight, but on a clear night, a full Moon can make the landscape bright enough to walk by. Without it, every night would be as dark as a new Moon.

> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
Something subtle would change too. The Earth and the Moon actually orbit a shared balance point, about four thousand seven hundred kilometres from the Earth's centre, still inside the planet. Every month, the Earth swings gently around that point. Without the Moon, that monthly wobble would stop. [pause] Nobody would feel it. But our planet's path around the Sun would become slightly smoother.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
That would have one beautiful side effect. Stars and the Milky Way would be easier to see, every night of the month. Astronomers would be delighted. [pause] But for many living things, the sudden darkness would be a disaster.

> i: owl | v: animals night
Many animals time their lives to the Moon. Some predators hunt by moonlight. Some prey animals hide from it. Dung beetles have been shown to use moonlight, and polarised light from the Moon, to navigate. Without it, ecosystems would be thrown off balance.

> i: coral reef | v: coral reef
On the Great Barrier Reef, many corals release their eggs and sperm into the water on the same few nights each year, a few days after a full Moon, in one of the largest spawning events on Earth. Scientists think moonlight helps them coordinate. [pause] Without the Moon, that ancient timing could fall apart.

## The Tides Shrink
mood: wonder
> i: ocean tides | v: ocean waves
The Moon's gravity pulls on the Earth's oceans, raising the tides. The Sun does too, but its effect is less than half as strong. If the Moon vanished, tides would not stop completely. But they would shrink dramatically, to less than half their usual range.

> i: tide pools | v: ocean
That would reshape coastlines all over the world. Tidal flats and estuaries, among the richest habitats on Earth, depend on the regular rise and fall of the water. Crabs, worms, shorebirds and fish that live in these zones would see their world change overnight.

> i: ocean | v: ocean waves
Tides also help stir the deep ocean. As tidal currents flow over underwater mountains and ridges, they help mix cold deep water with warmer water above. Some scientists estimate that the tides supply a large share of the energy that keeps the deep ocean mixing. [pause] Weaker tides could slowly change ocean currents, and with them, the climate.

> i: early earth | v: ocean waves
Some scientists have even suggested that tides helped life begin. On the early Earth, when the Moon was much closer, tides were far higher. The regular flooding and drying of coastal pools may have concentrated chemicals in ways that encouraged the first complex molecules to form. [pause] It is only a hypothesis. But the Moon may be part of why we exist at all.

## The Wobbling Planet
mood: dread
> i: earth tilt | v: earth rotation
The biggest long-term effect is invisible. The Earth is tilted on its axis by about twenty three and a half degrees. That tilt gives us our seasons. And it is remarkably stable, changing by only a degree or two over tens of thousands of years.

> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
Part of the reason is the Moon. Its gravity tugs on the Earth's equatorial bulge, steadying the planet's spin like a stabilising hand. Without that hand, the tilt could be nudged much more by the gravity of the other planets.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
In the 1990s, the astronomer Jacques Laskar ran simulations suggesting that, without the Moon, the Earth's tilt could vary chaotically and enormously over millions of years. Later studies found the swings might be smaller than that, but still larger than today. [pause] Seasons could become far more extreme over long timescales, with ice ages and hot periods unlike anything we know.

> i: mars | v: mars rotation
We can see a real example next door. Mars has only two tiny moons, too small to steady it. Its tilt is thought to have swung widely over millions of years, possibly driving huge changes in its climate. [pause] The Earth's calm seasons may owe a lot to our unusually large Moon.

## Longer Days Stop
mood: wonder
> i: clock | v: time lapse
The Moon has been slowly slowing the Earth's rotation for billions of years. Its tides act like a brake, making each day slightly longer. Hundreds of millions of years ago, a day lasted only around twenty two hours.

> i: earth rotation | v: earth rotation
Without the Moon, that braking would mostly stop. The Sun's tides would continue, but much more weakly. Days would stay roughly as long as they are now for a very long time. [pause] Strangely, the Earth would keep spinning faster than it otherwise would have.

> i: solar eclipse | v: solar eclipse
And of course, there would be no more eclipses. No total solar eclipses, when the Moon's disc perfectly covers the Sun and the sky goes dark at noon. No blood-red lunar eclipses either. [pause] Some of the most breathtaking sights in nature would simply end.

> i: moon surface | v: moon surface
We would also lose the closest stepping stone into space. Space agencies are planning to return humans to the Moon, to search its polar craters for ice, to build bases, and to learn how to live on another world before travelling further. [pause] Without the Moon, humanity's first step beyond the Earth would be a much bigger leap.

## What The Moon Gives Us
mood: wonder
> i: full moon | v: moon
Could the Earth survive without the Moon? Yes. The planet would keep orbiting the Sun almost exactly as it does now. Life would continue. But the world would be different, and over millions of years, perhaps very different.

> i: calendar | v: moon
And human culture would be different too. The word month comes from Moon. Many calendars, from the Islamic calendar to the Chinese lunar calendar, are built around its phases. Festivals, fishing, farming traditions and religious holidays across the world are timed by it.

> i: earthrise | v: earth moon
Twelve human beings have walked on the Moon. It is the only other world people have ever touched. Without it, the space age might have looked completely different. [pause] So tonight, if the Moon is up, take a moment to look at it. It is not just decoration. It is part of the machinery that made the Earth the world it is.
