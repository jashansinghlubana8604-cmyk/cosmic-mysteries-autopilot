Sixty six million years ago, on an ordinary day, the Earth belonged to the dinosaurs. They had ruled it for more than one hundred and fifty million years. Forests of conifers and flowering plants. Shallow warm seas. Herds of horned giants. Flocks of feathered hunters.

> i: asteroid | v: asteroid
Far above, unseen, a rock about ten kilometres wide was falling towards the planet at around twenty kilometres per second. Fast enough to cross the width of the United States in a few minutes.

> i: asteroid impact earth | v: asteroid impact
It struck the shallow sea off what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. In the time it takes to read this sentence, it punched a hole in the Earth's crust tens of kilometres deep. [pause] And it changed the history of life forever.

> i: asteroid impact | v: asteroid impact
Within hours, most of the world was burning, shaking or flooding. Within months, the sky went dark. Within a few years, around three quarters of all species on Earth were gone. [pause] This is the day the sky fell.

## The Clue In The Clay
mood: mystery
> i: rock layers | v: rock layers
For a long time, nobody knew why the dinosaurs disappeared. In the late 1970s, a geologist named {{Walter Alvarez|WALTER ALVAREZ}} was studying rock layers near the town of Gubbio, in Italy. Between limestone from the age of dinosaurs and limestone from the age after, there was a thin layer of clay, about a centimetre thick.

> i: rock layers | v: geology
Walter asked his father, the Nobel Prize-winning physicist Luis Alvarez, to help measure how long that layer had taken to form. They tested it for iridium, a metal that is very rare in the Earth's crust, but more common in asteroids. The clay contained far more iridium than expected. Dozens of times more.

> i: rock layers | v: geology
The same iridium-rich layer turned up around the world, in Denmark, in New Zealand, everywhere they looked, always at the same moment in time. In 1980, the Alvarez team made a bold proposal. A giant asteroid had hit the Earth and caused the extinction. [pause] But if that was true, where was the crater?

> i: chicxulub crater | v: chicxulub crater
The answer had already been found by an oil company. Geophysicists searching for oil in Mexico had noticed a huge buried ring-shaped structure under the Yucatán. In 1991, scientists confirmed it was an impact crater, about one hundred and eighty kilometres wide, and exactly the right age. They named it after a nearby town. {{Chicxulub|CHICXULUB}}.

## The First Minutes
mood: dread
> i: asteroid impact earth | v: asteroid impact
The asteroid hit the Earth at a steep angle, perhaps around sixty degrees. In an instant, it released energy estimated at more than a billion times the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. The rock and the sea floor around it were vaporised.

> i: impact crater | v: asteroid impact
A crater about thirty kilometres deep opened in seconds, then collapsed outward and rebounded, forming a ring of mountains in the middle. Molten rock and vapour were blasted out of the atmosphere entirely, into space.

> i: fireball | v: explosion
A fireball of superheated gas and vaporised rock swelled outward from the impact site. Anything within hundreds of kilometres was likely killed almost instantly by the heat and the blast. Winds stronger than any hurricane tore across the land, flattening forests far beyond the horizon.

> i: earthquake | v: earthquake
The impact shook the planet with an earthquake far larger than any ever recorded by humans. Close to the impact, a wall of water perhaps more than a kilometre high surged outward. Tsunamis raced across the oceans, scouring coastlines thousands of kilometres away. [pause] And that was only the beginning.

## The Sky On Fire
mood: dread
> i: meteor shower | v: meteor shower
The material thrown into space came back down. All over the world, trillions of tiny glassy beads and rock fragments re-entered the atmosphere, glowing like countless shooting stars. Some scientists think that, for a while, the heat from this falling debris turned the sky above parts of the planet into a broiler.

> i: forest fire | v: forest fire
Wildfires may have broken out across whole continents. The amount of soot found in the clay layer suggests huge fires burned somewhere. [pause] For animals caught in the open, there would have been nowhere to hide from the heat raining from above.

> i: fossil site | v: fossils
In North Dakota, at a site called {{Tanis|TANIS}}, scientists have found an extraordinary deposit that may record the first hours after the impact, more than three thousand kilometres away. Fish fossils with tiny glass beads from the impact lodged in their gills. Trees and animals tangled together by a sudden surge of water. Some researchers think the details suggest the impact happened in spring in the northern hemisphere. [pause] A snapshot of the day the world ended.

## The Long Darkness
mood: dread
> i: dark sky | v: dark clouds
Then came the worst part. The impact had hit rocks rich in sulphur and carbon. It threw enormous amounts of dust, soot and sulphur gas high into the atmosphere, where they spread around the entire planet.

> i: earth atmosphere | v: storm clouds
They blocked out the Sun. For months, maybe years, it may have been dark enough to stop photosynthesis in many places. Temperatures across the world plunged. Scientists call it an {{impact winter|IMPACT WINTER}}.

> i: frozen landscape | v: snow storm
Plants died. Then the animals that ate plants. Then the animals that ate them. In the oceans, tiny plankton at the base of the food chain collapsed. Around three quarters of all species on Earth disappeared, including every dinosaur that could not fly. [pause] It was one of the worst disasters in the history of life.

> i: volcano | v: volcano
Some scientists think enormous volcanic eruptions in India, which were already underway, made things even worse. How much each one contributed is still debated. [pause] But the asteroid delivered the knockout blow.

## What Survived
mood: wonder
> i: bird | v: birds flying
Not everything died. Small creatures that could burrow, hide or eat almost anything had the best chance. Some mammals, most of them no bigger than a rat. Some crocodiles, turtles and lizards. [pause] And some dinosaurs did survive. We call them birds.

> i: chicxulub crater | v: ocean
In 2016, scientists drilled into the crater itself, deep under the sea floor. They found that life returned to the crater surprisingly quickly, with tiny creatures living there within years of the impact. The planet had begun to heal.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
With the giant dinosaurs gone, mammals had a world full of empty niches. Over millions of years, they spread, grew and diversified into whales, horses, elephants and primates. [pause] Without that rock from space, there would almost certainly be no humans.

> i: asteroid | v: asteroid
Recent studies of the impact layer suggest the asteroid itself came from beyond the orbit of Jupiter, a dark, carbon-rich rock that wandered into the inner solar system. One stray object, on one unlucky path, ended one world and opened the door to ours. And the timing was cruel. In 2017, a study argued that only about thirteen percent of the Earth's surface was covered in rocks rich enough in sulphur and oil-like organic matter to produce such a deadly sky. Hit the deep ocean, or almost anywhere else, and the dinosaurs might have survived. [pause] We are, in a very real sense, children of a catastrophe. And that is why, today, we watch the sky.
