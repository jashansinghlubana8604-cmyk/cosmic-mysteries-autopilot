## Worlds Of Glass And Iron
mood: dread
> v: hd 189733b | i: hd 189733b
Sixty three light years away is a planet called HD 189733b. Seen from a distance, it is a striking cobalt blue, the colour of Earth's oceans. But there is no water here.

> i: hot jupiter | v: exoplanet atmosphere
It is a gas giant larger than Jupiter, orbiting so close to its star that one year lasts just over two days. Its atmosphere is heated to around a thousand degrees. That blue colour comes from clouds laced with silicate particles, tiny grains of glass.

> v: exoplanet clouds wind | i: exoplanet winds
And the winds are monstrous, measured at around {{eight thousand kilometres per hour|8,700 KM/H}}, seven times the speed of sound. On HD 189733b, it may rain glass, and the rain would not fall. It would scream sideways.

> v: wasp-76b | i: wasp-76b iron rain
Six hundred light years away is somewhere worse. WASP-76b always shows the same face to its star. On the day side, the temperature climbs past two thousand degrees, hot enough to vaporise iron. Winds carry the iron vapour around to the cooler night side, where it condenses. [pause] And there, it rains {{molten iron|IT RAINS IRON}}.

> i: dark exoplanet | v: dark planet
And then there is TrES-2b, perhaps the darkest planet ever found. It reflects less than one percent of the light that hits it, darker than coal, darker than black paint. Yet it faintly glows a deep red, like the embers of a fire, from its own blistering heat.

> v: disintegrating planet | i: planet disintegrating
And some worlds are simply falling apart. Around a star more than a thousand light years away, astronomers found a small rocky planet so close to its sun that its surface is boiling away into space. It trails a long tail of dust behind it like a comet, and every time it passes in front of its star, the shadow is different. The planet is evaporating, a little more on every orbit, until one day nothing will be left.

## The Dead Stars
mood: dread
> v: neutron star | i: neutron star
When a giant star dies, its core can collapse into a ball just twenty kilometres wide, containing more mass than the entire Sun. This is a {{neutron star|NEUTRON STAR}}.

> i: neutron star surface | v: neutron star rotating
A teaspoon of its material would weigh as much as a mountain. Its gravity is so strong that the tallest mountains on its surface would be only millimetres high. A single marshmallow, falling onto it from far away, would strike with the energy of a nuclear bomb.

> v: magnetar flare | i: magnetar
Some neutron stars are even worse. They are called magnetars, and they have the most powerful magnetic fields in the known universe, a thousand trillion times stronger than the Earth's.

> i: magnetar artist impression | v: magnetar
Fields that strong do strange things. Long before you came close, they would distort the atoms in your body and tear them apart. A magnetar could wipe the data from every credit card on Earth from halfway to the Moon.

> v: magnetar outburst | i: magnetar flare
In December 2004, a magnetar called SGR 1806-20, about fifty thousand light years away, unleashed a giant flare. In a fraction of a second, it released more energy than the Sun gives off in a hundred thousand years. The blast was so strong that it measurably disturbed Earth's upper atmosphere, from the other side of the galaxy.

## The Star About To Explode
mood: mystery
> v: eta carinae | i: eta carinae hubble
In the southern sky, seven and a half thousand light years away, is one of the most massive and unstable stars in our galaxy. Its name is {{Eta Carinae|ETA CARINAE}}.

> i: eta carinae | v: eta carinae zoom
It is actually a pair of stars, the larger of which may be around a hundred times the mass of the Sun, burning so fiercely that it is pushing itself apart with its own light.

> v: stellar eruption | i: eta carinae nebula
In the 1840s, it suddenly erupted. For a few years it became the second brightest star in the night sky, even though it is thousands of light years away. In that outburst it threw off around ten times the mass of the Sun, forming the twin glowing clouds that still surround it today.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Eta Carinae is expected to explode as a supernova, perhaps even a hypernova, one of the most violent explosions in nature. It could happen tomorrow, or a hundred thousand years from now. [pause] When it does, it may shine so brightly that it can be seen from Earth even in daylight.

## The Heart Of The Galaxy
mood: wonder
> v: milky way centre zoom | i: milky way centre
Twenty six thousand light years away, at the centre of our own galaxy, is a place no living thing could ever survive.

> i: sagittarius a star | v: black hole simulation
Here sits Sagittarius A star, a black hole four million times the mass of the Sun. Stars swing around it at thousands of kilometres per second, on orbits that take just a few years. One of them, called S2, whips past it every sixteen years at more than two and a half percent of the speed of light. Gas clouds are stretched into long streams as they fall toward it.

> i: sagittarius a star event horizon telescope | v: milky way centre zoom
In 2022, we finally saw it. The Event Horizon Telescope, a network of radio dishes as wide as the Earth, captured the shadow of Sagittarius A star, a dark heart inside a ring of glowing gas. Every day, that ring flickers and flares, sometimes brightening many times over within an hour. [pause] Something is always falling in.

> v: stars orbiting black hole | i: galactic centre stars
Sometimes, a pair of stars passes too close. One is captured. The other is flung out of the galaxy entirely, at millions of kilometres per hour, never to return. Astronomers have found these runaway stars, {{hypervelocity stars|HYPERVELOCITY STARS}}, racing away from the centre of the Milky Way.

> i: galactic centre infrared | v: galactic centre
The whole region is soaked in radiation, crowded with exploded stars, and wrapped in enormous magnetic filaments. If there is a place in our galaxy that most resembles a battlefield, this is it.

## The Brightest Explosion Ever Seen
mood: dread
> v: gamma-ray burst | i: gamma-ray burst
On the 9th of October 2022, satellites around the Earth were suddenly blinded. A flash of gamma rays had arrived, so intense that astronomers called it {{the BOAT|THE BOAT}}: the brightest of all time.

> i: gamma-ray burst jet | v: gamma ray burst jet
It came from a collapsing star about two and a half billion light years away. Most likely, a giant star had died, its core collapsing into a black hole and blasting out twin jets of energy at nearly the speed of light. One of those jets was pointed almost directly at us.

> v: earth atmosphere | i: earth from space
Even from that unimaginable distance, it disturbed the Earth's upper atmosphere, as strongly as a large solar flare. Astronomers estimate a burst this bright reaches us perhaps once every ten thousand years.

> i: gamma-ray burst | v: gamma ray burst animation
Now imagine one much closer. A gamma ray burst within a few thousand light years, aimed at Earth, could strip away much of our ozone layer and expose the surface to the Sun's ultraviolet light. Some scientists have even wondered whether a burst like this contributed to a mass extinction four hundred and forty million years ago. It is only a hypothesis. [pause] But it is not impossible.

> v: cosmic web simulation | i: cosmic void
And beyond all of these, on the largest scales, there is a different kind of terror. Not violence, but emptiness. Vast voids hundreds of millions of light years across, where almost nothing exists at all. If our galaxy had formed in one, we might have grown up believing we were alone in the entire universe.

> v: zoom out milky way | i: milky way
The universe is not cruel. It does not know we are here. But it is vast, and violent, and full of places where the laws of physics are pushed to their limits. [pause] And for now, we are lucky enough to be watching all of it from somewhere safe.
