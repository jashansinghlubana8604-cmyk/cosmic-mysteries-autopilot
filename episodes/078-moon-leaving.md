Every year, the Moon moves about three point eight centimetres further away from the Earth. That is roughly the speed your fingernails grow. Too slow for anyone to notice in a lifetime.

> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
But it has been happening for billions of years. And it has changed our planet in ways you might never have imagined. It has stretched our days. It has shaped our tides. It has even decided whether we get to see total eclipses of the Sun.

> i: moon | v: moon orbit
When the Moon was born, it hung in the sky perhaps fifteen times closer than it is today, a colossal glowing disc. Days on Earth lasted only a few hours. [pause] And one day, far in the future, the last total solar eclipse will happen. After that, there will never be another.

> i: apollo retroreflector | v: moon surface
How do we know any of this? Because astronauts left mirrors on the Moon. [pause] And for more than fifty years, scientists have been firing lasers at them.

## Mirrors On The Moon
mood: wonder
> i: apollo 11 | v: apollo moon landing
In July 1969, the crew of Apollo 11 did more than plant a flag. They set up a small panel of carefully shaped mirrors on the lunar surface. Later Apollo crews left two more, and two Soviet robotic rovers, called Lunokhod, carried others.

> i: apollo retroreflector | v: moon surface
These mirrors are special. They reflect light straight back in the direction it came from, no matter what angle it arrives at. So if you fire a laser at one from the Earth, a tiny fraction of the light comes right back to you.

> i: laser observatory | v: laser
Observatories on Earth do exactly that. They fire powerful laser pulses at the Moon and time how long the light takes to make the round trip, about two and a half seconds. Of the trillions of photons sent out, only a handful come back. [pause] But that is enough to measure the distance to the Moon to within millimetres.

> i: moon | v: moon orbit
After decades of these measurements, the result is clear. The Moon is spiralling away from us, at about {{3.8 centimetres a year|3.8 CM / YEAR}}. The experiment the Apollo astronauts set up is still running today.

## The Tidal Tug Of War
mood: mystery
> i: ocean tides | v: ocean waves
Why is it leaving? Because of the tides. The Moon's gravity pulls on the Earth's oceans, raising two bulges of water, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. As the Earth spins, we move through those bulges, and that is why the tide comes in and goes out twice a day.

> i: earth rotation | v: earth rotation
But the Earth spins much faster than the Moon orbits. So the Earth's rotation drags those bulges slightly ahead of the Moon. And the bulge ahead of the Moon pulls on it, giving it a tiny forward tug in its orbit. [pause] Each tug lifts the Moon into a slightly higher, wider orbit.

> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
That energy has to come from somewhere. It comes from the Earth's spin. So as the Moon drifts away, {{the Earth slows down|THE EARTH SLOWS DOWN}}. Our days are getting longer, by around one or two thousandths of a second every century.

> i: clock | v: time lapse
That does not sound like much. But over hundreds of millions of years, it adds up to hours. [pause] And the evidence is written in stone.

## Days Of The Past
mood: wonder
> i: coral | v: coral reef
Corals grow in daily layers, like the rings of a tree, and in yearly bands too. In the 1960s, a scientist named John Wells counted the daily growth lines in fossil corals around four hundred million years old, from a time called the Devonian.

> i: fossil coral | v: fossils
He found about four hundred daily lines per year. Back then, a year still lasted about as long as it does now, because the Earth's orbit around the Sun has not changed much. But each day was shorter, about twenty two hours. [pause] So a year held around {{400 days|400 DAYS}}.

> i: rock layers | v: rock layers
Other clues come from ancient tidal deposits, rocks laid down in layers by the daily rise and fall of the sea. They suggest that around six hundred and twenty million years ago, a day lasted under twenty two hours. And studies of even older rocks hint at days of around eighteen hours, more than a billion years ago.

> i: early earth | v: early earth
Go back further still, to when the Moon was born, about four and a half billion years ago. It probably formed from the debris of a giant collision between the young Earth and a planet-sized body. The newborn Moon orbited far closer than it does today. [pause] And the Earth may have been spinning around once every five or six hours.

## The Last Eclipse
mood: dread
> i: solar eclipse | v: solar eclipse
Today, we live in a remarkable coincidence. The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, but also about four hundred times further away. So from the Earth, they appear almost exactly the same size. That is why the Moon can perfectly cover the Sun in a total solar eclipse.

> i: total solar eclipse | v: solar eclipse
During those precious minutes, the sky turns dark, the stars come out, and the Sun's ghostly outer atmosphere, the corona, blazes around the black disc. It is one of the most breathtaking sights in nature.

> i: annular eclipse | v: solar eclipse
But as the Moon drifts away, it appears slightly smaller in our sky. Eventually, it will no longer be big enough to cover the Sun completely. Every eclipse will become an annular one, leaving a ring of fire around the Moon. Scientists estimate that the last total solar eclipse on Earth will happen in roughly {{600 million years|600 MILLION YEARS}}. [pause] We happen to live in the window when they are possible.

> i: solar eclipse | v: solar eclipse
In the distant past, the opposite was true. When the Moon was much closer, it appeared far larger than the Sun, and eclipses would have blotted out not just the Sun's disc but its glowing corona too. [pause] The perfect, exact fit we see today is a fleeting alignment in the Earth's history, and humans arrived just in time to see it.

## The Far Future
mood: wonder
> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
The Moon matters for more than eclipses. Its gravity helps keep the Earth's tilt steady, varying only a little over thousands of years. That tilt gives us our stable seasons. Without a large moon, some studies suggest the Earth's axis could wobble far more wildly, with dramatic swings in climate.

> i: moon orbit | v: moon orbit
And the Moon will keep receding, and the Earth will keep slowing. If nothing else interfered, in tens of billions of years, the Earth's day and the Moon's month would become equal, around forty seven of our present days each. One side of the Earth would always face the Moon, and the Moon would hang motionless in its sky.

> i: sun red giant | v: sun
That will never quite happen. Long before then, in about five billion years, the Sun will swell into a red giant and change everything. [pause] But the slow dance between the Earth and the Moon will continue right up until that moment.

> i: full moon | v: moon
So tonight, if the Moon is up, take a good look at it. It is a little smaller in the sky than it was when the dinosaurs looked up. And a little bigger than it will be for anyone who comes after us. [pause] Three point eight centimetres a year. Slowly, quietly, it is saying goodbye.
