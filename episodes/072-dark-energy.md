In 1998, two teams of astronomers set out to measure something they thought they understood. The universe began in a hot Big Bang and has been expanding ever since. And gravity, pulling everything back together, should be slowing that expansion down.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
They wanted to measure how fast it was slowing. So they used exploding stars, billions of light-years away, as cosmic markers. And they found something that made no sense at all.

> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
The expansion is not slowing down. It is speeding up. Something is pushing the galaxies apart, faster and faster, as if space itself is filled with an energy that grows with every cubic metre of new space. [pause] Nobody knows what it is.

> i: dark energy | v: deep field zoom
Scientists call it {{dark energy|DARK ENERGY}}. It makes up around two thirds of everything in the universe. More than all the stars, all the galaxies, all the dark matter combined. [pause] And in the last few years, the first hints have appeared that it may be changing.

## Einstein's Mistake
mood: mystery
> i: albert einstein | v: spacetime curvature
The story begins with Einstein. In 1917, he applied his new theory of gravity to the whole universe. But his equations insisted the universe could not stand still. It had to be either expanding or collapsing.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
At the time, everyone believed the universe was static and eternal. So Einstein added an extra term to his equations, a kind of anti-gravity that would balance everything out. He called it the {{cosmological constant|COSMOLOGICAL CONSTANT}}.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
Not everyone agreed. In 1927, a Belgian priest and physicist named Georges Lemaître worked through Einstein's equations himself and argued that the universe was expanding, and must once have been packed into a tiny, dense state. Einstein reportedly told him his calculations were correct, but his physics was abominable. [pause] Lemaître was right.

> i: galaxies | v: galaxy zoom
A decade later, Edwin Hubble showed that distant galaxies are rushing away from us. The universe was expanding after all. Einstein dropped his constant, and according to a famous story, later called it his biggest blunder. [pause] Seventy years after that, it would come roaring back.

## The Surprise Of 1998
mood: mystery
> i: supernova type ia | v: supernova explosion
To measure the history of the expansion, astronomers needed distant objects whose true brightness they knew. They chose a special kind of exploding star, a {{Type Ia supernova|TYPE Ia SUPERNOVA}}, which happens when a white dwarf star gains too much mass and blows up. These explosions all reach a similar peak brightness.

> i: supernova | v: telescope night
So by measuring how bright they looked from Earth, astronomers could work out how far away they were. And by measuring how much their light had been stretched by the expansion of space, they could work out how much the universe had grown since they exploded.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
Two rival teams, one led by Saul Perlmutter, the other by Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess, raced to find and measure dozens of these distant supernovae. Both expected to find that the expansion was slowing down. [pause] Both found that the distant supernovae were fainter than expected. Further away than they should be.

> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
The only explanation was that the expansion had sped up during the billions of years their light was travelling. The universe was accelerating. In 2011, the leaders of the two teams shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. [pause] They had discovered that most of the universe is made of something we cannot see, cannot touch, and cannot explain.

## The Worst Prediction In Physics
mood: dread
> i: quantum vacuum | v: quantum
So what is dark energy? The simplest idea is that it is the energy of empty space itself. According to quantum physics, even a perfect vacuum is not truly empty. It seethes with fleeting particles appearing and vanishing. That activity should have energy.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
And here is the problem. When physicists calculate how much energy empty space should have, using quantum theory, they get a number larger than the observed value by a factor of around {{ten to the power of 120|10¹²⁰}}. A one followed by one hundred and twenty zeros.

> i: dark energy | v: deep field zoom
It has been called the worst prediction in the history of physics. Something must cancel out almost all of that energy, but not quite all. [pause] Why? Nobody knows. It is one of the deepest unsolved problems in all of science.

> i: galaxies | v: galaxy zoom
Other ideas say dark energy is not a constant at all, but a new kind of field that slowly changes over time. Or that it is not energy at all, and our theory of gravity simply breaks down on the largest scales. [pause] To tell them apart, astronomers needed to measure the expansion more precisely than ever before.

## A Changing Darkness?
mood: mystery
> i: desi telescope | v: telescope dome
At the Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona, a four-metre telescope has been fitted with an instrument called {{DESI|DESI}}, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument. It has five thousand tiny robotic fibres, each pointing at a different galaxy, capturing their light at once.

> i: cosmic web | v: cosmic web
DESI is building the largest three-dimensional map of the universe ever made, charting tens of millions of galaxies and quasars across eleven billion years. By measuring the spacing of galaxies at different times, it can track how fast the universe was expanding at each moment.

> i: galaxies | v: galaxy zoom
In 2024 and 2025, the DESI team announced something surprising. When they combined their map with other measurements, the data hinted that dark energy might not be constant. It might be getting {{weaker|WEAKER}} over time.

> i: dark energy | v: expanding universe
The result is not yet certain. It falls short of the level physicists usually require to claim a discovery. But if it holds up, it would overturn the simplest picture of the cosmos, the one built on Einstein's constant. [pause] Dark energy might have a history. And a future that is still being written.

## The Fate Of Everything
mood: wonder
> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
Why does it matter? First, consider the full recipe of the cosmos. Ordinary matter, everything we have ever touched or seen, is about five percent. Dark matter, invisible but heavy, about twenty seven. Dark energy, about sixty eight. Almost everything is something we do not understand. And dark energy decides how the universe ends. If it stays constant, the expansion will accelerate forever. Galaxies will drift beyond each other's sight. The universe will grow colder and darker, in what is called the Big Freeze.

> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
If dark energy grows stronger, it could eventually tear apart galaxies, stars, planets and even atoms, in a Big Rip. And if it weakens enough, or even reverses, the expansion might one day slow, stop, and the universe might fall back in on itself in a Big Crunch.

> i: euclid telescope | v: euclid
New telescopes are being built to find out. The European Space Agency's Euclid telescope, launched in 2023, is mapping billions of galaxies across a third of the sky. The Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile will measure countless supernovae. Each will test whether dark energy is truly changing.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
Here is a haunting thought. Because of dark energy, very distant galaxies are being carried away from us faster than their light can ever cross the gap. In the far future, astronomers in our galaxy will look out and see nothing but darkness beyond their own home. [pause] They will never know that the universe was once full of galaxies. We live at the one time in history when we can still see them.
