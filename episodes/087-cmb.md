There is light all around you right now that is older than every star, every galaxy and every atom of your body except the hydrogen. It has been travelling for thirteen point eight billion years. And it is coming from every direction at once.

> i: night sky | v: night sky

## The Noise That Would Not Go Away
mood: mystery
> i: horn antenna | v: radio telescope
In 1964, at Bell Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey, two radio astronomers named {{Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson|PENZIAS & WILSON}} were working with a large, horn-shaped antenna. It had been built for early satellite communication, and they wanted to use it to measure faint radio signals from our galaxy.

> i: horn antenna | v: radio telescope night
But they kept hearing a faint, steady hiss of microwave noise. It was there day and night. It was there in every direction they pointed. It did not change with the seasons. It did not seem to come from the Earth, the Sun or the Milky Way.

> i: pigeon | v: birds
They checked every part of their equipment. They found a pair of pigeons nesting inside the horn, and a coating of what they politely called white dielectric material. They cleaned it out. They chased the pigeons away. [pause] The hiss was still there.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope night
Meanwhile, just forty or so kilometres away at Princeton University, a team led by physicist Robert Dicke was building an instrument to search for exactly that kind of signal. Theory predicted that if the universe began in a hot, dense state, a faint glow from that time should still fill all of space. When Dicke heard about the mysterious hiss, he reportedly told his colleagues: we have been scooped.

> i: horn antenna | v: radio telescope
The two teams published side by side in 1965. Penzias and Wilson simply described an unexplained noise. Dicke's team explained what it meant. In 1978, Penzias and Wilson received the Nobel Prize in Physics. [pause] Two engineers trying to fix a noisy antenna had found the echo of creation.

## Why It Is There
mood: wonder
> i: early universe | v: early universe
For its first few hundred thousand years, the universe was a hot, glowing fog. Matter was a seething plasma of protons and electrons, and light could not travel far before bouncing off a free electron. The universe was opaque, like the inside of a cloud.

> i: early universe | v: big bang
As space expanded, it cooled. When it reached around three thousand degrees, about three hundred and eighty thousand years after the beginning, protons and electrons finally combined into neutral atoms. Suddenly, light could travel freely. [pause] The fog cleared. The universe became transparent.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The light released at that moment has been travelling ever since. Over thirteen point eight billion years, the expansion of space has stretched its waves about a thousand times, cooling it from a red-hot glow to just {{2.7 degrees|2.7 K}} above absolute zero. [pause] The coldest light in the universe, and the oldest.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
That is why it comes from every direction. Wherever we look, far enough away, we are looking back to the moment the fog cleared. [pause] We are surrounded by the wall of the early universe.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
And there is a lot of it. Every cubic centimetre of space, everywhere in the universe, including the room you are in now, contains around four hundred photons of this ancient light. [pause] Far more photons from the Big Bang than from all the stars that have ever shone.

## A Perfect Glow
mood: wonder
> i: cobe satellite | v: satellite orbit
In 1989, NASA launched a satellite called {{COBE|COBE}}, the Cosmic Background Explorer, to measure this light precisely. Its first result was stunning. The light's spectrum matched a perfect curve, the kind of glow given off by an ideal heated object, more perfectly than anything ever measured in nature.

> i: cobe cmb | v: cosmic microwave background
When the result was shown to a meeting of astronomers in 1990, the audience burst into applause. It was the strongest evidence yet that the universe really had begun in a hot, dense state. The Big Bang was no longer just a theory people argued about.

> i: cmb map | v: cosmic microwave background
And COBE found something else, announced in 1992. Tiny variations in the temperature of the glow, from place to place across the sky, of about {{one part in a hundred thousand|1 IN 100,000}}. [pause] Those ripples were the seeds of everything. The slightly denser regions would, over billions of years, grow into galaxies, clusters and the vast web of the cosmos. The work later earned a Nobel Prize.

## The Baby Picture
mood: wonder
> i: wmap | v: satellite orbit
Two more space missions mapped those ripples in ever finer detail. NASA's WMAP, launched in 2001, and the European Space Agency's {{Planck|PLANCK}} satellite, launched in 2009, which produced the most precise map of the cosmic microwave background ever made.

> i: planck cmb | v: cosmic microwave background
From those patterns, scientists measured the age of the universe, about thirteen point eight billion years. They measured its ingredients: about five percent ordinary matter, about twenty seven percent dark matter, and about sixty eight percent dark energy. They found that space is very close to perfectly flat. [pause] All from a picture of a faint, ancient glow.

> i: cmb map | v: cosmic microwave background
It is one of the greatest achievements in science. From the tiny speckles in light that left the early universe before any star had formed, we can read the recipe of the entire cosmos. [pause] Like reading a person's entire life story from their baby photo.

## Strange Patterns
mood: mystery
> i: cmb cold spot | v: cosmic microwave background
But the picture also holds puzzles. There is an unusually large, cold region in the southern sky, called the CMB Cold Spot, colder and bigger than most models expect. Some think it is a statistical fluke. Others suggest it could be a gigantic void in front of it, or something more exotic.

> i: planck cmb | v: cosmic microwave background
There are also hints that the largest patterns in the map line up in ways that are hard to explain, and that one half of the sky is very slightly different from the other. Most cosmologists think these are probably coincidences. [pause] But a few wonder whether they are clues to something we do not yet understand.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
Scientists are also hunting for a faint swirl in the polarisation of this light, a pattern that ripples in space from the very first instant of time could have left behind. Finding it would be direct evidence of what happened in the first fraction of a second. [pause] So far, it has not been found.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: night sky
Tonight, wherever you are, that ancient light is falling on you, from every direction, as it has every moment of your life. A faint whisper from the time the universe first became transparent. [pause] The oldest light there is, still arriving.
