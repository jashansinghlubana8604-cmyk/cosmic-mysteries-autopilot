On the surface of Venus, it is hot enough to melt lead. The air pressure would crush a submarine. Clouds of sulphuric acid hide the sky. It is about as close to hell as any place in the solar system.

But about fifty kilometres up, inside those clouds, something surprising happens. The temperature drops to something like a warm day on Earth. The pressure is about the same as at sea level on our planet. [pause] If you could float there, in a balloon, you would feel almost comfortable.

For decades, a few scientists wondered whether life might exist up there, drifting forever in the clouds of a hellish planet. Most dismissed it.

> i: venus | v: venus

## A Habitable Layer
mood: wonder
> i: venus | v: venus
Venus is almost the same size as the Earth, and in its early history it may have been much more like our planet, perhaps with oceans of water. But a runaway greenhouse effect turned it into a furnace. Today, its surface is around four hundred and sixty degrees.

> i: venus clouds | v: venus clouds
But its atmosphere is enormously thick, and as you rise through it, it cools. Between about forty eight and sixty kilometres up, there is a layer where the temperature ranges roughly between zero and sixty degrees, and the pressure is similar to the Earth's at ground level. [pause] It is the most Earth-like environment anywhere in the solar system, outside the Earth itself.

> i: carl sagan | v: venus clouds
In 1967, Carl Sagan and the biophysicist Harold Morowitz published a paper suggesting that life could, in principle, float in the clouds of Venus. Since then, a handful of researchers have kept the idea alive.

> i: venus akatsuki | v: venus clouds
There is also a long-standing mystery in the clouds. Something in them absorbs ultraviolet light, creating dark, shifting patches. Scientists have proposed various chemicals to explain it, but no single explanation has been confirmed. [pause] A few have wondered whether it could be something biological.

## The Phosphine Announcement
mood: mystery
> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
In 2017, astronomer {{Jane Greaves|JANE GREAVES}} used the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope in Hawaii to look at Venus's atmosphere. She was searching for gases that might hint at life. She found a faint signal that seemed to match {{phosphine|PHOSPHINE}}, a molecule of one phosphorus atom and three hydrogen atoms.

> i: alma telescope | v: radio telescope
In 2019, her team observed Venus again with ALMA, a powerful array of radio telescopes in Chile, and saw a similar signal. In September 2020, they published their result: phosphine in the clouds of Venus, at about twenty parts per billion.

> i: venus clouds | v: venus clouds
Why did that matter? On rocky planets like the Earth, phosphine is not easy to make. It is produced by some microbes living in oxygen-free environments, like swamps and the guts of animals, and by human industry. The team could not find any natural, non-living process that would produce enough of it on Venus. [pause] They did not claim to have found life. But they said life was a possibility they could not rule out.

> i: venus | v: venus
The announcement made headlines everywhere. Some called it possibly the most important discovery in history. Within weeks, it was under heavy fire.

## The Fight
mood: dread
> i: alma telescope | v: radio telescope
Other scientists began reanalysing the data. A problem was found in the way some of the ALMA data had been processed. When it was corrected, the phosphine signal in those observations became much weaker, around a few parts per billion.

> i: venus atmosphere | v: venus clouds
Some researchers argued that the signal could be caused by sulphur dioxide, a gas that is abundant on Venus and absorbs light at a very similar wavelength. Others argued the signal came from higher in the atmosphere, where phosphine should be quickly destroyed by sunlight.

> i: pioneer venus | v: spacecraft
Supporters pointed to old data from NASA's Pioneer Venus probe, which plunged into the atmosphere in 1978. A reanalysis of its measurements found hints that could be consistent with phosphine. And the original team, with new observations, maintained that they still saw a signal. [pause] Years later, the question remains unresolved.

> i: venus clouds | v: venus clouds
The same team later reported tentative hints of ammonia in the clouds as well, another gas that would be surprising to find there. Those results, too, are preliminary and need confirmation. [pause] Venus has become a battleground between hope and caution.

## Could Anything Live There?
mood: mystery
> i: venus clouds | v: venus clouds
Even if phosphine is there, life would face enormous challenges. The cloud droplets are made mostly of concentrated sulphuric acid, far more acidic than anything known to support life on Earth.

> i: venus clouds | v: clouds
And they are incredibly dry. A study in 2021 estimated that the amount of available water in the clouds is far below the minimum that any known microbe needs to survive. [pause] By that measure, the clouds of Venus are drier than the driest place on Earth.

> i: physics laboratory | v: physics laboratory
On the other hand, some scientists have found that certain building blocks of life, like some amino acids, can remain stable in concentrated sulphuric acid. Life on Venus, if it existed, might use a completely different chemistry from life on Earth. [pause] We do not know the limits of what life can do.

> i: venus atmosphere | v: venus clouds
Some scientists have even sketched out how cloud life might survive over long periods. Microbes could live inside droplets, which slowly grow and sink as they collide. As they fall into hotter layers, the droplets would evaporate, leaving the microbes as dried, dormant spores in a haze below the clouds. Rising currents could later lift them back up, where they would reawaken. [pause] A life cycle written in the weather of another planet.

## Going Back
mood: wonder
> i: venus spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
The only way to settle it is to go there. NASA has planned a mission called {{DAVINCI|DAVINCI}}, which would drop a probe through the atmosphere of Venus, sampling gases all the way down to the surface. The European Space Agency is developing EnVision to study the planet from orbit.

> i: venus | v: venus
There have even been plans for small, privately funded probes designed specifically to measure the chemistry of the cloud droplets and look for signs of organic material.

> i: venus airship | v: venus clouds
Some have imagined humans going there too. In 2015, NASA engineers sketched a concept called HAVOC, in which astronauts would live in airships floating about fifty kilometres above the surface, where the pressure and temperature are comfortable. Breathable air would even float in Venus's heavy carbon dioxide atmosphere, like helium does on Earth. [pause] Cities in the clouds of a hellish world.

> i: venus clouds | v: venus clouds
Most scientists think the answer will probably turn out to be some strange, unknown chemistry, rather than life. But the possibility is tantalising. A biosphere floating forever above a planet that died, perhaps the last survivors of a time when Venus had oceans. [pause] Whatever the answer, the question has made the world look at Venus again. Our hellish neighbour may still have secrets in its sky.
