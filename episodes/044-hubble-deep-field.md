## A Risky Idea
mood: mystery
> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
By 1995, the Hubble Space Telescope had already survived a near disaster. When it launched in 1990, its main mirror had a tiny flaw that blurred every image. In 1993, astronauts flew up and fitted it with corrective optics, like a pair of glasses. Finally, Hubble could see clearly.

> i: robert williams | v: telescope control room
The director of the institute that ran Hubble, astronomer {{Robert Williams|ROBERT WILLIAMS}}, had a small amount of the telescope's time that he could spend however he wished. He decided to do something no committee would ever have approved.

> i: ursa major | v: night sky
He would point Hubble at one tiny, empty-looking patch of sky near the handle of the Big Dipper, far from the bright stars and dust of our own galaxy. And he would leave it there, for more than a hundred orbits of the Earth, collecting every faint photon that drifted in.

> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
His own colleagues warned him. If the picture showed nothing, he would have wasted one of the most valuable instruments in science on a black square. Some feared it could damage his reputation. [pause] Williams decided the question was too important not to ask.

## Ten Days Of Darkness
mood: wonder
> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble orbit
Between the eighteenth and the twenty eighth of December 1995, Hubble took more than three hundred and forty exposures of the same spot. Over and over. Light so faint that a few photons arrived per minute was slowly piled up, image on image.

> i: hubble deep field | v: hubble space telescope
The pictures were taken through four different filters, from ultraviolet to near infrared. Over the holidays, a small team worked to align them, clean out the streaks left by cosmic rays, and combine them into a single colour image. [pause] Nobody knew exactly what would appear until the layers came together.

> i: hubble deep field | v: hubble deep field
The patch was tiny. About one twenty-four-millionth of the whole sky. Like looking at the sky through a drinking straw, from across a room. Imagine a tennis ball seen from a hundred metres away. That small.

> i: hubble deep field | v: hubble deep field zoom
When the exposures were combined, the result was released in January 1996. And it changed astronomy. The so-called empty patch contained around three thousand galaxies. Some close. Some so distant that their light had been travelling for more than ten billion years. [pause] There was no such thing as empty sky.

> i: hubble deep field galaxies | v: galaxy zoom
And here is the part that is hard to take in. Hubble had not been lucky. That patch was chosen because it was ordinary. Point anywhere else in the sky, and you would see the same thing. [pause] Every speck of darkness above you is full of galaxies.

## Deeper And Deeper
mood: wonder
> i: hubble ultra deep field | v: hubble ultra deep field
Astronomers could not resist going further. In 2003 and 2004, with a new, more sensitive camera, Hubble stared at another patch in the southern sky for about eleven days. The {{Hubble Ultra Deep Field|ULTRA DEEP FIELD}} revealed around ten thousand galaxies.

> i: hubble ultra deep field | v: galaxy zoom
Some of them are tiny red smudges, seen as they were less than a billion years after the Big Bang, when the universe was a small fraction of its current age. These were baby galaxies, lumpy and irregular, still assembling themselves from clumps of stars.

> i: hubble extreme deep field | v: hubble extreme deep field
In 2012, astronomers combined ten years of images of the same spot into the eXtreme Deep Field, built from about two million seconds of exposure, more than twenty three days. It showed about five and a half thousand galaxies in an even smaller patch of sky.

> i: galaxies | v: galaxy zoom
Each deep field is a core sample through time. The nearby galaxies are the present. The faint ones behind them are the past. [pause] And the faintest red smudges are some of the oldest things we have ever seen.

> i: redshift | v: galaxy zoom
Why red? Because the universe is expanding. As light crosses billions of years of growing space, its waves are stretched, and stretched light becomes redder. The further away a galaxy is, the more its light is stretched. [pause] The colour of each smudge is a clock, telling us how long its light has been travelling.

## How Many Galaxies Are There?
mood: wonder
> i: observable universe | v: observable universe
The deep fields let astronomers do something remarkable. By counting the galaxies in one tiny patch and multiplying by the size of the whole sky, they could estimate how many galaxies there are in the entire observable universe.

> i: hubble deep field | v: galaxy zoom
The answer is somewhere in the {{hundreds of billions|HUNDREDS OF BILLIONS}}. One study in 2016 even suggested two trillion, if you include all the faint, small galaxies we cannot yet see. Later measurements, including some made by the New Horizons spacecraft far beyond Pluto, suggest the real number is lower. But still, hundreds of billions.

> i: milky way galaxy | v: milky way galaxy
Every one of those galaxies contains, on average, billions of stars. Most of those stars have planets. Our Milky Way, everything we know, everything that has ever happened to anyone, is one galaxy among all of them. [pause] One smudge, in a picture full of smudges.

## Webb Looks Again
mood: mystery
> i: webb deep field | v: webb first deep field
On the eleventh of July 2022, the first full-colour image from the James Webb Space Telescope was shown to the world. It was another deep field. A patch of sky about the size of a grain of sand at arm's length, toward a galaxy cluster called {{SMACS 0723|SMACS 0723}}.

> i: webb deep field | v: webb deep field zoom
Hubble's first deep field had taken ten days. Webb took about twelve and a half hours. And it saw thousands of galaxies, including faint, warped arcs of light from galaxies behind the cluster, bent and magnified by its gravity.

> i: webb jades | v: webb deep field
Since then, Webb has spent hundreds of hours staring at patches of so-called empty sky. It has found galaxies from just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang, galaxies that are brighter and more developed than anyone expected that early. [pause] The deeper we look, the stranger the beginning of the universe seems.

> i: webb jades | v: webb deep field
In 2024 and 2025, Webb confirmed galaxies whose light left them less than three hundred million years after the Big Bang. When that light set out, the universe was about two percent of its current age. [pause] And those galaxies were already shining brightly, as if they had been in a hurry to exist.

> i: hubble deep field | v: hubble deep field zoom
It all began with one director, one risky decision, and one empty patch of sky that nobody thought was worth looking at. [pause] Next time you look up at a dark gap between the stars, remember. It is not empty. It is full of galaxies, too far away to see. Waiting for someone patient enough to look.
