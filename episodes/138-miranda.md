In January 1986, the Voyager 2 spacecraft flew past Uranus, the first and only visit ever made to that distant world. In the final hours, it passed close to a small icy moon called Miranda, and sent back pictures that left scientists stunned.

## The Smallest Round Moon
mood: wonder
> i: uranus moons | v: uranus
Miranda was discovered in 1948 by the astronomer Gerard Kuiper, the same astronomer whose name was later given to the Kuiper Belt. It was the last of Uranus's five large moons to be found, and it is the smallest of them, only about four hundred and seventy kilometres across.

> i: miranda | v: miranda
That makes it one of the smallest bodies in the solar system known to have been pulled into a roughly round shape by its own gravity. Like Uranus's other moons, it was named after a character from Shakespeare, the heroine of The Tempest.

> i: uranus | v: uranus rotation
For almost forty years, Miranda was just a faint dot near Uranus. Then Voyager 2 arrived. Because of the way its route was planned, the spacecraft passed closer to Miranda than to any other moon of Uranus. [pause] So, by chance, the smallest of the five became the best photographed.

> i: uranus | v: uranus rotation
Miranda circles Uranus once every thirty four hours, closer than any of the other large moons. And Uranus itself is tipped on its side, its axis tilted by almost ninety eight degrees, so its moons orbit around its equator like a giant bullseye pointed at the Sun. At this distance, nearly three billion kilometres from the Sun, Miranda's surface sits at around minus one hundred and ninety degrees Celsius. [pause] A frozen world, in a sideways system, at the edge of the light.

## A Patchwork World
mood: mystery
> i: miranda voyager | v: miranda
Voyager's images revealed a surface unlike any other. Large areas of Miranda are old, rolling, cratered terrain. But cutting across them are three enormous regions called {{coronae|CORONAE}}, each hundreds of kilometres across, covered in parallel ridges and grooves.

> i: miranda | v: miranda
One, called Inverness Corona, has a strange, bright chevron shape at its centre, like a giant letter V. Another, Arden Corona, looks like a racetrack, with grooves running around its edges. Another, Elsinore, is covered in curving bands. [pause] It looks as if different worlds have been glued together.

> i: miranda | v: miranda
The boundaries between these regions are sharp, with steep cliffs, faults and canyons. Something tore Miranda's crust apart in a dramatic way. And because the coronae have relatively few craters, they seem younger than the surrounding terrain.

> i: miranda voyager | v: miranda
Miranda is made of a mixture of water ice and rock, roughly in similar amounts. A world that small should have frozen solid early in its history and stayed quiet ever since, like a cratered ball of ice. Many small icy moons look exactly like that. [pause] Miranda does not. Something gave this tiny moon enough energy to rip and rebuild its surface.

## The Tallest Cliff
mood: dread
> i: verona rupes | v: miranda
Among Miranda's features is a cliff called {{Verona Rupes|VERONA RUPES}}. Estimates of its height vary, but it is thought to be several kilometres tall, perhaps up to around ten kilometres, and some estimates go higher. It is often described as the tallest known cliff in the solar system.

> i: verona rupes | v: miranda
For comparison, the tallest cliffs on Earth are about one and a quarter kilometres high. Verona Rupes may be many times taller, on a moon less than five hundred kilometres wide.

> i: miranda | v: miranda
And Miranda's gravity is incredibly weak, less than one percent of the Earth's. If you stepped off the top of Verona Rupes, you would fall very slowly at first, and it could take several minutes to reach the bottom. [pause] By the end, you would be moving at around the speed of a car on a motorway. A long, silent fall, with Uranus hanging in the sky above you.

> i: verona rupes | v: miranda
The most famous image of Verona Rupes was taken by Voyager 2 from the side, catching the cliff almost edge-on, a bright wall of ice rising out of the shadows. It is thought to be a giant fault, where the crust was pulled apart and one side dropped far below the other. [pause] And because it is so sharp and has so few craters, it may be relatively young, as moons go.

## What Happened To Miranda?
mood: mystery
> i: asteroid impact | v: asteroid impact
The first explanation was dramatic. Perhaps Miranda had been shattered by a giant impact, and its pieces had drifted back together under gravity, landing in a jumbled mess. That idea gave rise to the Frankenstein nickname.

> i: miranda interior | v: miranda
Later studies suggested something more subtle. Miranda's orbit around Uranus may once have been more stretched than it is now, so Uranus's tides would have squeezed and heated its interior. Warm, buoyant ice could have risen through the crust in great slow plumes, cracking and pushing up the surface to form the coronae. [pause] Instead of being smashed apart, Miranda may have been churned from within.

> i: miranda | v: uranus moons
In 2024, a study used computer models of Miranda's surface features to suggest that it may have had a subsurface ocean, possibly around a hundred kilometres deep, within the last few hundred million years. Some of that ocean might even survive today. [pause] A tiny moon, at the edge of the solar system, might once have been an ocean world.

> i: uranus moons | v: uranus
What could have stretched Miranda's orbit in the first place? The likely answer is a gravitational dance with its neighbours. At times in the past, Miranda's orbit may have fallen into step with another moon, such as Umbriel, the two repeatedly tugging on each other at the same point in their paths. Those regular tugs could have pumped up Miranda's orbit and its internal heat. [pause] One clue survives today: Miranda's orbit is tilted by a few degrees, much more than the other large moons, as if something disturbed it long ago.

## Waiting For A Return
mood: wonder
> i: voyager 2 | v: voyager 2
Voyager 2 only imaged the southern hemisphere of Miranda, because the northern half was in darkness during the flyby. Half of this strange moon has never been seen.

> i: uranus | v: spacecraft
A dedicated mission to Uranus has been identified as a top priority for planetary science. If it goes ahead, it would orbit the planet for years and make repeated passes by its moons, including Miranda. [pause] It could finally reveal the rest of the Frankenstein moon, and whether an ocean still lurks beneath its scars.

> i: voyager 2 | v: spacecraft
In 2022, the American planetary science community named a Uranus orbiter and probe as its highest priority for a new flagship mission. Uranus is so far away that the journey would take more than a decade, and launch windows that use Jupiter's gravity to speed the trip are rare. [pause] So any spacecraft that reaches Miranda will likely leave Earth years before it arrives.

> i: miranda voyager | v: miranda
A small, cold moon, named after a heroine of Shakespeare. A patchwork surface, a cliff taller than any on Earth, and perhaps a hidden sea. [pause] We have seen it only once, for a few hours, almost forty years ago. Imagine what we will find when we go back.
