Beneath the ice of Jupiter's moon Europa, there is an ocean. It holds roughly twice as much water as all the oceans on Earth combined. It has been there for billions of years, in total darkness.

> i: europa surface | v: europa
It may be one of the best places in the solar system to look for life beyond the Earth. [pause] And right now, a spacecraft is on its way there.

> i: europa clipper | v: rocket launch
On the fourteenth of October 2024, a Falcon Heavy rocket launched NASA's {{Europa Clipper|EUROPA CLIPPER}} from Florida. With its solar panels open, it is about as wide as a basketball court is long. It is the largest spacecraft NASA has ever built for a mission to another planet.

> i: europa clipper | v: europa clipper
Its job is not to find life directly. It is to answer a simpler, more important question first. [pause] Could anything live in Europa's hidden ocean?

## Why Europa
mood: wonder
> i: europa | v: europa rotating
Europa is a little smaller than our Moon. From afar, it looks like a cracked white billiard ball, its icy surface criss-crossed with dark lines and ridges. There are very few craters, which means the surface is geologically young, constantly renewed from below.

> i: europa ice shell | v: europa interior
In the 1990s, NASA's Galileo spacecraft found strong evidence for a salty ocean beneath the ice. As Europa moved through Jupiter's magnetic field, it created a magnetic response of its own, like a conductor, exactly as expected for a global layer of salty water.

> i: jupiter europa | v: jupiter
The ocean stays liquid because of Jupiter. As Europa orbits, the giant planet's gravity squeezes and flexes it, generating heat inside. [pause] That heat may power hydrothermal vents on the seafloor, similar to the ones where life thrives in the deep oceans of the Earth.

> i: hydrothermal vent | v: hydrothermal vent
Life as we know it needs three things: liquid water, the right chemical ingredients and a source of energy. Europa may have all three. [pause] Clipper's goal is to find out.

> i: europa surface | v: james webb telescope
There are already clues. In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope found carbon dioxide on Europa's surface, concentrated in a region of young, disrupted ice called Tara Regio. The carbon most likely came up from the ocean below. [pause] Carbon is one of the key ingredients of life as we know it.

## Built For Radiation
mood: dread
> i: jupiter radiation belts | v: jupiter magnetosphere
There is one big problem. Europa orbits inside Jupiter's radiation belts, one of the harshest environments in the solar system. Standing unprotected on its surface, a human would receive a deadly dose of radiation within days.

> i: europa clipper | v: spacecraft
A spacecraft orbiting Europa would be destroyed quickly. So Clipper will not orbit Europa at all. Instead, it will orbit {{Jupiter|JUPITER}} on long loops, diving past Europa about {{forty nine times|49 FLYBYS}} and then retreating to safer distances to send its data home.

> i: europa clipper vault | v: spacecraft
Its most sensitive electronics are protected inside a thick-walled vault made of aluminium and zinc. [pause] In 2024, engineers discovered that some of its transistors might not withstand the radiation as well as hoped. After months of testing, they concluded the spacecraft would survive, by adjusting its path through the most intense regions.

> i: europa surface | v: europa flyby
On its closest passes, Clipper will skim just {{twenty five kilometres|25 KM}} above Europa's surface. Each flyby will cover a different part of the moon. [pause] Over time, they will build up a map of almost the whole globe.

## Nine Instruments
mood: mystery
> i: europa clipper | v: europa clipper
Clipper carries nine science instruments, plus a radio experiment to measure Europa's gravity. Together, they will study the ice, the ocean beneath it, and the moon's thin atmosphere.

> i: europa ice shell | v: europa interior
One of the most important is a radar called {{REASON|REASON}}. It sends radio waves down into the ice and listens for echoes, to measure how thick the shell is. Estimates range from just a few kilometres to more than twenty. [pause] It may also find pockets of liquid water trapped inside the ice itself.

> i: europa surface | v: europa
Cameras will photograph the surface in fine detail. Spectrometers will map the salts, ices and organic compounds on the surface, some of which may have come up from the ocean. A thermal camera will search for warm spots that could reveal recent eruptions.

> i: europa plumes | v: europa plumes
Two instruments will sample the space around Europa directly. One, called {{MASPEX|MASPEX}}, will analyse gases. The other, called {{SUDA|SUDA}}, will catch tiny dust grains knocked off the surface, and measure what they are made of. [pause] If Europa really vents water into space, as the Hubble telescope has hinted, Clipper may fly straight through it and taste the ocean.

> i: europa | v: europa rotating
A magnetometer will measure the ocean's depth and saltiness by how it responds to Jupiter's magnetic field. [pause] By the end, scientists hope to know how deep the ocean is, how salty, and whether it has the chemistry and energy life would need.

## The Long Road
mood: epic
> i: mars | v: mars rotating
Even with a powerful rocket, Clipper cannot fly straight to Jupiter. It needs extra speed from the gravity of other planets. In March 2025, it swept past Mars. At the end of 2026, it was due to swing past the Earth for another boost.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotating
It is scheduled to arrive at Jupiter in {{April 2030|APRIL 2030}}, after a journey of nearly three billion kilometres. Its main science flybys of Europa should run for about four years.

> i: europa clipper | v: spacecraft
At Jupiter, sunlight is about twenty five times weaker than at Earth. That is why Clipper's solar panels are so enormous, more than thirty metres from tip to tip. [pause] Even so, the whole spacecraft will run on around six hundred watts, less than a single household hair dryer.

> i: juice spacecraft | v: spacecraft
It will not be alone. The European Space Agency's {{JUICE|JUICE}} mission, launched in April 2023, is also headed for Jupiter, arriving in 2031. JUICE will fly past Europa twice, then go into orbit around Ganymede, another moon thought to hold a hidden ocean. [pause] For the first time, two spacecraft will be studying Jupiter's ocean worlds at once.

> i: europa clipper | v: europa clipper
Clipper also carries a message. Engraved on a metal plate on its vault is a poem by the American poet laureate Ada Limón, written for the mission, along with the word water in over a hundred languages. A microchip holds the names of more than two and a half million people who signed up to send them along.

## What If
mood: wonder
> i: europa | v: europa
Clipper cannot see life. It has no microscope, and it cannot drill through the ice. But it can tell us whether Europa's ocean is habitable, and where a future lander should go to look.

> i: europa lander | v: europa surface
If it finds thin ice, warm spots, organic molecules and plumes of ocean water, the case for sending a lander will be overwhelming. [pause] Such a mission could one day scoop up material that came from the ocean and search it for signs of life.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotating
Finding life on Europa would change everything. It would mean life began twice in one solar system, in completely separate places. [pause] And if it happened twice here, it has likely happened countless times across the galaxy.

> i: europa | v: europa rotating
For now, Clipper is still crossing the dark between the planets, carrying a poem, millions of names and nine instruments built to answer one of humanity's oldest questions. [pause] In 2030, it arrives. And under that cracked ice, the ocean is waiting.
