Venus is wrapped in clouds so thick that no ordinary telescope has ever seen its surface. For most of history, nobody knew what lay beneath. Jungles, some imagined. Oceans. Swamps full of strange life.

> i: venus magellan | v: venus
In the early 1990s, a spacecraft called Magellan used radar to peer through the clouds and map almost the entire planet. What it revealed was a world shaped by fire. Volcanoes everywhere. Tens of thousands of them. Lava plains that cover most of the surface.

> i: venus volcano | v: lava
But were any of them still active? For decades, nobody could say for sure. The surface looked young, but no one had ever caught a volcano in the act. [pause] Then, in 2023, two scientists went back through those old radar images, frame by frame.

They found something that had been hiding in the data for more than thirty years. A volcanic vent that had changed shape in just eight months. [pause] Venus, it turns out, is still erupting.

## A Planet Covered In Volcanoes
mood: wonder
> i: venus magellan | v: venus
NASA's Magellan spacecraft arrived at Venus in 1990 and spent four years mapping its surface with radar, bouncing radio waves off the ground and timing the echoes. It mapped around ninety eight percent of the planet in remarkable detail.

> i: venus volcano | v: venus
The surface it revealed was dominated by volcanism. Vast lava plains. Long, winding channels carved by flowing lava, one of them about seven thousand kilometres long, longer than the River Nile. Strange pancake-shaped domes of thick lava. And huge shield volcanoes, rising kilometres above the plains.

> i: maat mons | v: venus
The tallest, {{Maat Mons|MAAT MONS}}, rises about eight kilometres above the average surface. A study in 2023 catalogued around eighty five thousand volcanoes across the planet, most of them small. [pause] Venus has more volcanoes than any other planet in the solar system.

> i: venus surface | v: venus
And the surface is young. Venus has surprisingly few impact craters, only about a thousand. That suggests most of its surface was covered in fresh lava within the last several hundred million years. [pause] Something resurfaced the entire planet, relatively recently.

## Hints Of Fire
mood: mystery
> i: venus express | v: spacecraft
Between 2006 and 2014, the European Space Agency's Venus Express spacecraft orbited Venus. It could not see the surface directly, but it could measure heat glowing through the clouds in certain wavelengths of infrared light.

> i: venus volcano | v: lava
It found regions that seemed to be covered in relatively fresh lava, less weathered by the harsh atmosphere than older rock. At one volcano, called {{Idunn Mons|IDUNN MONS}}, the flows looked perhaps only a few hundred thousand years old, or even younger.

> i: venus clouds | v: venus clouds
Venus Express also saw the amount of sulphur dioxide in the upper atmosphere rise and fall dramatically. On Earth, volcanoes are a major source of sulphur dioxide. Some scientists suggested the swings could be caused by eruptions. [pause] Others thought the atmosphere itself could be responsible. The evidence was tantalising, but not conclusive.

> i: venera lander | v: venus surface
Earlier, in the 1970s and 1980s, Soviet Venera landers had survived on the surface for an hour or two, long enough to photograph flat, slabby rocks and analyse them. Most of what they found was basalt, the same kind of volcanic rock that makes up the ocean floors of the Earth and the islands of Hawaii. [pause] Every clue pointed to a volcanic world.

## The Vent That Changed
mood: dread
> i: venus magellan | v: venus
Magellan imaged parts of Venus more than once, months apart, as it orbited. In 2023, geophysicist Robert Herrick and radar expert Scott Hensley realised that comparing those repeated images might reveal changes. It was painstaking work. The images were taken from different angles, and computer tools could not compare them automatically.

> i: maat mons | v: venus
Herrick searched by eye, for hundreds of hours. Near Maat Mons, he found a volcanic vent. In images from February 1991, it was roughly circular and about two square kilometres in area. In images from October 1991, it had become irregular, about twice as large, and appeared to be filled almost to the brim with something. [pause] A lava lake.

> i: lava lake | v: lava
The team ran computer models of different possibilities, including landslides and differences in viewing angle. The best explanation was a volcanic eruption, happening while Magellan was watching. They published their findings in the journal Science in 2023. [pause] The first direct evidence of an active volcano on Venus.

> i: lava | v: lava
Lava on Venus would behave differently from lava on Earth. The surface is already around four hundred and sixty degrees, so lava would cool much more slowly and could flow for longer distances before freezing. [pause] On Venus, a lava flow might keep glowing long after one on Earth would have turned to black stone.

## More Lava
mood: dread
> i: venus volcano | v: lava
In 2024, a team of Italian scientists published another study. They compared Magellan images of two other regions, Sif Mons and Niobe Planitia, and found changes that looked like {{new lava flows|NEW LAVA FLOWS}} appearing between observations.

> i: lava | v: lava
The new flows were estimated to cover tens of square kilometres, with enough lava to fill tens of thousands of Olympic swimming pools in one region. That suggests Venus may be erupting on a scale similar to Earth's volcanoes today.

> i: venus surface | v: venus
And these discoveries came from just a few small areas, using data that was never designed to look for changes. [pause] If eruptions show up there, they are probably happening all over the planet.

> i: venus magellan | v: venus
Why did it take thirty years to notice? Magellan's images were low resolution by today's standards, and the spacecraft viewed each region from different angles on different passes, making changes very hard to spot. Only in recent years have scientists had the time, tools and motivation to comb through the archive this way. [pause] The evidence had been sitting in a database since 1991.

## Going Back
mood: wonder
> i: venus spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
Several missions are being prepared to study Venus in much more detail. NASA's {{VERITAS|VERITAS}} would map the surface with far sharper radar than Magellan, and could spot changes caused by ongoing volcanism. The European Space Agency's EnVision would study the surface and interior from orbit.

> i: venus | v: venus
NASA's DAVINCI mission would drop a probe through the atmosphere, measuring its chemistry all the way down and taking pictures of the surface as it falls. Together, these missions could show whether Venus is still geologically alive, and why it became so different from the Earth.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
That matters for us. Venus and the Earth are nearly the same size, formed at roughly the same time from similar materials. Yet one became a living blue planet, and the other became a furnace. Volcanism may be part of the reason. The Earth releases its inner heat steadily, through moving plates and volcanoes along their edges. Venus has no moving plates. Some scientists think it may instead build up heat for hundreds of millions of years, then resurface itself in great outbursts of lava across the whole planet. [pause] Understanding Venus's fire may help us understand what keeps a planet habitable, and what makes one fail.

> i: maat mons | v: venus
Somewhere under those thick yellow clouds, right now, lava may be spilling out of a vent on the slopes of Maat Mons, glowing in a world where the air itself is hotter than an oven. [pause] Venus is not a dead planet. It is a planet whose fire never went out.
