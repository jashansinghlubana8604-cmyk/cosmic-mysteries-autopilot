Jupiter is often called a failed star. It is made of almost the same stuff as the Sun, mostly hydrogen and helium. It gives off more heat than it receives. It has a family of moons circling it like a miniature solar system.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter
So what if it had not failed? What if, four and a half billion years ago, Jupiter had gathered enough gas to ignite, and become a second sun in our sky?

> i: binary star artist impression | v: binary star
It is not a crazy idea. Around half of all stars like our Sun have a companion star. Two-sun systems are everywhere in the galaxy. [pause] Ours just happens to be a single.

> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
Let's turn Jupiter into a star, and follow what happens. To the Earth. To our night sky. To Jupiter's moons. [pause] Some of it is beautiful. And some of it is terrifying.

## How Far From A Star?
mood: mystery
> i: jupiter | v: jupiter clouds
First, a correction. Jupiter is not really a failed star. Stars form by the direct collapse of a cloud of gas. Jupiter is thought to have formed like a planet, building a solid core first and then sweeping up gas around it. [pause] But it is still worth asking how close it came.

> i: brown dwarf | v: brown dwarf
To start fusing ordinary hydrogen in its core, the way the Sun does, an object needs to be about {{80 times heavier|80 × JUPITER}} than Jupiter. Below about thirteen Jupiter masses, nothing ignites at all. In between lie brown dwarfs, objects that glow faintly for a while, burning a rare form of hydrogen, then slowly fade.

> i: jupiter | v: jupiter rotation
So Jupiter was not close. It would need eighty times more mass. But imagine it got it. Imagine that, in the early solar system, the cloud around Jupiter had been far richer, and it had swallowed enough gas to cross the line. [pause] Our solar system would have been born with two suns.

> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
And here is the surprising part. The new star would barely be bigger than Jupiter is today. Squeezed by its own gravity, it would be about the same size, but eighty times heavier. A {{red dwarf|RED DWARF}}. The smallest kind of true star, and the most common kind in the entire galaxy.

## The Second Sun In Our Sky
mood: wonder
> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
A tiny red dwarf is dim. Very dim. It would shine with only a few ten-thousandths of the Sun's power. Placed where Jupiter is, more than four times further from us than the Sun, it would not warm the Earth at all.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
But it would change the night. From the Earth, it would look like an intense red-orange point of light, a few times brighter than the full Moon. Bright enough to read by. Bright enough to cast clear red shadows.

> i: sunset | v: sunset
For months of the year, it would rise as the Sun set, and nights would never be truly dark. At other times, it would sit in the daytime sky, a fierce red point visible beside the Sun. [pause] Every culture in human history would have grown up with two lights in the sky. Our myths, our calendars, our religions would all be different.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
What about its gravity on us? Eighty Jupiters sounds frightening. But it would still be only around a twelfth of the Sun's mass, and far away. Its tides on the Earth would be tiny. The Earth's orbit, close to the Sun, would very probably stay stable. [pause] We would keep our seasons, our oceans and our year.

## The Wreckage
mood: dread
> i: asteroid belt | v: asteroid belt
Further out, things would be very different. With two stars orbiting each other, only certain regions of space would allow planets to move in stable orbits. Close to the Sun, everything would be calm. But the zone between the Sun and the new star would be a battlefield.

> i: asteroid belt | v: asteroids
The asteroid belt, which already lies between Mars and Jupiter, would probably be torn apart. Simulations of double-star systems suggest stable orbits around the main star would only extend out to around two Earth-Sun distances. {{Beyond that, chaos|CHAOS}}.

> i: saturn | v: saturn rings
And Saturn, Uranus and Neptune? A planet orbiting too close to two stars at once gets kicked around by both. Saturn would likely be thrown into a wild orbit, or flung out of the solar system entirely. The outer planets as we know them might never have formed at all. [pause] The solar system would be smaller, and emptier.

> i: comet | v: comet
Even the comets would change. Jupiter today helps shape the paths of comets, sometimes flinging them away, sometimes steering them towards us. A star in its place would do that far more violently. Our early Earth might have been bombarded more, or less. [pause] Nobody can say for sure which.

## Moons Turned Into Planets
mood: wonder
> i: jupiter moons | v: jupiter moons
And then there are Jupiter's moons. Today, Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto circle a planet. In our thought experiment, they would be circling a star. And they are very close to it.

> i: europa | v: europa
Europa, with its hidden ocean under a shell of ice, would suddenly be receiving many times more energy than the Earth gets from the Sun. Its ice would boil away. Its ocean would turn to steam. [pause] The best place to look for life in the solar system would be scorched.

> i: ganymede | v: ganymede
Ganymede, a bit further out, would also be far too hot. But {{Callisto|CALLISTO}}, the outermost of the four, might end up with roughly as much energy as the Earth gets. A frozen moon, suddenly sitting in the warm zone of a little red sun.

> i: callisto | v: callisto
Could Callisto have become a living world? It is smaller than Mercury, perhaps too small to hold onto a thick atmosphere for billions of years. And red dwarfs, especially when young, spit out violent flares that could strip a nearby moon bare. [pause] But for a while, at least, there might have been a second habitable world, right next door.

## Two Suns Are Normal
mood: wonder
> i: binary star artist impression | v: binary star
Here is the strangest thought of all. A solar system like this would not be unusual. It would be ordinary. Most stars in the galaxy are red dwarfs, and many stars live in pairs or larger groups.

> i: exoplanet two suns | v: binary star
Astronomers have already found real planets orbiting two stars at once, like {{Kepler-16b|KEPLER-16 b}}, where a sunset would show two suns going down together. And they have found planets orbiting one star of a pair, while the other shines in their sky like a bright red lamp.

> i: alpha centauri | v: star field
Our nearest neighbours, the Alpha Centauri system, are a triple. Two Sun-like stars orbiting each other, and a tiny red dwarf, Proxima, circling far out. If anyone lived on a planet there, our single, lonely Sun might look strange to them.

> i: jupiter juno | v: jupiter rotation
So Jupiter did not fail. It became exactly what it needed to be, a giant planet that helped shape a quiet, stable system. Stable enough for a small blue world to stay in the same orbit for four and a half billion years. [pause] Long enough for life to begin, evolve, and finally look up, and wonder what would have happened if there had been two suns.
