About one point three billion years ago, when life on Earth was still nothing more than single cells, two black holes were circling each other in a distant galaxy. Each was around thirty times heavier than the Sun.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
In their final moments, they whirled around each other dozens of times a second, then collided and merged. In a fraction of a second, they turned about three Suns' worth of mass into pure energy. [pause] Not as light. As a ripple in space itself.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
That ripple spread out across the universe at the speed of light. For more than a billion years it travelled, growing weaker and weaker. By the time it reached the Earth, it stretched and squeezed our planet by far less than the width of an atom.

And on the fourteenth of September 2015, at 09:50 universal time, two machines in the United States felt it. [pause] For the first time in history, humanity heard the universe shake.

## Einstein's Doubt
mood: mystery
> i: albert einstein | v: spacetime curvature
In 1916, Albert Einstein realised that his new theory of gravity predicted something strange. If mass bends space and time, then moving masses should send out waves in that bending, rippling outward at the speed of light. {{Gravitational waves|GRAVITATIONAL WAVES}}.

> i: spacetime curvature | v: spacetime curvature
But Einstein also calculated how weak those waves would be. Even from the most violent events imaginable, they would be unimaginably tiny by the time they reached us. He doubted anyone would ever detect them. At one point, he even wondered whether they were real at all.

> i: physics laboratory | v: physics laboratory
In the 1960s, a physicist named Joseph Weber built large aluminium cylinders, hoping a passing wave would make them ring like a bell. He announced detections. But nobody else could reproduce them, and his claims were eventually rejected. [pause] The waves seemed to be beyond reach.

> i: binary pulsar | v: neutron star
Then, in 1974, astronomers found a pair of neutron stars orbiting each other. Over the years, their orbit shrank at exactly the rate Einstein's theory predicted, as if energy was leaking away in gravitational waves. It was strong indirect evidence. [pause] But nobody had felt a wave directly.

## A Machine To Feel Space
mood: wonder
> i: ligo | v: ligo
The plan to catch them took decades. Physicists including Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne and Ronald Drever designed an instrument called {{LIGO|LIGO}}, the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory. Two identical detectors, three thousand kilometres apart, in Louisiana and Washington State.

> i: ligo aerial | v: ligo
Each detector is shaped like a giant letter L, with two arms four kilometres long, inside vacuum tubes emptier than outer space. A laser beam is split in two, sent down each arm, bounced back and forth by mirrors, and recombined.

> i: ligo | v: laser
Normally, the two beams cancel each other out perfectly. But if a gravitational wave passes through, it stretches one arm and squeezes the other, by a minuscule amount. The beams no longer match. A flicker of light appears. [pause] That flicker is the universe moving.

> i: ligo | v: ligo
The change LIGO has to detect is about a ten-thousandth of the width of a proton. Like measuring the distance to the nearest star to the width of a human hair. Trucks on distant roads, ocean waves crashing on far-off shores and even the vibrations of atoms in the mirrors all had to be filtered out.

## The Chirp
mood: wonder
> i: ligo | v: ligo
In September 2015, LIGO had just been upgraded. It was not even officially switched on for science yet. The engineers were still running final tests. And at 09:50 universal time on the fourteenth, a signal swept through both detectors.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
It hit the detector in Louisiana first, then, seven thousandths of a second later, the one in Washington. Exactly the delay you would expect for something travelling at the speed of light. It lasted about two tenths of a second.

> i: computer data | v: data visualization
The first person to see it was a young researcher named Marco Drago, working at the Max Planck Institute in Hannover, Germany, where it was still morning. An automatic program flagged the event within minutes. He looked at the plots and could hardly believe them. The signal was so clean, so perfectly shaped, that his first thought was that it might be a test. [pause] It was not.

> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
The wave rose in strength and pitch, faster and faster, then stopped abruptly. When the data was turned into sound, it made a short upward sweep, a {{chirp|THE CHIRP}}. It matched, almost perfectly, the predicted signal from two black holes spiralling together and merging.

> i: ligo control room | v: physics laboratory
The team spent five months checking it, ruling out every other possibility, including the chance that someone had secretly injected a fake signal to test them. On the eleventh of February 2016, they announced it to the world. [pause] Gravitational waves were real. Einstein had been right, a hundred years later.

## The Most Powerful Event Ever Seen
mood: dread
> i: black hole merger | v: black hole merger
The signal, named {{GW150914|GW150914}}, came from two black holes, about thirty six and twenty nine times the mass of the Sun. They merged into a single black hole of about sixty two Suns.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
The missing three Suns' worth of mass had been converted into gravitational waves, in a fraction of a second. At its peak, the merger was releasing more power than all the stars in the observable universe combined. [pause] And yet it gave off no light at all. We would never have known it happened without LIGO.

> i: nobel prize | v: physics laboratory
In 2017, Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne and Barry Barish received the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. A new way of studying the universe had been born. Not with light, but with the trembling of space itself.

## Listening To The Universe
mood: wonder
> i: black hole merger | v: gravitational waves
Since 2015, LIGO, together with the Virgo detector in Italy and KAGRA in Japan, has detected hundreds of collisions. Mostly pairs of black holes, but also neutron stars, and mixed pairs of the two. Some of the black holes are heavier than astronomers thought stars could make.

> i: neutron star merger | v: neutron star merger
In 2017, a collision between two neutron stars was both felt as gravitational waves and seen as a flash of light, revealing where much of the gold in the universe is made. Each detection is a message written in the shape of space.

> i: lisa spacecraft | v: spacecraft
The next step is even bolder. The European Space Agency is planning a mission called {{LISA|LISA}}, three spacecraft flying in a vast triangle millions of kilometres across, connected by lasers. It will listen for the slower, deeper waves from giant black holes merging at the hearts of galaxies.

> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
And in 2023, astronomers announced evidence of something even slower. By timing dozens of pulsars across our galaxy for many years, they found tiny, shared shifts in their ticking, the signature of a constant background of gravitational waves washing through the Milky Way. [pause] Probably the combined hum of giant black hole pairs, orbiting each other all across the universe.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
Right now, as you watch this, gravitational waves are passing through you. Through the Earth, through everyone you know. Faint ripples from collisions across the universe, stretching and squeezing everything by amounts too small to feel. [pause] For all of human history, the universe has been shaking. In 2015, we finally felt it.
