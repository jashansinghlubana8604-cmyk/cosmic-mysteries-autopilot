## The Burst In The Archive
mood: mystery
> i: parkes radio telescope | v: radio telescope dish
In 2007, an astronomer named Duncan Lorimer asked a student, David Narkevic, to search through old data from the Parkes radio telescope in Australia. They were looking for pulsars, spinning dead stars that flash like lighthouses.

> v: pulsar | i: pulsar artist impression
Buried in recordings from 2001, Narkevic found something that was not a pulsar. It was a single, enormous spike of radio waves. It lasted less than five thousandths of a second. [pause] And it never repeated.

> v: radio waves animation | i: radio telescope
What made it astonishing was a detail called dispersion. When radio waves travel through space, they pass through thin clouds of electrons. Those electrons slow down the lower frequencies slightly more than the higher ones, so a sharp burst arrives smeared out, with the high notes first and the low notes trailing behind.

> v: zoom deep field | i: distant galaxies
The more matter a signal passes through, the bigger that delay. And this burst was delayed far more than anything inside our own galaxy could explain. It had travelled through so much space that it must have come from billions of light years away.

> v: galaxy flight | i: galaxy cluster
For something that far away to look that bright, it would have to be unimaginably powerful. The discovery was published, and became known as the {{Lorimer Burst|THE LORIMER BURST}}. [pause] And then, for years, almost nobody could find another one.

## The Microwave Oven Mystery
mood: wonder
> i: parkes observatory | v: radio telescope dish rotating
Instead, the Parkes telescope began picking up something else. Strange bursts that looked a little like the Lorimer Burst, but not quite. Astronomers called them {{perytons|PERYTONS}}, after a mythical creature that casts the shadow of something else.

> v: radio telescope night | i: radio telescope
Perytons were confusing. They looked like they came from the sky, but they appeared across many of the telescope's receivers at once, as if they were coming from nearby. Some astronomers began to wonder if the Lorimer Burst itself was just a peryton, and if the whole idea of cosmic bursts was a mistake.

> i: observatory building | v: observatory
In 2015, an astronomer named Emily Petroff decided to solve it. She noticed that perytons tended to arrive around lunchtime. She installed a new monitor at the observatory, waited, and caught the culprit red handed.

> i: microwave oven | v: radio telescope
It was the {{microwave oven|MICROWAVE OVEN}} in the staff kitchen. [pause] When someone opened the door before the timer finished, the oven released a brief burst of radio waves, and the giant telescope next door picked it up.

> v: radio telescope dish | i: parkes telescope
It sounds like a joke, but it was a triumph. Perytons had a simple local cause. And because the real bursts looked nothing like the microwave, it meant they were genuine. Something really was flashing in deep space.

## Too Fast To Be Anything We Know
mood: mystery
> v: magnetar | i: neutron star
As more bursts were found, they were given a name: {{Fast Radio Bursts|FRB}}. And the more astronomers studied them, the stranger they became.

> v: neutron star rotating | i: neutron star artist impression
Their speed is a clue. A flash that lasts only a millisecond must come from something small, because a signal cannot switch on and off faster than light can cross the object producing it. That means the source is probably no more than a few hundred kilometres across, smaller than a country, yet blazing across billions of light years.

> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova remnant
Dozens of explanations were proposed. Collapsing neutron stars. Colliding dead stars. Black holes. Exotic particles. Even, in one paper, the leakage from enormous alien transmitters used to push spacecraft across the galaxy. At one point, there were more theories for fast radio bursts than bursts that had actually been found.

> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope array
Then, in 2016, astronomers studying a burst found with the Arecibo telescope in Puerto Rico saw something that ruled out half of those ideas. [pause] It flashed again. And again. It was a {{repeater|IT REPEATS}}.

> i: dwarf galaxy | v: dwarf galaxy zoom
Whatever made it could not have destroyed itself in a single explosion. And because it kept repeating, astronomers could finally track it down. In 2017, they traced it to a small, faint galaxy, about three billion light years away.

## The Culprit Next Door
mood: wonder
> v: radio telescope chime | i: radio telescope array
A new telescope changed everything. In Canada, the CHIME telescope, a set of half-pipe shaped reflectors with no moving parts, began staring at the sky in 2018. It started finding fast radio bursts almost every day, hundreds, then thousands of them.

> v: magnetar flare | i: magnetar artist impression
And in April 2020, it caught one that was close. Not billions of light years away, but inside our own Milky Way. It came from a known object called {{SGR 1935+2154|SGR 1935+2154}}, a magnetar.

> i: magnetar | v: neutron star magnetic field
A magnetar is the collapsed core of a massive star, just twenty kilometres wide, with a magnetic field a thousand trillion times stronger than the Earth's. It is the most powerful magnet known in the universe. As it flared, it released a burst of radio waves that looked like a weaker version of a fast radio burst.

> v: neutron star | i: magnetar
For many astronomers, that settled it. At least some fast radio bursts come from magnetars. [pause] But not all of the puzzle fits.

## The Pieces That Don't Fit
mood: mystery
> v: globular cluster | i: globular cluster
In 2022, a repeating burst was traced to a globular cluster near the galaxy M81, a dense ball of stars billions of years old. Magnetars are supposed to be young, born from recent explosions. Finding one in a cluster of ancient stars is like finding a newborn in a retirement home.

> i: star cluster | v: star cluster zoom
Other bursts repeat on a strict schedule. One, discovered by CHIME, turns on for a few days and then goes silent, in a cycle that repeats every sixteen days, as if its source is orbiting something, or wobbling like a spinning top.

> v: galaxy | i: distant galaxy
And some bursts are so powerful that even a magnetar strains to explain them. Most bursts have never repeated at all, and many astronomers now suspect there may be more than one kind of source hiding under the same name.

> v: cosmic web simulation | i: cosmic web
Ironically, fast radio bursts have already become one of our most useful tools. Because every burst carries a record of all the matter it passed through, astronomers have used them to weigh the thin gas between galaxies, and to find ordinary matter that had been missing from our maps of the universe for decades.

> v: zoom deep field | i: hubble ultra deep field
They also reach astonishingly far. In 2023, astronomers traced one burst to a group of galaxies so distant that its signal had been travelling for about eight billion years. It left its source when the universe was barely more than a third of its current age, long before the Sun and the Earth had formed.

> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope array night
And the hunt is accelerating. New radio telescopes are being built that will find and pinpoint bursts by the thousands, tracing each one back to its home galaxy within seconds. Astronomers hope that with enough of them, the different kinds of bursts will finally separate, and every source will be named.

> v: radio telescope night | i: radio telescope milky way
So tonight, while you sleep, thousands of these flashes will cross the universe, and a handful will reach the Earth. Most were sent before the Earth even existed. [pause] We know where some of them come from. We still do not know what all of them are.
