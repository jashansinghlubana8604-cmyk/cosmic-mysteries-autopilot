Every few days, somewhere close to the Sun, a comet dies. It falls in from the dark outer solar system, accelerating as it goes, aiming almost straight at the Sun. It skims through the Sun's blazing outer atmosphere at hundreds of kilometres per second.

> i: soho comet | v: comet sun
Most of these comets are small, just a few metres to tens of metres across. They evaporate in a matter of hours, vanishing in the fierce heat before they even reach their closest approach. Nobody on Earth sees them.

> i: soho spacecraft | v: spacecraft
But one spacecraft does. Since 1995, a sun-watching observatory called SOHO has photographed more than five thousand of these doomed comets. Most of them were discovered not by professional astronomers, but by volunteers, scanning the images from their homes. [pause] SOHO has become the most prolific comet hunter in history.

> i: sungrazing comet | v: comet tail
They are called {{sungrazers|SUNGRAZERS}}. Kamikaze comets. And some of the brightest comets in human history belonged to their family.

## The Watcher By The Sun
mood: wonder
> i: soho spacecraft | v: spacecraft
SOHO, the Solar and Heliospheric Observatory, is a joint mission of the European Space Agency and NASA. It launched in December 1995 and sits about one and a half million kilometres from Earth, in a spot where it can stare at the Sun without interruption.

> i: solar corona | v: solar corona
One of its instruments, called LASCO, uses a disc to block out the bright face of the Sun, creating an artificial eclipse, so it can photograph the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the corona. It was designed to watch eruptions of solar gas.

> i: soho comet | v: comet sun
But scientists soon noticed something else in the images: small, fuzzy streaks plunging towards the blocked-out Sun. Comets. Lots of them. [pause] SOHO had not been built to find comets. It had accidentally become a comet factory.

> i: soho spacecraft | v: spacecraft
And SOHO nearly did not survive. In June 1998, after a series of errors during routine operations, ground controllers lost contact with it. The spacecraft was tumbling, frozen and silent. Engineers used the giant Arecibo radio telescope to bounce signals off it and find its position, then slowly coaxed it back to life over several months. [pause] It has kept working for decades since.

> i: computer data | v: data visualization
Because SOHO's images are posted publicly online, amateur astronomers around the world began checking them for new comets. In 2024, SOHO reached its five thousandth comet discovery. [pause] Thousands of comets, found by people looking at pictures of the Sun on their computers.

## The Kreutz Family
mood: mystery
> i: comet | v: comet tail
Most of SOHO's comets share almost exactly the same orbit. They come from the same direction and dive past the Sun at nearly the same point. In the late 1800s, the German astronomer Heinrich Kreutz realised that several great comets followed this same path. They became known as the {{Kreutz sungrazers|KREUTZ SUNGRAZERS}}.

> i: comet breakup | v: comet
Astronomers think they are all pieces of one enormous comet that broke apart, probably more than two thousand years ago, as it swept close to the Sun. Each time a fragment returns and grazes the Sun, it can split again, creating smaller and smaller pieces. [pause] The comets SOHO sees dying every few days may be the crumbs of a single giant.

> i: solar corona | v: solar corona
Kreutz comets pass incredibly close to the Sun, often within a solar diameter or less of its surface, right through the hot outer corona. There, sunlight is tens of thousands of times stronger than on Earth, hot enough to vaporise rock. [pause] For a small comet, it is a death sentence.

> i: comet tail | v: comet tail
Some historians have even wondered whether a great comet recorded by ancient Greek writers, including Aristotle, could have been part of that original body. That link is uncertain. But it shows how long this family of comets may have been lighting up our sky.

## The Great Ones
mood: wonder
> i: great comet 1882 | v: comet tail
Some of the Kreutz comets were spectacular. The Great September Comet of 1882 is considered one of the brightest comets ever recorded. It was visible in broad daylight, right next to the Sun. After passing the Sun, its nucleus was seen to break into several pieces, which will return as separate comets centuries from now.

> i: comet tail | v: comet tail
Decades earlier, the Great March Comet of 1843, another Kreutz sungrazer, had grown a tail estimated at hundreds of millions of kilometres long, longer than the distance from the Sun to Mars. For well over a century, it was among the longest comet tails ever measured. [pause] A thread of light stretching across a large part of the inner solar system.

> i: ikeya-seki | v: comet tail
In 1965, two young Japanese amateur astronomers, Kaoru Ikeya and Tsutomu Seki, independently discovered another member. Comet {{Ikeya-Seki|IKEYA-SEKI}} passed only about four hundred and fifty thousand kilometres above the Sun's surface and became so bright that it could be seen in daylight. It, too, broke into pieces.

> i: comet lovejoy | v: comet
In December 2011, comet {{Lovejoy|LOVEJOY}}, discovered by an Australian amateur, did something astonishing. It flew straight through the Sun's corona, where temperatures exceed a million degrees, and came out the other side. Astronomers expected it to be destroyed. Instead, it emerged with a long, glowing tail, before slowly falling apart over the following days.

## The One That Died
mood: dread
> i: comet ison | v: comet sun
In 2013, a comet called {{ISON|ISON}} was heading for an extremely close pass of the Sun on the twenty eighth of November. Some excited predictions called it a possible comet of the century, perhaps bright enough to see in daylight.

> i: soho comet | v: comet sun
Telescopes on Earth and in space watched it approach. SOHO and other spacecraft saw it vanish behind the disc used to block out the Sun. Then, a faint smudge emerged on the other side. For a few hours, people hoped it had survived.

> i: comet | v: comet
It had not. What came out was a cloud of dust and fragments that quickly faded. The heat and the Sun's tides had torn ISON apart. Not a Kreutz comet, but a fresh visitor from the outer solar system, it was simply not tough enough. [pause] The comet of the century became a lesson in how unpredictable comets are.

## Why Comets Fall In
mood: mystery
> i: comet orbit | v: comet orbit
How do comets end up on such suicidal paths? Many begin far out in the Oort Cloud or Kuiper Belt. The gravity of passing stars, the galaxy, or the giant planets can nudge them onto orbits that bring them into the inner solar system.

> i: sun | v: sun
Sungrazers are on orbits that pass so close to the Sun that the heat boils away their ice and the Sun's gravity stretches them. Small ones vanish completely. Larger ones survive a few passes, splitting each time.

> i: sungrazing comet | v: comet tail
Studying them helps scientists understand what comets are made of, and how fragile they are. They also probe the Sun's environment, acting like natural test particles flying through the solar wind and corona. Newer spacecraft, like Solar Orbiter and the Parker Solar Probe, have also caught sungrazers from much closer, adding to SOHO's long watch. [pause] Somewhere out in the dark right now, another fragment of an ancient giant comet is falling towards the Sun. In a few days, or a few centuries, it will burn. And SOHO, or someone, will be watching.
