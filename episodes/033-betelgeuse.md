## A Star Bigger Than Our Orbit
mood: wonder
> i: betelgeuse | v: betelgeuse
Betelgeuse is a {{red supergiant|RED SUPERGIANT}}. It began its life only around ten million years ago, a young, hot, blue star perhaps fifteen to twenty times heavier than our Sun. Heavy stars live fast. Our Sun will shine for ten billion years. Betelgeuse has burned through almost all of its life in a fraction of a percent of that time.

> i: betelgeuse size comparison | v: star size comparison
As its core ran out of hydrogen, the star swelled. Today it is so big that, if you put it where our Sun is, its surface would swallow Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars, and reach out into the asteroid belt. [pause] We would be living inside it.

> i: betelgeuse surface | v: betelgeuse surface
And yet it is not solid in any sense we understand. Its outer layers are so thin they are close to a vacuum. Giant bubbles of hot gas, each one bigger than the orbit of the Earth, rise and fall across its surface over months and years. The star is literally boiling.

> i: orion constellation | v: night sky
It sits roughly five hundred and fifty to seven hundred light-years away. That distance is surprisingly hard to pin down, because a star this big and this blotchy does not have a sharp edge to measure. [pause] Which means the light you see tonight left Betelgeuse long before anyone alive today was born.

## The Great Dimming
mood: mystery
> i: betelgeuse dimming | v: betelgeuse
Betelgeuse has always flickered a little. But at the end of 2019, it did something nobody had seen in more than a century of careful records. It dimmed, and kept dimming. By February 2020, it had faded to well under half of its normal brightness.

> i: orion constellation | v: night sky
People who had looked up at Orion their whole lives noticed that his shoulder was missing. Professional astronomers pointed their biggest telescopes at it. Social media filled with countdowns. {{The Great Dimming|THE GREAT DIMMING}} had begun.

> i: betelgeuse dimming hubble | v: betelgeuse
Then, as quietly as it had started, it stopped. By April, Betelgeuse was back to its normal brightness. No explosion. No collapse. So what had happened?

> i: betelgeuse dust | v: betelgeuse surface
Hubble had caught the answer in the months before. A huge plume of hot gas had burst from the star's southern side, carrying away material equal to several times the mass of our Moon. As that gas drifted outward and cooled, it condensed into a dark cloud of dust. [pause] The star had not dimmed. It had coughed out a veil and hidden behind it.

> i: betelgeuse surface | v: betelgeuse
Astronomers call it a surface mass ejection, and it was hundreds of billions of times bigger than the eruptions we see on our own Sun. It took years for the star to settle down afterwards. [pause] It was not the end. But it was a reminder that this is a star in its final chapter.

## What Happens Inside
mood: dread
> i: star interior | v: star core
The real drama is happening where we cannot see it. At the heart of Betelgeuse, the star has already burned its hydrogen into helium. It is now burning helium into carbon and oxygen. Once that runs out, it will go on to heavier and heavier elements, each stage faster than the last.

> i: star layers | v: star interior
Carbon may last a few hundred years. Neon, around a year. Oxygen, months. And the final stage, turning silicon into iron, might take only {{a single day|ONE DAY}}. The core will be layered like an onion, with a ball of iron at the very centre.

> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova
And iron is where the story ends. Fusing iron does not release energy. It costs energy. So when the iron core grows heavy enough, nothing can hold it up. In less than a second, a core about the size of the Earth collapses to the size of a city.

> v: neutron star collapse | i: neutron star
The collapse bounces, and a shock wave tears outward through the star. Within hours it reaches the surface, and Betelgeuse will be blown apart in a {{supernova|SUPERNOVA}}. [pause] What is left of the core will probably become a neutron star, a ball of matter so dense that a teaspoon would weigh a billion tonnes.

## A Second Sun In The Sky
mood: wonder
> i: neutrino detector | v: neutrino detector
The first warning will not be light at all. It will be neutrinos, ghostly particles that pour out of the collapsing core and pass straight through the star, and straight through the Earth. Detectors buried deep underground, in Japan, in Antarctica and elsewhere, will register a sudden surge. [pause] For a few hours, we will know before we can see.

> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova sky
Then the light arrives. Scientists estimate that at its peak, the supernova could shine about as brightly as a half Moon. A blazing point of light, so bright it would cast shadows at night. And during the day, it would be clearly visible in a blue sky.

> i: orion constellation | v: night sky
For weeks, it would be the third brightest thing in the heavens. Then, over months, it would slowly fade, from white to orange to red, until after a year or two it disappeared to the naked eye. [pause] And when it did, Orion would have lost his shoulder forever.

> i: supernova remnant | v: supernova remnant
For thousands of years after that, telescopes would watch a glowing shell of gas expand where the star used to be. Inside it, carbon, oxygen, calcium and iron, forged in the star's heart, would scatter into space. [pause] Some of it, one day, may end up in new stars, new planets, and perhaps new life.

## When
mood: mystery
> i: betelgeuse | v: betelgeuse
So when? This is the part that disappoints most people. The best estimates from studying how Betelgeuse pulses suggest it is still burning helium in its core. That would give it around {{100,000 years|100,000 YEARS}} left. Perhaps less. Perhaps more.

> i: betelgeuse surface | v: star field
Some studies have argued it may be further along than that, and could go within a few centuries. Most astronomers are cautious. The truth is that nobody has ever watched a star like this right before the end. [pause] It could be tonight. It is far more likely to be long after all of us are gone.

> i: earth from space | v: earth
And is it dangerous? No. A supernova needs to be within roughly fifty light-years to seriously harm life on Earth. Betelgeuse is more than ten times further than that. We will get a show, not a catastrophe.

> i: orion constellation | v: milky way time-lapse
There is one more strange thought. Because its light takes centuries to reach us, Betelgeuse might already have exploded. The flash could be on its way right now, crossing the dark, about to arrive. [pause] So the next time you see Orion, look at his shoulder. You might be one of the last people to see that star at all.
