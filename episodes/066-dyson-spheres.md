Every second, the Sun pours out an almost unimaginable amount of energy. In one second, more than all of humanity has used in its entire history. And almost all of it is wasted, streaming off into empty space in every direction.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
The Earth catches less than one billionth of it. The rest just leaves. Gone forever, lighting up nothing.

> i: dyson swarm | v: dyson sphere
Now imagine a civilisation far more advanced than ours, one that needs more power than its planet can provide. Sooner or later, it might do the obvious thing. Surround its star with gigantic collectors, and catch it all. [pause] A structure the size of a solar system.

> i: dyson sphere | v: dyson sphere
It is called a Dyson sphere. And astronomers are actually searching for them. In 2024, a team scanning millions of stars announced seven candidates that glow in a way they could not easily explain. [pause] So what did they find?

## A Physicist's Thought Experiment
mood: wonder
> i: freeman dyson | v: physics laboratory
In 1960, the British-American physicist {{Freeman Dyson|FREEMAN DYSON}} published a short paper in the journal Science. He asked a simple question. If an alien civilisation kept growing, using more and more energy, what would it eventually do?

> i: dyson swarm | v: dyson sphere
His answer: it would harvest the light of its own star. Not with a single solid shell, which would be unstable and could be torn apart, but with a vast swarm of orbiting collectors, mirrors and habitats, so many that they almost completely surround the star. [pause] Today, we call this a {{Dyson swarm|DYSON SWARM}}.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Dyson was partly inspired by a science fiction novel from 1937, Star Maker by Olaf Stapledon, which imagined civilisations wrapping their stars in light traps. But Dyson's twist was practical. He was not interested in whether we could build one. He was interested in how we might spot one.

> i: infrared galaxy | v: infrared telescope
And he realised something crucial. A Dyson swarm cannot hide its heat. The collectors absorb starlight, use it, and must then get rid of the waste energy as warmth. That warmth glows in infrared light. [pause] So a star wrapped in a swarm would look dim in visible light, but strangely bright in infrared. Like a glowing ember in the dark.

## Taking A Planet Apart
mood: dread
> i: mercury | v: mercury
How would you build such a thing? You would need an astonishing amount of material. Some have calculated that you could make a thin swarm around the Sun by dismantling an entire planet. {{Mercury|MERCURY}}, for example.

> i: dyson swarm | v: spacecraft
Self-replicating robots could mine the planet, build solar collectors, launch them into orbit, and use the energy from those collectors to build even more robots. The swarm would grow exponentially. Some estimates suggest that, once it started, it might take only decades or centuries to finish.

> i: dyson sphere | v: dyson sphere
The payoff would be staggering. A full swarm around a star like the Sun would capture around ten trillion times the power humanity uses today. [pause] Enough energy to do almost anything we can imagine, and many things we cannot.

> i: ringworld | v: space habitat
Science fiction has imagined even stranger versions. In Larry Niven's novel Ringworld, a single colossal ring circles a star, with a habitable surface millions of times larger than the Earth. Engineers point out that such a ring would be unstable and could drift into its sun. [pause] Which is exactly why a loose swarm of separate pieces is the version scientists take seriously.

> i: kardashev scale | v: galaxy
In 1964, the Soviet astronomer Nikolai Kardashev proposed ranking civilisations by their energy use. A Type One uses all the energy available on its planet. A Type Two harnesses its entire star, with something like a Dyson swarm. A Type Three commands the power of a whole galaxy. [pause] By that scale, we are not even a Type One yet.

## The Search
mood: mystery
> i: infrared telescope | v: telescope dome
Since the 1980s, astronomers have searched infrared sky surveys for stars with too much heat. Most of the time, the explanation is ordinary. Young stars surrounded by dust. Old stars puffing out smoky shells. Distant galaxies hiding behind a star.

> i: wise telescope | v: satellite orbit
In 2015, one team used NASA's WISE space telescope to search not individual stars, but around a hundred thousand entire galaxies, looking for any that glowed too brightly in infrared, as if a galaxy-spanning civilisation had captured its starlight. They found no clear sign of one. [pause] If Type Three civilisations exist, they are rare.

> i: tabby's star | v: star
Then there was Tabby's star, a star whose light dipped in bizarre, irregular ways, sometimes by more than a fifth. For a while, the idea of an alien megastructure made headlines around the world. But careful observations showed the dimming depended on colour in a way that pointed to dust, not solid panels. [pause] The mystery shrank. The search did not stop.

## Seven Candidates
mood: mystery
> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
In 2024, a team in Sweden led by astronomer Matías Suazo ran a project called {{Project Hephaistos|PROJECT HEPHAISTOS}}, named after the Greek god of blacksmiths and craftsmen. They combined data from three surveys, including the Gaia mission, to examine around five million stars.

> i: infrared galaxy | v: infrared telescope
They looked for stars that were slightly too faint in visible light and too bright in mid-infrared, the pattern a partial Dyson swarm might make. After filtering out every source they could explain, they were left with {{seven candidates|7 CANDIDATES}}. All of them small red dwarf stars, all within about nine hundred light-years.

> i: red dwarf star | v: star field
The team was careful to say they had not found aliens. Only stars with an infrared glow they could not easily explain. And soon, other astronomers offered a natural answer. In some of the images, distant galaxies hidden right behind the stars, glowing brightly in infrared, could produce exactly that effect. [pause] For at least some of the seven, a background galaxy is the likely culprit.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
The next step is to look closer, with telescopes that can tell a star's own glow apart from anything behind it. That is how science should work. Find something strange. Rule out the ordinary. [pause] And only then, if nothing else fits, consider the extraordinary.

## What If We Found One?
mood: wonder
> i: dyson sphere | v: dyson sphere
Imagine that one day, a star really did show every sign of being wrapped in machinery. The waste heat. The dimming. Perhaps a pattern of eclipses no cloud of dust could produce. It would be the most important discovery in human history.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
It would not just tell us that we are not alone. It would tell us that civilisations can survive long enough, and grow powerful enough, to rebuild their solar systems. [pause] That the future can be enormous.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
And if we never find one? That tells us something too. Perhaps advanced civilisations do not grow forever. Perhaps they become more efficient instead of bigger. Or perhaps there are very few of them out there.

> i: white dwarf | v: black hole accretion disk
Some researchers suggest we may be looking in the wrong places. A truly advanced civilisation might prefer to build around a long-lived white dwarf, or even feed on the energy of a black hole, where the efficiency could be far higher. Searches have started to consider those too. [pause] The universe may be hiding its engineers where we least expect them.

> i: sun solar dynamics observatory | v: sun
Meanwhile, our own Sun keeps pouring trillions of times our needs into the dark, untouched. [pause] Somewhere in the galaxy, perhaps, someone else stopped wasting theirs. And the infrared glow of their work is still crossing the stars, waiting for someone to notice it.
