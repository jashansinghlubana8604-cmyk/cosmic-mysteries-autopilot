## Born In A Second
mood: dread
> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova
Every neutron star begins with a death. A star at least eight times heavier than the Sun spends millions of years fusing lighter elements into heavier ones in its core. Hydrogen into helium, helium into carbon, and on and on, until it reaches iron.

> i: star interior | v: star core
Iron is the end of the line. Fusing it does not produce energy, so the core stops making heat. And without heat pushing outward, nothing can hold up the crushing weight of the star above.

> v: neutron star collapse | i: neutron star
The core collapses in less than a second. It falls inward at up to a quarter of the speed of light. Atoms are crushed. Electrons are forced into the nuclei, where they merge with protons and turn into neutrons. A ball the size of the Earth becomes a ball about {{twenty kilometres|20 KM}} across.

> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova remnant
The collapse stops suddenly, when the neutrons are packed so tightly that they refuse to be squeezed any more. The falling outer layers slam into this new, rigid core and rebound. A shock wave tears the rest of the star apart in a supernova.

> i: crab nebula | v: crab nebula
In 1054, Chinese astronomers recorded a new star so bright it was visible in daylight for weeks. Today, at that spot, we see the Crab Nebula, the cloud from that explosion. And right at its heart, a neutron star, spinning thirty times every second. [pause] A city-sized corpse, still glowing almost a thousand years after the star died.

## Gravity You Cannot Imagine
mood: dread
> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
The gravity at the surface of a neutron star is around a hundred billion times stronger than on Earth. That number is so big it means almost nothing, so let's make it real.

> v: falling object | i: neutron star artist impression
If you dropped a single marshmallow onto a neutron star from far away, it would hit the surface at about half the speed of light. It would release as much energy as a nuclear weapon. [pause] A sweet you could hold in your hand, turned into a city-destroying blast, by gravity alone.

> i: neutron star surface | v: neutron star
This gravity also flattens everything. The tallest mountains on a neutron star are thought to be less than a few millimetres high. Its surface would be smoother than any mirror ever made on Earth.

> i: gravitational lensing | v: light bending
And it bends light. Light leaving the far side of the star is curved around it so strongly that, looking at a neutron star, you could see more than half of its surface at once. [pause] You would see part of its back, wrapped around its edge, as if the star were turning itself inside out to show you.

> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
To escape from its surface, you would need to travel at around a third to a half of the speed of light. Anything slower simply falls back. A neutron star is not quite a black hole. [pause] But it is the last stop before one.

## The Strongest Material In The Universe
mood: mystery
> i: neutron star interior | v: neutron star interior
What is a neutron star actually made of? On the outside, there is a thin atmosphere, maybe just centimetres thick. Below it, a solid crust of iron nuclei, arranged in a crystal lattice, swimming in a sea of electrons.

> i: neutron star crust | v: neutron star interior
Go deeper, and the nuclei are squeezed closer and closer, until they start to merge. And here, physicists think, matter forms shapes. Long strands like spaghetti. Flat sheets like lasagne. Tubes and bubbles. They call it {{nuclear pasta|NUCLEAR PASTA}}.

> i: neutron star interior | v: neutron star
In 2018, computer simulations suggested that nuclear pasta could be the strongest material in the universe, perhaps ten billion times stronger than steel. Nothing we will ever make could come close.

> i: neutron star core | v: neutron star interior
Below the pasta lies the core, mostly neutrons, flowing as a superfluid, a liquid with no friction at all. And at the very centre? Nobody knows. The pressure may be so great that neutrons themselves break down into their smallest parts, quarks, loose in a soup that exists nowhere else.

> i: nicer telescope | v: international space station
Since 2017, an instrument on the International Space Station called {{NICER|NICER}} has been measuring neutron stars more precisely than ever, timing the X-rays from hot spots on their surfaces as they spin. Its results suggest a typical neutron star is around twenty four kilometres across. [pause] Every measurement narrows down what kind of matter could possibly be inside.

## The Spinning Lighthouses
mood: wonder
> i: pulsar | v: pulsar
When a star collapses, it spins faster, the way an ice skater speeds up when they pull their arms in. So most young neutron stars spin incredibly fast. And they have powerful magnetic fields that funnel beams of radiation out of their magnetic poles.

> v: pulsar beam | i: pulsar
If those beams happen to sweep past the Earth, we see a flash, again and again, like a lighthouse. These are {{pulsars|PULSARS}}. The first was discovered in 1967 by a young astronomer named Jocelyn Bell, who noticed a tiny, regular flicker in her radio data.

> i: pulsar | v: neutron star
Some pulsars spin a few times a second. Others are terrifyingly fast. The fastest known spins about {{716 times a second|716 / SECOND}}. A ball of matter heavier than the Sun, rotating so fast that its equator moves at around a quarter of the speed of light.

> i: vela pulsar | v: pulsar
And sometimes, a pulsar suddenly jumps. Its spin speeds up in an instant, a tiny glitch. Scientists think this happens when the superfluid inside catches on the crust and transfers its spin all at once. [pause] We can feel the inside of a star we will never touch, just by timing its heartbeat.

## The Black Widow
mood: dread
> i: black widow pulsar | v: pulsar
Some neutron stars are not alone. They orbit a companion star, and slowly eat it. The neutron star pulls gas off its partner, spins faster and faster, and blasts it with radiation until the companion is boiling away.

> i: pulsar companion | v: binary star
Astronomers call these {{black widow pulsars|BLACK WIDOW}}. In 2022, they announced that one of them, PSR J0952-0607, is the heaviest neutron star ever measured, at about two point three five times the mass of the Sun. It has grown fat by devouring its companion.

> v: neutron star collapse | i: black hole
And that matters. There is a limit to how heavy a neutron star can be. Somewhere around two and a half times the mass of the Sun, the neutrons can no longer hold up their own weight. Push beyond it, and the star collapses again, this time into a black hole.

> i: kilonova | v: neutron star merger
When two neutron stars spiral together and collide, they throw out vast amounts of gold, platinum and other heavy elements. Much of the gold on Earth may have been made that way. [pause] The ring on someone's finger may once have been part of a dead star.

> i: neutron star | v: star field
So next time you look up at the night sky, remember. Out there, scattered through the galaxy, are hundreds of millions of these silent spheres. Each one the size of a city. Each one heavier than the Sun. [pause] The ghosts of the giant stars that came before, still spinning in the dark.
