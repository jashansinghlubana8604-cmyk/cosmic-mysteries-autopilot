According to our best theories, the Big Bang should have created matter and antimatter in exactly equal amounts. For every particle, an opposite twin, with the same mass but the opposite charge.

> i: particle collision | v: particle collision
And when matter and antimatter meet, they destroy each other completely, in a flash of pure energy. So if the early universe had equal amounts of both, they should have wiped each other out. Totally.

> i: early universe | v: early universe
There should be no stars. No galaxies. No planets. No people. Just a universe filled with faint light, the leftover glow of a perfect annihilation. [pause] And yet, here we are.

> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
Somehow, a tiny bit of matter survived. About one particle for every billion that were destroyed. Everything you have ever seen is made from that tiny leftover. [pause] Why? It is one of the deepest unsolved mysteries in all of physics.

## The Mirror World
mood: wonder
> i: paul dirac | v: physics laboratory
In 1928, the British physicist {{Paul Dirac|PAUL DIRAC}} wrote down an equation describing electrons moving close to the speed of light, combining quantum physics with Einstein's relativity. It worked beautifully. But it had a strange feature.

> i: particle collision | v: particle collision
The equation allowed for two kinds of solutions. One described the familiar electron. The other seemed to describe a particle identical to the electron in every way, except that its charge was positive. Dirac eventually concluded that it must be real: a new kind of particle. [pause] An anti-electron.

> i: cloud chamber | v: particle collision
In 1932, the American physicist Carl Anderson was photographing cosmic rays passing through a cloud chamber, a device that makes the paths of particles visible as trails of droplets. He saw a particle curving the wrong way in a magnetic field. A positive electron. He called it the {{positron|POSITRON}}. [pause] Antimatter was real.

> i: particle accelerator | v: particle accelerator
Since then, physicists have found that every type of particle has an antimatter twin. Antiprotons. Antineutrons. Even whole antiatoms. At CERN, near Geneva, scientists make antihydrogen atoms and trap them in magnetic fields to study them. [pause] As far as we can tell, they behave almost exactly like normal hydrogen.

## Total Annihilation
mood: dread
> i: particle collision | v: particle collision
When a particle meets its antiparticle, both vanish, and their mass is turned completely into energy, according to Einstein's famous equation, E equals m c squared. It is the most efficient energy release possible. A hundred percent.

> i: nuclear explosion | v: explosion
To put that in perspective, a single gram of antimatter meeting a gram of matter would release energy comparable to a large nuclear bomb, many tens of kilotons. [pause] Science fiction loves antimatter engines for exactly this reason.

> i: particle accelerator | v: particle accelerator
But making antimatter is absurdly hard. All the antimatter ever made in particle accelerators adds up to only billionths of a gram. Estimates of the cost of producing a single gram run into the trillions of dollars, and it would take far longer than a human lifetime with current technology. [pause] Antimatter is the most expensive substance on Earth.

> i: hospital scanner | v: medical scanner
Antimatter is not only a cosmic mystery. It is used every day in hospitals. PET scans work by injecting a substance that releases positrons. When they meet electrons in the body, they annihilate, producing pairs of gamma rays that a scanner detects, revealing how organs are working. [pause] Every PET scan is a tiny matter-antimatter explosion inside a human being.

> i: banana | v: food
Even a banana produces a little antimatter. It contains a slightly radioactive form of potassium which, very occasionally, emits a positron. The amount is tiny and completely harmless. [pause] But it means antimatter is not exotic. It is all around us, constantly being made and destroyed.

## The Mystery Of The Leftover
mood: mystery
> i: early universe | v: early universe
So back to the beginning. In the first instants after the Big Bang, the universe was so hot that particles and antiparticles were constantly being created from energy and annihilating back into energy.

> i: big bang | v: big bang
As the universe expanded and cooled, that creation stopped, and the annihilation won. Almost all the matter and antimatter destroyed each other. But for some reason, there was a tiny excess of matter. For roughly every billion antiparticles, there were a billion and one particles. [pause] The leftover one in a billion became everything.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
We can measure this ratio. The cosmic microwave background, the faint glow left over from the early universe, contains the energy of all that annihilation. Comparing the number of its photons with the amount of matter in the universe gives the answer: about {{a billion to one|1,000,000,000 : 1}}.

> i: andrei sakharov | v: physics laboratory
In 1967, the Soviet physicist Andrei Sakharov worked out the conditions needed for a universe to end up with more matter than antimatter. One of them is that the laws of physics must treat matter and antimatter slightly differently. [pause] They must not be perfect mirror images. The universe must also have passed through a period when it was out of balance, changing too fast for things to even out, and some process must have been able to change the total number of particles. All three conditions had to be met in the first instants of time.

## A Tiny Difference
mood: mystery
> i: particle accelerator | v: particle accelerator
Physicists have found such differences. In 1964, an experiment showed that certain particles, called kaons, decay in a slightly different way from their antiparticles. Later experiments found similar differences in other particles. The effect is called {{CP violation|CP VIOLATION}}.

> i: particle collision | v: particle collision
But here is the problem. The differences we have measured are far too small. They can explain only a minuscule fraction of the matter we see. Something else must have tipped the balance in the early universe. [pause] Some new physics, not yet discovered.

> i: neutrino detector | v: neutrino detector
One leading suspect is the neutrino, a ghostly particle that barely interacts with anything. If neutrinos and antineutrinos behave differently enough, they might explain the imbalance. Huge experiments in Japan and the United States are being built to measure exactly that.

> i: physics laboratory | v: physics laboratory
At CERN, scientists also test whether antimatter falls the same way as matter under gravity. In 2023, the ALPHA experiment showed that antihydrogen falls downward, just like normal matter. [pause] No antigravity. The mystery has to lie somewhere else.

## Why We Are Here
mood: wonder
> i: galaxy | v: galaxy zoom
Could there be regions of the universe made entirely of antimatter, antigalaxies with antistars? Astronomers have looked. If antimatter galaxies existed near normal ones, the boundaries between them would glow with gamma rays from annihilation. No such glow has been found.

> i: international space station | v: international space station
On the International Space Station, an instrument called the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer has spent years sorting through cosmic rays, searching for anti-helium nuclei, which would be a sign of antimatter stars somewhere out there. A handful of possible candidates have been reported, but none has been confirmed. [pause] If one ever is, it would turn physics upside down.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
So the universe really does seem to be made of matter. A cosmic accident, a tiny imbalance in the laws of physics, left behind just enough to build every star, every planet and every living thing.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
Think about that. You are made of the survivors of the greatest destruction in history. A billion particles were annihilated for each one that lived. [pause] Every atom in your body is a leftover from a battle at the beginning of time. And we still do not know why matter won.
