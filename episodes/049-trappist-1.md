## A Tiny Star With A Big Family
mood: wonder
> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
{{TRAPPIST-1|TRAPPIST-1}} is an ultracool red dwarf. It has less than a tenth of the Sun's mass, and its surface is only around two and a half thousand degrees, less than half as hot as the Sun's. It glows a deep, dull red, mostly in infrared light our eyes cannot see.

> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
In 2016, a team led by Belgian astronomer Michaël Gillon used a small robotic telescope in Chile, called TRAPPIST, to watch the star for tiny dips in brightness. Each dip meant a planet was passing in front of it. They found three.

> i: spitzer telescope | v: spitzer
Then NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope stared at the star for almost three weeks without a break. When the results were announced in February 2017, there were not three planets. There were {{seven|SEVEN}}. [pause] The largest number of Earth-sized planets ever found around a single star.

> i: trappist-1 planets | v: trappist-1 planets
They were named with the letters b to h. All seven orbit their star in less than three weeks. The closest takes just one and a half days. The whole system would fit comfortably inside the orbit of Mercury. [pause] A full solar system, squeezed into a space smaller than our closest planet's path around the Sun.

## Worlds In The Sky
mood: wonder
> i: trappist-1 surface | v: exoplanet surface
Imagine standing on one of them. Overhead hangs a huge, dim red sun, appearing several times wider than our Sun does from Earth. The light is warm and orange, like a permanent sunset.

> i: trappist-1 artist impression | v: exoplanet
And in the sky, there are other worlds. Because the planets are packed so tightly together, at their closest some of them would look bigger than the full Moon does from Earth. You could see their clouds, their continents, if they have any, their day and night sides. [pause] Your neighbouring planets would not be dots. They would be landscapes.

> i: trappist-1 orbits | v: trappist-1 orbits
The planets are also locked together in an astonishing rhythm. For every eight orbits the innermost planet makes, the next one makes five, the next three, and so on, in a chain of simple ratios. If you turned their orbits into musical notes, they would form a harmony.

> i: trappist-1 planets | v: exoplanet
That resonance is a clue. It suggests the planets formed further out and drifted inward together, gently, without colliding. [pause] Their orbits are a fossil record of how this system was born.

> i: trappist-1 planets | v: exoplanet
Because the planets tug on each other, their timing shifts by a few minutes from orbit to orbit. By measuring those shifts, astronomers weighed all seven worlds. They turned out to be remarkably similar to each other, rocky, and slightly less dense than the Earth. [pause] Perhaps they have a little less iron. Or perhaps they hold a lot of water, locked in their rocks or spread across their surfaces.

## The Habitable Three
mood: mystery
> i: habitable zone | v: habitable zone
Three of the seven, planets {{e, f and g|e · f · g}}, orbit in what astronomers call the habitable zone. That is the range of distances where a rocky planet with the right atmosphere could keep liquid water on its surface.

> i: trappist-1e | v: exoplanet
TRAPPIST-1e is especially interesting. It is a little smaller than the Earth, and it receives roughly half the light we get from the Sun. If it has an atmosphere and water, it could be one of the most Earth-like places we know.

> i: tidally locked planet | v: exoplanet rotation
But these are not Earth twins. Planets this close to their star are almost certainly tidally locked, with one side always facing the star and the other in permanent night. Life, if any, might cluster along the twilight line between day and night, where the sun hangs forever on the horizon.

> i: red dwarf flare | v: red dwarf flare
And there is a bigger problem. Red dwarfs, especially young ones, are violent. They erupt with powerful flares, blasting their planets with X-rays and ultraviolet radiation. [pause] Those flares could have stripped away the planets' air long ago. The only way to know was to look.

## What Webb Saw
mood: mystery
> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
Webb began observing TRAPPIST-1 soon after it started work. First, it measured the heat coming from the innermost planet, {{TRAPPIST-1b|TRAPPIST-1 b}}, as it passed behind its star. The day side was around two hundred and thirty degrees, with no sign of a thick atmosphere carrying heat around the planet.

> i: trappist-1 | v: exoplanet
Then planet c. Again, no sign of a thick, carbon dioxide atmosphere like Venus. A later study of planet d found no evidence of an Earth-like atmosphere either. One by one, the inner worlds looked bare, or close to it.

> i: trappist-1e | v: exoplanet atmosphere
Webb has also been studying planet e, the most promising of all, watching it pass in front of its star again and again. The early results ruled out a thick hydrogen atmosphere like the one it may have been born with. But they could not yet tell whether it has a thinner atmosphere like the Earth's. [pause] More observations are being collected. The answer is still open.

> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
It is hard work. The star itself is covered in spots and bright patches that change the light passing through any atmosphere, and can mimic or hide its signals. Separating the star's fingerprints from the planet's is one of the toughest problems Webb faces.

## A Star That Will Outlive Everything
mood: wonder
> i: trappist-1 | v: trappist-1
Even if TRAPPIST-1's planets are barren today, there is something extraordinary about this system. Small red dwarfs burn their fuel incredibly slowly. Our Sun will run out in about five billion years. TRAPPIST-1 will keep shining for {{trillions of years|TRILLIONS OF YEARS}}.

> i: red dwarf star | v: red dwarf
It is already old. Estimates suggest TRAPPIST-1 is around seven to eight billion years old, likely billions of years older than our solar system. [pause] If life ever started on one of its planets, it has had far longer to evolve than life on Earth.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
When our Sun is long dead, when the Milky Way has merged with Andromeda, when most of the bright stars in our sky have burned out, TRAPPIST-1 will still be glowing, steady and red. Its seven planets will still be circling it in their musical rhythm.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
And there are countless systems like it. Red dwarfs make up about three quarters of all the stars in the galaxy. If even a small fraction of their planets can hold onto air and water, then the most common kind of habitable world in the universe would look nothing like the Earth. [pause] It would be a planet like these, under a dim red sun, with other worlds in its sky.

> i: trappist-1 planets | v: trappist-1 planets
Forty light-years. Seven worlds. Close enough that Webb can taste their skies, one starlit sliver at a time. [pause] Somewhere in that light, the answer to one of our oldest questions may already be waiting.
