## A World That Could Float
mood: wonder
> v: saturn | i: saturn
Saturn is the second largest planet in the solar system. Seven hundred and sixty Earths could fit inside it. Yet it is made mostly of hydrogen and helium, so light that its average density is less than that of water.

> i: saturn | v: saturn rotation
If you could find a bathtub big enough, Saturn would float. Of course, there is no surface to stand on. Descend through its clouds, and the gas just becomes thicker, hotter and denser, until it behaves like a strange metallic liquid, deep inside.

> v: saturn storm | i: saturn storm
It spins fast, once every ten and a half hours, and that rotation drives ferocious winds, up to around eighteen hundred kilometres per hour near the equator. Every few decades, an enormous white storm erupts and wraps around the entire planet. The last great one began in 2010, and its lightning was thousands of times more powerful than lightning on Earth.

> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rotation
Even the length of Saturn's day was a mystery for decades. With no solid surface to track, astronomers could not tell how fast the planet really turned. The answer finally came from the rings themselves. Vibrations inside Saturn create tiny ripples in the ring particles, and by studying those ripples, scientists worked out that a day on Saturn lasts about ten hours and thirty three minutes.

## The Hexagon
mood: mystery
> i: saturn hexagon | v: saturn hexagon
In 1981, the Voyager spacecraft photographed something no one could explain. Around Saturn's north pole, the clouds formed a near perfect hexagon.

> v: saturn north pole | i: saturn north pole
Decades later, the Cassini spacecraft saw it in astonishing detail. The {{hexagon|THE HEXAGON}} is about thirty thousand kilometres across. Each of its six sides is longer than the diameter of the Earth. At its centre spins a hurricane, with an eye about fifty times larger than a typical hurricane eye on Earth.

> i: saturn hexagon colour | v: saturn pole
Straight lines are rare in nature, especially at this scale. Scientists now think the hexagon is a kind of standing wave, formed by a fast-flowing jet stream that meanders in a stable six-sided pattern. Experiments with spinning tanks of liquid have produced similar shapes. But why Saturn's is so stable, lasting at least decades, is still not fully understood.

> v: saturn hexagon | i: saturn hexagon
Over the years Cassini watched, the hexagon even changed colour, from blue to golden, as the seasons changed and sunlight began to fall on the northern pole.

## The Vanishing Rings
mood: mystery
> v: saturn rings | i: saturn rings
Saturn's rings stretch around two hundred and eighty thousand kilometres from edge to edge. Yet in most places, they are only around ten metres thick. If you made a scale model the width of a football pitch, the rings would be thinner than a sheet of paper.

> i: saturn rings close up | v: saturn rings close up
They are made of countless chunks of water ice, from grains of dust to boulders as large as houses. And they are bright and clean, as if they were made recently. Many scientists now think the rings may be only a few hundred million years old, much younger than Saturn itself.

> v: cassini grand finale | i: cassini saturn
At the end of its mission, Cassini made a series of daring dives through the gap between the rings and the planet. It found that ring material is falling into Saturn constantly, pulled down by gravity and the planet's magnetic field. Scientists call it {{ring rain|RING RAIN}}.

> i: saturn rings | v: saturn
At the current rate, the rings could largely disappear within a few hundred million years. We happen to live at the right moment in cosmic history to see them at their best. And in March 2025, from Earth's point of view, the rings briefly seemed to vanish altogether, as we saw them perfectly edge on, a thin bright line across the planet.

> v: saturn rings shepherd moons | i: saturn rings detail
The rings are also alive with structure. Small moons orbit inside the gaps, carving sharp edges and stirring up waves. Some rings are braided. Some have vertical walls of particles kilometres high, rising above the flat plane like mountain ranges. Cassini even watched what may be a tiny new moon being born at the outer edge of the main rings.

## Death Of A Spacecraft
mood: wonder
> v: cassini spacecraft | i: cassini spacecraft
Cassini arrived at Saturn in 2004 and explored it for thirteen years. It carried a probe called Huygens, which parachuted down to the surface of Saturn's largest moon, Titan, the most distant landing ever made.

> i: cassini grand finale | v: cassini grand finale
By 2017, Cassini was running out of fuel. Its controllers had a problem. If the spacecraft were left drifting, it could one day crash into one of Saturn's moons and contaminate a world that might harbour life. So they chose to destroy it.

> v: cassini saturn plunge | i: saturn
On the 15th of September 2017, Cassini plunged into Saturn's atmosphere, sending data until the very last moment, before burning up like a meteor. Its final act was to protect the moons it had discovered might be habitable.

## The Moons
mood: wonder
> i: enceladus plumes | v: enceladus geysers
Saturn has the most known moons of any planet, more than two hundred and seventy. Some are among the strangest worlds in the solar system.

> v: enceladus | i: enceladus
Enceladus, small and icy, sprays fountains of water into space from cracks near its south pole, from an ocean beneath its ice. Cassini flew through those plumes and found salt, silica and organic molecules. It is one of the most promising places to look for life beyond Earth.

> i: titan | v: titan
Titan has a thick orange atmosphere, rain, rivers and seas, but they are made of liquid methane and ethane, at nearly minus one hundred and eighty degrees. NASA's Dragonfly mission is planned to send a nuclear-powered drone helicopter to fly across its surface.

> v: iapetus | i: iapetus
And Iapetus is two-faced, one side as dark as coal, the other bright as snow, with a mountain ridge running around its equator like the seam of a walnut.

> i: mimas | v: mimas
Even Mimas, a small moon with a giant crater that makes it look like the Death Star from Star Wars, may be hiding a surprise. In 2024, scientists studying tiny wobbles in its orbit concluded that it probably has a young ocean beneath its icy shell, one that may have formed only in the last few tens of millions of years.

> i: saturn | v: saturn
From Earth, Saturn looks calm and elegant. [pause] Up close, it is a world of colossal storms, impossible shapes, vanishing rings, and moons that may hide the ingredients for life.
