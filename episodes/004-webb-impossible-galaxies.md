## A Time Machine Made Of Gold
mood: wonder
> v: webb telescope unfolding | i: james webb space telescope
On Christmas Day 2021, the James Webb Space Telescope launched from French Guiana. It carried a mirror six and a half metres wide, made of eighteen gold-coated segments, folded up like origami to fit inside the rocket.

> i: webb sunshield | v: webb deployment
It travelled one and a half million kilometres from Earth, unfolded a sunshield the size of a tennis court, and cooled itself to around minus two hundred and thirty degrees. Every step had to work perfectly, far beyond the reach of any repair mission.

> v: zoom deep field | i: deep field galaxies
Webb is built to see infrared light. That matters, because the universe is expanding. Light from the most distant galaxies has been travelling for more than thirteen billion years, and as space stretched, that light was stretched too, from visible colours into the infrared. To see the first galaxies, you need infrared eyes.

> i: webb deep field | v: webb deep field pan
Every time Webb looks at a distant galaxy, it is looking back in time. A galaxy thirteen billion light years away is seen as it was thirteen billion years ago. Webb is, in a very real sense, a {{time machine|TIME MACHINE}}.

> i: webb first deep field smacs 0723 | v: webb first deep field
In July 2022, the world saw its first full image. It showed a patch of sky so small that you could cover it with a grain of sand held at arm's length. In that tiny patch were thousands of galaxies, some of them seen as they were more than thirteen billion years ago, their light bent and magnified by a massive cluster of galaxies in front of them. It was the deepest picture of the universe ever taken. [pause] And it was only a test.

## Too Big, Too Soon
mood: mystery
> v: early universe | i: early universe galaxies
Astronomers thought they knew what Webb would find. In the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, galaxies should have been small, faint and messy, slowly gathering gas and forming their first stars.

> i: webb galaxies early | v: zoom early galaxy
Within weeks of its first images, teams began reporting surprises. Distant galaxies were brighter and more numerous than expected. In early 2023, one team announced six candidates that appeared astonishingly massive, as heavy as our own Milky Way, but existing only about five to seven hundred million years after the Big Bang.

> v: galaxy formation simulation | i: galaxy formation
If those masses were right, the universe would have had to turn almost all of its available gas into stars, almost instantly. Some called them universe breakers.

> i: deep field | v: deep field zoom
Later observations showed that many of those first estimates were too high. Some of the light came from hot gas or growing black holes rather than stars. The crisis softened. [pause] But it did not disappear.

## The Most Distant Galaxy
mood: wonder
> v: jades deep field | i: jades deep field
In 2024, Webb confirmed a galaxy called {{JADES-GS-z14-0|JADES-GS-z14-0}}. Its light left it just two hundred and ninety million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was about two percent of its current age.

> i: distant galaxy | v: zoom distant galaxy
It was not a faint smudge. It was bright, and surprisingly large, around sixteen hundred light years across. And in its light, astronomers found the signature of oxygen, an element that can only be made inside stars, and released when they die.

> v: star formation | i: star forming region
That means that by the time we see it, generations of stars had already lived and died there. In a universe less than three hundred million years old, that is remarkably fast.

> i: webb deep field galaxies | v: webb deep field
Since then, Webb has found even more distant candidates, pushing the cosmic dawn closer and closer to the Big Bang itself. Galaxies seem to have switched on earlier, and burned brighter, than almost anyone predicted.

> v: deep field zoom | i: webb deep field galaxies
In 2025, the record was broken again, with a galaxy seen just two hundred and eighty million years after the Big Bang, bright and already rich in heavy elements. Each new record holder has been brighter than the models predicted. One surprise can be luck. A pattern of surprises is a clue.

## The Little Red Dots
mood: mystery
> i: little red dots webb | v: deep field zoom
And then came something even stranger. In Webb's deep images, astronomers kept finding tiny, compact, intensely red objects scattered across the early universe. They were nicknamed the {{little red dots|LITTLE RED DOTS}}.

> v: black hole accretion disc | i: supermassive black hole
At first, they looked like impossibly dense galaxies, packed with more stars than seemed possible. Then many turned out to have the signature of fast-moving gas, the mark of growing black holes. But they did not behave like any black holes we knew.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: black hole simulation
They were too red, and too faint in X-rays. Some of the black holes inside them seemed far too heavy compared to their tiny host galaxies. One recent idea is that they are black holes wrapped in a dense cocoon of gas, so thick that the whole object glows almost like an enormous star, a {{black hole star|BLACK HOLE STAR}}.

> v: early universe black hole | i: early black hole
Whatever they are, they were common for a while in the young universe, and then they seem to have vanished. Something happened in the first billion years that we do not yet understand.

> i: black hole early universe x-ray | v: black hole feeding
Webb has also caught supermassive black holes that already existed when the universe was less than five hundred million years old, some weighing millions of Suns. Growing a black hole that large that fast is extremely difficult unless it began life already enormous, perhaps from a vast cloud of gas that collapsed directly into a black hole, skipping the stage of being a star at all.

> v: early universe | i: early universe galaxies
Together, these discoveries paint a picture of a young universe that was busier, brighter and more violent than we imagined. The cosmic dawn did not arrive slowly. It seems to have arrived in a rush.

## Is Cosmology Broken?
mood: wonder
> v: cosmic microwave background | i: cosmic microwave background
So did James Webb break our model of the universe? Not yet. The standard picture of the Big Bang, dark matter and dark energy still explains an enormous amount, from the glow of the cosmic microwave background to the way galaxies cluster today.

> i: galaxy formation | v: galaxy formation simulation
But the details of how the first galaxies formed are clearly more surprising than we thought. Perhaps early stars formed far more efficiently. Perhaps the first stars were much more massive, making galaxies brighter than their weight suggests. Perhaps black holes formed faster, from enormous collapsing clouds.

> v: zoom webb deep field | i: webb deep field
Each possibility tells us something new about the first chapter of cosmic history, the chapter no human had ever seen until now.

> i: hubble ultra deep field | v: zoom out universe
Webb is expected to keep working for many years yet, peering further back than ever. Somewhere in that darkness are the very first galaxies, and perhaps the very first stars. [pause] And if they are as surprising as everything Webb has found so far, the story of how the universe began may still need to be rewritten.
