A hundred years ago, most astronomers thought the Milky Way was the entire universe. The faint fuzzy patches in their telescopes were thought to be clouds of gas inside it.

> i: andromeda galaxy | v: andromeda galaxy
Then, in the 1920s, Edwin Hubble proved that one of those fuzzy patches, Andromeda, was far beyond the Milky Way. It was a whole galaxy, with hundreds of billions of stars of its own.

> i: galaxy collection | v: galaxy zoom
Today we know the observable universe contains perhaps two trillion galaxies. And they come in a stunning variety of shapes and sizes. Graceful spirals, giant glowing eggs, tiny faint ghosts, rings, and galaxies trailing tentacles like jellyfish.

> i: spiral galaxy | v: spiral galaxy rotating
Each shape tells a story of how that galaxy was born, how it grew and what has happened to it since. [pause] This is every type of galaxy, explained.

## The Spirals
mood: wonder
> i: hubble tuning fork | v: galaxy
In 1926, Hubble sorted galaxies by their appearance into a simple diagram that became known as the {{tuning fork|THE TUNING FORK}}. It is still the starting point for describing galaxies today.

> i: spiral galaxy | v: spiral galaxy rotating
The most famous type is the {{spiral galaxy|SPIRAL}}. It has a flat, rotating disc of stars, gas and dust, with a bright central bulge and arms that wind outwards. The arms glow blue with young, hot stars, born from the gas and dust concentrated there.

> i: spiral galaxy arms | v: spiral galaxy
The arms are not solid structures. They are more like traffic jams, regions where stars and gas bunch up as they orbit, then move on. [pause] The pattern stays, while the stars pass through it.

> i: whirlpool galaxy | v: spiral galaxy
Some spirals have two long, sharply defined arms, like the Whirlpool galaxy. Astronomers call them grand design spirals. Others have many short, patchy arm fragments, which give them a fluffy look. [pause] Those are known as flocculent spirals, from a word meaning tufts of wool.

> i: barred spiral galaxy | v: barred spiral galaxy
About two thirds of spiral galaxies have a straight bar of stars across their centre, with the arms starting from its ends. These are {{barred spirals|BARRED SPIRALS}}. Our own Milky Way is one of them. [pause] Bars act like funnels, channelling gas towards the centre, where it can feed new stars or the central black hole.

> i: milky way | v: milky way
The Milky Way is about a hundred thousand light-years across and holds perhaps a hundred to four hundred billion stars. [pause] Seen from outside, it would look much like the spirals we photograph, with the Sun in a quiet suburb about halfway out.

## The Giants And The Lenses
mood: epic
> i: elliptical galaxy | v: elliptical galaxy
At the other end of Hubble's diagram are the {{elliptical galaxies|ELLIPTICALS}}. They have no disc and no arms. They are smooth, egg-shaped swarms of stars, buzzing around the centre in every direction, like bees around a hive.

> i: m87 galaxy | v: elliptical galaxy
Ellipticals contain mostly old, red stars and very little gas for making new ones. They range from small to enormous. The giant elliptical Messier 87, at the heart of a nearby galaxy cluster, holds trillions of stars and the first black hole ever photographed.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
The biggest ellipticals sit at the centres of galaxy clusters, where they have grown fat by swallowing their neighbours. [pause] Some are so large that the Milky Way would look small beside them.

> i: lenticular galaxy | v: galaxy
Between the spirals and the ellipticals are the {{lenticular galaxies|LENTICULARS}}, shaped like a lens. They have a disc and a bulge, like a spiral, but no spiral arms and little gas. [pause] Many may be former spirals that used up or lost their gas and faded into retirement.

> i: sombrero galaxy | v: sombrero galaxy
The famous Sombrero galaxy, with its bright bulge and dark ring of dust, sits near this boundary, with features of both spirals and ellipticals. [pause] Nature does not always fit our neat categories.

## The Small Ones
mood: mystery
> i: large magellanic cloud | v: magellanic clouds
Not all galaxies have regular shapes. {{Irregular galaxies|IRREGULAR}} are chaotic, often rich in gas and bursting with new stars. The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, two galaxies orbiting our own, are well-known examples. From the southern hemisphere, they are visible to the naked eye.

> i: dwarf galaxy | v: dwarf galaxy
The most common galaxies of all are {{dwarf galaxies|DWARF GALAXIES}}. They contain anywhere from a few thousand to a few billion stars. Dozens orbit the Milky Way, like moons around a planet.

> i: dwarf spheroidal galaxy | v: star field
Some are so faint that they were only discovered in the last twenty years by careful surveys. These ultra-faint dwarfs can contain just a few thousand stars, but enormous amounts of dark matter. [pause] In some, dark matter outweighs the stars by more than a thousand to one.

> i: dwarf galaxy | v: galaxy
Dwarf galaxies are thought to be the building blocks of larger galaxies. Over billions of years, the Milky Way has grown by swallowing many of them. Streams of stars from torn-apart dwarfs still wrap around our galaxy today. [pause] We are living inside the result of countless cosmic meals.

## The Strange Ones
mood: mystery
> i: ring galaxy | v: galaxy collision
Some galaxies break the rules entirely. {{Ring galaxies|RING GALAXIES}} have a bright ring of stars surrounding a core, sometimes with nothing but dark space in between. Most form when a smaller galaxy plunges straight through the centre of a larger one, sending a ripple of star formation outwards like a stone dropped in a pond.

> i: jellyfish galaxy | v: jellyfish galaxy
{{Jellyfish galaxies|JELLYFISH GALAXIES}} are spirals falling at high speed into galaxy clusters. As they plunge through the hot gas filling the cluster, their own gas is stripped away and trails behind them in long glowing streamers, studded with newborn stars. [pause] They look like jellyfish drifting through space.

> i: ultra diffuse galaxy | v: galaxy
And then there are the ghosts. {{Ultra-diffuse galaxies|ULTRA-DIFFUSE}} can be as wide as the Milky Way but contain only around a hundredth as many stars. They are so faint that they were largely missed until 2015. One, called Dragonfly 44, seems to be made almost entirely of dark matter.

> i: starburst galaxy | v: starburst galaxy
{{Starburst galaxies|STARBURST}} are making stars at furious rates, often triggered by a close encounter with another galaxy. The Cigar galaxy, Messier 82, is forming stars about ten times faster than the Milky Way, and its exploding stars drive huge winds of gas out of it. [pause] Some galaxies are in their prime, and some are dying.

## Galaxies Change
mood: epic
> i: galaxy merger | v: galaxy collision
The shapes of galaxies are not fixed. Galaxies collide, merge and transform. When two spirals of similar size collide, their discs are scrambled, and over hundreds of millions of years, they can settle into a single elliptical.

> i: antennae galaxies | v: galaxy collision
The Antennae galaxies, about sixty million light-years away, are caught in the middle of such a collision. Two long tails of stars, flung out by gravity, stretch away from them like insect antennae. [pause] Their collision is triggering the birth of millions of new stars.

> i: andromeda milky way collision | v: galaxy collision
Our own galaxy may face the same fate. Andromeda is approaching us at more than a hundred kilometres per second. Recent studies suggest there is roughly a fifty percent chance that the two will merge within the next ten billion years. [pause] If they do, the result will likely be a giant elliptical galaxy.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
When the James Webb telescope looks back to the young universe, it sees galaxies that are smaller, clumpier and more irregular. Yet it has also found surprisingly mature discs and early galaxies with shapes resembling bars. [pause] Galaxy shapes seem to have formed earlier than expected.

> i: galaxy collection | v: galaxy zoom
Spirals, ellipticals, lenses, irregulars, dwarfs, rings, jellyfish and ghosts. Two trillion galaxies, each with a story written in its shape. [pause] And all of them, like ours, are still changing.
