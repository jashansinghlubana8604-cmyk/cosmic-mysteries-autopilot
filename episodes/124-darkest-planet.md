About seven hundred and fifty light-years away, in the constellation Draco, there is a planet the size of Jupiter that is almost perfectly black. Not dark grey. Not dark blue. Black.

> i: dark planet | v: hot jupiter
It reflects less than one percent of the starlight that falls on it. That makes it darker than coal. Darker than black acrylic paint. Darker than any planet, moon or asteroid in our own solar system.

> i: hot jupiter | v: exoplanet
If you could see it against the night sky, it would look like a hole, a disc of darkness against the stars. Only up close would you notice a faint, sullen glow, a deep red, like the last embers of a dying fire. [pause] It is so hot that it shines with its own heat.

> i: tres-2b | v: dark planet
Its name is {{TrES-2b|TrES-2b}}. And it is the darkest planet ever found. [pause] So what makes a giant world swallow almost all the light that touches it?

## A Giant Too Close
mood: wonder
> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
TrES-2b was discovered in 2006 by the Trans-Atlantic Exoplanet Survey, a network of small telescopes watching thousands of stars for the tiny, regular dips in brightness caused by planets passing in front of them.

> i: hot jupiter | v: hot jupiter
It turned out to be a {{hot Jupiter|HOT JUPITER}}. A gas giant a little larger and heavier than Jupiter, but orbiting incredibly close to its star, only about five million kilometres away. One year on TrES-2b lasts just two and a half Earth days.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
Its star is too faint to see with the naked eye, but a decent backyard telescope can pick it out among the stars of Draco, the dragon, which coils around the northern sky between the Plough and the Little Bear. [pause] Somewhere next to that faint point of light, a black giant is racing around it.

> i: star | v: star
Its star is similar to our Sun. At that tiny distance, it fills an enormous part of the planet's sky, and heats its atmosphere to more than a thousand degrees. Like most hot Jupiters, TrES-2b is probably tidally locked, with one side facing its star forever.

> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
TrES-2b happened to lie in the field of view of NASA's Kepler Space Telescope, which watched the same patch of sky continuously for years with extraordinary precision. That gave astronomers a rare chance. [pause] Not just to see the planet passing in front of its star, but to measure the faint light the planet itself reflects.

## Measuring A Shadow
mood: mystery
> i: exoplanet transit | v: exoplanet transit
As TrES-2b orbits, we see different amounts of its day side, just as we see the phases of the Moon. When the bright side faces us, the total light from the star and planet together should be very slightly higher. When the night side faces us, slightly lower.

> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
In 2011, astronomers David Kipping and David Spiegel searched for those changes in more than fifty orbits of Kepler data. The variation they found was astonishingly small, about six parts in a million. [pause] Kepler could detect it only because it was so stable and watched for so long.

> i: kepler telescope | v: kepler
To put that in perspective, it is like noticing a moth fluttering near a stadium floodlight from many kilometres away, and then working out the colour of the moth's wings. [pause] Kepler was built to find Earth-sized planets. It turned out to be sensitive enough to measure the darkness of a distant world.

> i: dark planet | v: dark planet
From that tiny signal, they worked out how much light the planet reflects. The answer was {{less than one percent|< 1% REFLECTED}}. Jupiter reflects about a third of the sunlight that hits it. The Earth reflects about thirty percent. Even our Moon, which looks bright in the night sky, reflects about twelve percent. Fresh asphalt reflects around five.

> i: tres-2b | v: dark planet
TrES-2b is darker than all of them. It absorbs more than ninety nine percent of the light that hits it. [pause] Some of the faint glow Kepler detected was not reflected light at all, but heat, the planet's own red glow.

## Why So Dark?
mood: mystery
> i: exoplanet atmosphere | v: exoplanet atmosphere
Why is it so black? Jupiter is bright because it has high clouds of ammonia ice that reflect sunlight back into space. TrES-2b is far too hot for clouds like that. Without reflective clouds, sunlight plunges deep into its atmosphere.

> i: hot jupiter | v: exoplanet atmosphere
And its atmosphere seems to be full of substances that absorb light very efficiently. Scientists suspect gaseous sodium and potassium, which absorb strongly in certain colours, and perhaps molecules like titanium oxide or vanadium oxide, which absorb across much of the visible spectrum.

> i: tres-2b | v: hot jupiter
Together, they act like an enormous light trap. Starlight goes in and does not come out. It is converted into heat, which the planet then radiates away as a dull infrared and red glow. [pause] Even so, the scientists admitted they could not fully explain why it is quite this dark.

> i: dark planet | v: dark planet
If you painted a ball with the blackest ordinary paint you could buy, it would still reflect a few percent of the light that hits it. TrES-2b does better than that. [pause] Nature has made a planet darker than most black things humans have ever manufactured.

## The Dark Family
mood: dread
> i: wasp-12b | v: hot jupiter
TrES-2b is not alone. Other hot Jupiters have turned out to be remarkably dark too. {{WASP-12b|WASP-12b}}, a giant planet so close to its star that it is being slowly stretched and consumed, was found by Hubble to reflect very little light, similar to fresh asphalt. Its day side is so hot that molecules there are thought to be broken apart, unable to form clouds.

> i: dark planet | v: dark planet
In 2018, astronomers reported another, WASP-104b, which they described as darker than charcoal. It seems that very hot, cloudless giant planets often end up as some of the blackest objects we know.

> i: hot jupiter | v: hot jupiter
It turns out that the most common colour for a scorching giant planet may not be the bright banded beige of Jupiter, or the blue of Neptune. [pause] It may be the black of a burned-out furnace.

> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
The James Webb Space Telescope can now study the atmospheres of planets like these in far greater detail, measuring which molecules are present and how heat moves around them. [pause] Each one helps explain why some giant worlds shine and others vanish into darkness.

## A Hole In The Sky
mood: wonder
> i: tres-2b | v: dark planet
Imagine floating near TrES-2b. On its day side, beneath a blazing sun, there would be almost nothing to see. Just a vast, dark curve, absorbing the light, with perhaps a faint red shimmer where the hottest gas glows.

> i: dark planet | v: hot jupiter
On its night side, it would glow very faintly in deep red, like iron cooling in a forge. Fierce winds would carry heat from the day side around to the night, in an atmosphere hotter than any oven on Earth.

> i: star field | v: star field
TrES-2b is a reminder that planets come in colours and conditions we would never have imagined from our own solar system. Not every giant world is a striped pastel ball. [pause] Some are black, glowing faintly, swallowing the light of their suns. Out there in Draco, one of them is circling its star every two and a half days. A planet made almost entirely of shadow.
