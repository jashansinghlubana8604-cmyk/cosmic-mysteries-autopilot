## Where Rings Can Live
mood: wonder
> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
First, where would the rings be? Rings can only survive close to a planet, inside a boundary called the {{Roche limit|ROCHE LIMIT}}. Inside it, the planet's gravity pulls harder on the near side of an object than on its far side, tearing apart anything held together only by its own gravity.

> i: moon | v: moon orbit
For the Earth, this limit lies within roughly eighteen thousand kilometres of the planet's centre, depending on what the ring is made of. Our Moon orbits about twenty times farther out. [pause] If the Moon came within the limit, it would be torn apart, and its pieces would spread into a ring.

> i: saturn rings close up | v: saturn rings
Inside that boundary, debris cannot clump together into a moon. Instead, it spreads into a flat, thin disc lined up with the planet's equator. Collisions between particles flatten it, the way Saturn's rings are flattened. [pause] So an Earth ring would circle directly above the equator, starting a few thousand kilometres up and extending outwards for thousands more.

> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
Let's imagine our ring is mostly ice, like Saturn's, so it shines brightly in sunlight. [pause] Now let's travel around the world and look up.

## The View From Earth
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky timelapse
If you stood exactly on the equator, the rings would be almost invisible. You would see them edge-on, as a thin bright line running straight overhead, from the eastern horizon to the western one.

> i: earth with rings | v: saturn rings
But move away from the equator and the rings open up. From places like India, Mexico or Egypt, they would arch high across the sky as a {{great glowing band|A GREAT ARCH}}, many times wider than the Moon. From the northern hemisphere, they would stretch across the southern sky. From the southern hemisphere, across the northern sky.

> i: saturn rings close up | v: saturn rings
Further north, in places like London, Moscow or Toronto, the arch would sit lower. The inner part of the ring would be hidden below the horizon, leaving only an outer band hanging above it. [pause] Near the poles, the rings would disappear entirely below the horizon.

> i: night sky | v: night sky stars
At night, the rings would keep reflecting sunlight, because much of them would still be in sunshine high above the Earth's shadow. Nights would be far brighter, perhaps as bright as a full Moon, every night of the year. [pause] It would be harder to see faint stars. Astronomy, and much of human mythology, might have developed very differently.

> i: earth shadow | v: earth from space
And every night you would see something beautiful: the Earth's own round shadow, cast across the glowing rings, sweeping slowly through the hours. [pause] A visible reminder that we live on a sphere.

## Shadows And Seasons
mood: mystery
> i: saturn ring shadow | v: saturn rings
On Saturn, the rings cast huge shadows on the planet. Earth's rings would do the same. Because the Earth is tilted, the shadow would fall on different places through the year.

> i: earth seasons | v: earth orbit
In northern winter, the Sun sits low in the southern sky, and the rings would lie between the Sun and much of the northern hemisphere. A broad band of shadow would fall across the northern tropics and beyond, blocking part of the sunlight. [pause] Then in southern winter, the shadow would move south.

> i: winter landscape | v: snow falling
That means the shadow would fall on whichever hemisphere was already having winter, making winters colder still in those regions. Places beneath the shadow could see weeks or months where the Sun was dimmed or eclipsed for part of each day. [pause] Climates, crops and the places where people chose to live would all be affected.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotating
And here is where the imaginary becomes real. In 2024, a team of geologists noticed that twenty one impact craters from around four hundred and sixty six million years ago all seem to lie close to the equator of that time. They proposed that a large asteroid broke apart near the Earth and formed a ring, which then rained down debris for millions of years. [pause] The idea is still debated. But they even suggested that the ring's shadow may have helped trigger one of the coldest periods in Earth's history.

## Rockets And Satellites
mood: dread
> i: satellite orbiting earth | v: satellite orbiting earth
A ring would also be a problem for modern life. Much of the space around the equator, out to thousands of kilometres, would be filled with rock and ice moving at several kilometres per second.

> i: rocket launch | v: rocket launch
Rockets would need to avoid flying through the ring plane. Launch sites might move to high latitudes so that rockets could climb up and away from the rings. [pause] Even a pebble striking a spacecraft at those speeds would hit with the force of an explosion.

> i: geostationary satellite | v: satellite orbiting earth
Geostationary satellites, which hang above the equator about thirty six thousand kilometres up, sit far beyond any ring, so they might survive. But many satellites in lower orbits would be in danger. [pause] Our space age would be far more dangerous, and far more expensive.

> i: meteor shower | v: meteor shower
And rings do not last forever. Particles slowly lose energy and fall into the planet. A ring around the Earth would feed a steady shower of meteors along the equator. [pause] Spectacular to watch, but a constant reminder that the ring was slowly falling.

## Rings Come And Go
mood: epic
> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
Rings may be more common, and more temporary, than we used to think. Measurements by the Cassini spacecraft suggested that Saturn's rings may be far younger than Saturn itself, perhaps a few hundred million years old, though that is still debated. [pause] And they are slowly raining into the planet.

> i: phobos | v: mars moon
Mars may get rings in the future. Its moon Phobos is spiralling inwards and, within around fifty million years, it should be torn apart, possibly forming a ring around Mars.

> i: quaoar | v: dwarf planet
Even small worlds have rings. Astronomers have found rings around the small icy body Chariklo, the dwarf planet Haumea and the distant world {{Quaoar|QUAOAR}}, whose ring orbits far outside where theory said it should be able to survive. [pause] Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune all have faint rings too.

> i: moon formation | v: planet collision
And our own Moon may have started as a ring. After a planet-sized body hit the young Earth, debris spread into a disc around our planet, and the Moon formed from that material just outside the Roche limit.

> i: earth with rings | v: night sky
So the Earth may have had rings at least twice in its past. Today, our sky is ringless, lit at night only by the Moon and stars. [pause] But on the long timescales of the universe, rings come and go. And somewhere out there, on worlds we have yet to find, a sky like this one may be shining right now.
