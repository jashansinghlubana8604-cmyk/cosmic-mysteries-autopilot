Right now, as you watch this, you are moving very fast. If you live near the equator, the ground beneath you is carrying you eastward at about one thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres per hour. Faster than a passenger jet. Faster than the speed of sound.

> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
You do not feel it, because everything around you is moving too. The air, the oceans, the buildings, the ground. All of it, spinning together, as it has for four and a half billion years.

> i: earth night | v: earth night
So what would happen if the Earth suddenly stopped spinning? Not slowed down. Stopped. [pause] It is physically impossible. Nothing could stop a planet that fast. But following the idea step by step reveals how much our world depends on that steady turning.

> i: storm | v: storm clouds
The first few seconds would be the most violent moments in human history. [pause] And the months after would reshape the entire planet.

## The First Second
mood: dread
> i: earth rotation | v: earth rotation
If the solid Earth stopped instantly, everything that is not firmly attached to it would keep going. Inertia. The same reason you lurch forward when a bus brakes hard.

> i: storm | v: hurricane
Near the equator, the atmosphere would keep racing east at around {{1,670 km/h|1,670 KM/H}}. That is a wind several times stronger than the most powerful hurricane ever recorded. It would scour the surface, tearing up trees, buildings and soil, and hurling them eastward.

> i: ocean waves | v: ocean waves
The oceans would do the same. Vast walls of water would surge eastward across the continents, in tsunamis kilometres high in places. People, animals, cars and loose rocks near the equator would be flung across the landscape at the speed the Earth had been turning.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
Near the poles, things would be much calmer, because the ground there moves far more slowly. At the poles themselves, it barely moves at all. [pause] Ironically, the safest places on the planet would be the Arctic and Antarctic.

> i: volcano | v: volcano
The friction from all that sudden movement would release enormous amounts of heat. Some scientists have even suggested the stress could trigger earthquakes and volcanic eruptions around the world. [pause] A sudden stop would be a planetary catastrophe.

## A Gentler Stop
mood: mystery
> i: earth from space | v: earth from space
So let's imagine a gentler version. Suppose the Earth slowed down gradually, over many years, until it stopped. No sudden winds. No instant tsunamis. What would change then?

> i: earth bulge | v: earth rotation
The first surprise would be the oceans. The Earth is not a perfect sphere. Its spin makes it bulge at the equator by about twenty one kilometres, and the oceans bulge even more than the rock beneath them, flung outward by the spin.

> i: ocean | v: ocean waves
Without the spin, that bulge of water would no longer be held up at the equator. Gravity would pull the oceans towards the poles. Simulations suggest the result would be two enormous polar oceans, one in the north and one in the south, and a single giant continent wrapped around the middle of the planet. [pause] The map of the world would be redrawn completely.

> i: mountain | v: mountain
In places, the land around the equator would rise kilometres above the new sea level. The air would be thin and freezing. Coastlines that people have lived on for thousands of years would be left high and dry, while lands near the poles would vanish beneath the water.

## A Year-Long Day
mood: wonder
> i: sunrise | v: sunrise
Now think about the day itself. Today, the Earth turns once every twenty four hours, giving us day and night. If it stopped spinning completely, the Sun would still appear to move slowly across the sky, but only because the Earth goes around it.

> i: sunset | v: sunset
One full day would last {{a whole year|1 DAY = 1 YEAR}}. Six months of continuous daylight, followed by six months of darkness. On the day side, the ground would bake under a Sun that never set. On the night side, temperatures would plunge far below anything on Earth today.

> i: forest | v: plants
Life itself would be thrown into confusion. Almost every living thing on Earth, from bacteria to trees to people, runs on an internal clock tuned to a twenty four hour cycle. Plants open and close their leaves, animals sleep and hunt, and our own bodies release hormones, all to that rhythm. [pause] A day lasting a year would break the clock that life has kept for billions of years.

> i: venus | v: venus
We have a real example of a slowly spinning planet. Venus takes two hundred and forty three Earth days to turn once, and it spins backwards. Its thick atmosphere spreads heat around, so its night side is just as scorching as its day side. [pause] The Earth, with its much thinner air, would not be so lucky.

> i: storm clouds | v: storm clouds
Our weather would change too. Today, the Earth's spin twists winds into great curving systems, an effect called the Coriolis effect. It is why hurricanes spin. Without rotation, weather would be driven mostly by the giant temperature difference between the endless day and the endless night, with vast winds blowing from one to the other.

## Invisible Changes
mood: dread
> i: earth magnetic field | v: earth magnetic field
There would be hidden consequences too. The Earth's magnetic field is generated by liquid iron churning in its outer core, and the planet's rotation helps organise that churning. Some scientists think that without the spin, the field could weaken or change dramatically.

> i: aurora | v: aurora
A weaker magnetic field would let more charged particles from the Sun reach the upper atmosphere. Over long periods, it could leave the surface more exposed to radiation. [pause] Our invisible shield might start to fail.

> i: sea turtle | v: birds flying
And many animals would lose their way. Sea turtles, migrating birds and even some fish are thought to use the Earth's magnetic field like a compass, sensing it to navigate across oceans and continents. [pause] If the field faded or changed, journeys their ancestors have made for millions of years could lead them nowhere.

> i: satellites orbit | v: satellites orbit
Our technology would be affected in strange ways. Communications satellites in geostationary orbit hover over one spot on the Earth only because they orbit at exactly the same rate the planet turns. Without the spin, they would drift across the sky.

> i: scale | v: earth rotation
Even your weight would change. The spin slightly counteracts gravity at the equator. If the Earth stopped, a person at the equator would weigh about {{a third of a percent more|+0.3%}}. Not much. But a strange reminder that, right now, the spin of the planet is very slightly lifting you.

## The Spin We Take For Granted
mood: wonder
> i: earth moon | v: earth moon
The good news is that this cannot happen. Nothing in the solar system has the power to stop the Earth's rotation. It is slowing, very gradually, because of the Moon's tides, making each day a tiny fraction of a second longer every century. [pause] But it will keep turning for billions of years.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
And that turning shapes everything. It gives us sunrises and sunsets. It keeps the oceans where they are. It shapes the winds and the weather, and helps power the magnetic field that protects us.

> i: earth night | v: earth night
So the next time you watch the Sun go down, remember what is really happening. The Sun is not moving. You are. You are riding the edge of a spinning planet, turning away from the light at hundreds of kilometres per hour. [pause] And in a few hours, it will carry you back around into the morning, exactly as it has every day of your life.
