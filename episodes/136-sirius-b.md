Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky. On winter evenings in the northern hemisphere, it blazes low in the south, flashing with colour as its light passes through the atmosphere. People have watched it for thousands of years. The ancient Egyptians built their calendar around it.

> i: sirius b | v: binary star
But Sirius is not alone. Right next to it, hidden in its glare, there is a second star. Ten thousand times fainter. About the size of the Earth. And yet, it weighs about as much as the Sun.

> i: sirius white dwarf | v: white dwarf
When astronomers first realised how small and heavy it was, they did not believe it. A teaspoon of its material would weigh several tonnes. Nothing like it was known to exist. [pause] To explain it, physicists needed a brand new branch of science: quantum mechanics.

> i: sirius | v: star
This is Sirius B. The first white dwarf ever discovered. [pause] And the story of how the brightest star in the sky led us to the strange fate awaiting our own Sun.

## The Dog Star
mood: wonder
> i: night sky | v: night sky
Sirius is just over eight and a half light-years away, one of the closest stars to the Sun. It is about twice as heavy as the Sun and around twenty five times brighter. Its closeness and power make it the brightest star in our sky. When it is low near the horizon, its light passes through a thick layer of turbulent air, which splits and bends it, so the star seems to flicker red, green and blue. Many reports of strange flashing lights in the sky have turned out to be Sirius.

> i: canis major | v: night sky
It sits in the constellation Canis Major, the great dog, which is why it is often called the Dog Star. The ancient Greeks linked its appearance in the summer dawn sky with the hottest days of the year, which is where the phrase the dog days of summer comes from.

> i: nile egypt | v: river
In ancient Egypt, the first appearance of Sirius before sunrise, after months hidden in the Sun's glare, came around the time the River Nile began to flood. That flood brought the fertile soil that made Egyptian civilisation possible. [pause] Sirius marked the start of their year.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
In the twentieth century, a controversial claim spread that the Dogon people of Mali had ancient knowledge of a small, heavy companion to Sirius, long before Western astronomers. Later researchers found serious problems with the original accounts, and many concluded the information most likely came from contact with outsiders. [pause] It remains a popular story, but not good evidence.

## The Wobble
mood: mystery
> i: sirius | v: star
In 1844, the German astronomer {{Friedrich Bessel|FRIEDRICH BESSEL}} was carefully measuring the positions of stars. He noticed that Sirius was not moving across the sky in a straight line. It was wobbling, swinging slightly back and forth along its path, over a period of about fifty years.

> i: binary star | v: binary star
Bessel concluded that Sirius must be orbiting an unseen companion. Something heavy enough to pull it around, but too faint to see. He was predicting an invisible star from its gravity alone, years before anyone could confirm it. [pause] It was one of the first times astronomers had deduced a hidden object purely by its pull.

> i: telescope dome | v: telescope
On the thirty first of January 1862, the American telescope maker {{Alvan Graham Clark|ALVAN GRAHAM CLARK}} was testing a new lens, the largest of its kind at the time, by pointing it at Sirius. Next to the dazzling star, he saw a tiny, faint point of light. [pause] Bessel's invisible companion had been found. It was named Sirius B.

> i: binary star | v: binary star
We now know the two stars orbit each other every fifty years, on a stretched path that brings them as close as about eight times the Earth-Sun distance, and as far apart as about thirty one. [pause] When they are furthest apart, astronomers have the best chance of seeing the faint companion beside its dazzling partner.

## Impossibly Dense
mood: dread
> i: sirius white dwarf | v: white dwarf
At first, astronomers assumed Sirius B was simply a dim, cool star. But in 1915, astronomer Walter Adams studied its light and found something shocking. It was hot. White hot, with a surface far hotter than the Sun's.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
A hot star should be bright. Sirius B was faint. The only way a hot object can be faint is if it is very small. Calculations showed it must be roughly the size of the Earth. Yet its orbit with Sirius showed it weighed about as much as the Sun.

> i: white dwarf | v: neutron star
That meant its material had to be incredibly dense. A cubic centimetre, the size of a sugar cube, would weigh about a tonne. The astronomer Arthur Eddington wrote that such a result seemed absurd. [pause] But the evidence would not go away.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
Gravity at its surface is hundreds of thousands of times stronger than on Earth. A person standing there, if that were possible, would weigh as much as a fleet of lorries, and be instantly crushed flat against the surface. [pause] Everything about Sirius B was extreme.

## Quantum Matter
mood: mystery
> i: physics laboratory | v: quantum
The explanation came from the new science of quantum mechanics. In 1926, the physicist Ralph Fowler showed that when matter is squeezed hard enough, its electrons resist being packed any tighter. Not because of heat, but because of a basic quantum rule: no two electrons can occupy the same state.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
This resistance, called {{electron degeneracy pressure|DEGENERACY PRESSURE}}, can hold up a dead star against its own gravity, even after its nuclear fires have gone out. Sirius B, and all white dwarfs, are held up by quantum physics.

> i: subrahmanyan chandrasekhar | v: physics laboratory
In 1930, a nineteen-year-old Indian physicist named {{Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar|CHANDRASEKHAR}}, travelling by ship to study in England, calculated that this pressure has a limit. Above about one point four times the mass of the Sun, a white dwarf would collapse. Eddington publicly mocked the idea. [pause] Chandrasekhar was right. In 1983, he won the Nobel Prize, and the limit now bears his name.

## Our Sun's Future
mood: wonder
> i: sirius b | v: binary star
Sirius B was once a much bigger star than Sirius A, perhaps around five times the mass of the Sun. Being more massive, it burned out faster. Roughly a hundred million years ago, it swelled into a red giant, shed its outer layers and left behind its exposed core. [pause] The faint white dwarf we see today.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
Its surface is still around twenty five thousand degrees, but it has no fuel left. It is simply cooling, very slowly, like an ember after a fire. Over billions of years, it will dim and redden. [pause] White dwarfs cool so slowly that none in the universe has yet had time to go completely dark.

> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
In 2005, Hubble measured the light from Sirius B precisely enough to see it stretched slightly by its own powerful gravity, just as Einstein's general relativity predicts. From that stretch, astronomers weighed it at almost exactly one solar mass.

> i: sun | v: sun
White dwarfs are the most common fate of stars. In about five billion years, our own Sun will become one, a dim, glowing ember the size of the Earth. [pause] So when you look up at Sirius, blazing in the winter sky, remember the faint companion hidden beside it. You are looking at the future of our own star.
