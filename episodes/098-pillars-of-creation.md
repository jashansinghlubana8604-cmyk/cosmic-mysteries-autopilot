## The Picture That Changed Astronomy
mood: wonder
> i: hubble space telescope | v: hubble space telescope
On the first of April 1995, astronomers Jeff Hester and Paul Scowen used Hubble's newly repaired camera to photograph part of the Eagle Nebula, a vast cloud of gas and dust in the constellation Serpens, about six and a half thousand light-years away.

> i: pillars of creation | v: pillars of creation
They had hoped to study how stars form. What they got was a work of art. When the image was released later that year, it appeared on magazine covers, posters, stamps and T-shirts around the world. For many people, it was the first time they had felt the beauty of deep space.

> i: eagle nebula | v: nebula
The colours in that famous image were chosen by the scientists. Hubble photographed the pillars through filters that each let through the light of one element. Glowing hydrogen, sulphur and oxygen were each assigned a colour and combined. [pause] The result is not exactly what your eye would see. It is a map of what the pillars are made of, painted in light.

> i: pillars of creation | v: nebula
In 2014, to mark Hubble's twenty fifth anniversary, the team photographed the pillars again with a newer, sharper camera. The two images, nearly twenty years apart, revealed small changes. A jet of material from a newborn star had moved. [pause] The pillars were not frozen. They were alive.

## Light Versus Darkness
mood: dread
> i: eagle nebula | v: eagle nebula
Just above the pillars, out of frame, sits a cluster of young, hot, massive stars called NGC 6611. Some of them are many times heavier than the Sun and hundreds of thousands of times brighter.

> i: star cluster | v: star cluster
They blast the surrounding cloud with intense ultraviolet light and powerful winds of charged particles. That radiation heats the gas at the surface of the pillars and strips it away, a process called {{photoevaporation|PHOTOEVAPORATION}}. Like a hairdryer blowing on a sandcastle.

> i: pillars of creation | v: pillars of creation
The pillars survive because their tips are denser than the gas around them. These dense knots shield the material behind them, like a stone in a stream shielding the sand downstream. That is what gives the pillars their shape. [pause] Long columns, each pointing back towards the stars that are eroding them.

> i: pillars of creation | v: nebula
If you could somehow float near the pillars, they would not look like the bright, colourful images we know. The gas is so thin that, to the naked eye, it would appear dim and ghostly, a faint glow against the stars. [pause] Only long exposures, collecting light for hours, reveal their full shape.

> i: pillars of creation webb | v: nebula
But the shielding is not permanent. The pillars are steadily losing mass. Astronomers estimate that they will survive only a few million years more before being blown away completely. [pause] On the scale of a galaxy, that is the blink of an eye.

## Stars Being Born
mood: wonder
> i: pillars of creation | v: pillars of creation
And yet, inside the pillars, creation is happening. In the densest regions, gas and dust are collapsing under their own gravity. Hubble spotted small knots at the tips of the pillars, nicknamed {{EGGs|EGGs}}: evaporating gaseous globules. Some of them contain baby stars.

> i: pillars of creation webb | v: pillars of creation
Thin as it is, there is a lot of it. The pillars are so vast that, together, they hold enough gas and dust to build many stars like the Sun. Each one is a stellar nursery, slowly giving birth while it is being torn down.

> i: protostar | v: star forming region
It is a race. Can the stars inside finish forming before the radiation strips away the gas that feeds them? Some will make it, and emerge as fully formed stars. Others will be left stunted, cut off from their supply of material before they could grow. [pause] The same light that reveals them may also decide how big they get.

> i: pillars of creation webb | v: pillars of creation
In October 2022, the James Webb Space Telescope looked at the pillars in infrared light, which passes through dust. The image was stunning. The pillars became translucent, and thousands of stars appeared, glowing behind and inside them.

> i: pillars of creation webb | v: nebula
Webb also caught something wonderful at the edges of the pillars: wavy, bright red lines, like glowing lava. These are jets of material being fired out by newborn stars that are only a few hundred thousand years old. [pause] We are watching stars at the very beginning of their lives.

## Already Gone?
mood: mystery
> i: supernova remnant | v: supernova explosion
In 2007, the Spitzer Space Telescope found a cloud of hot dust near the pillars. Some astronomers suggested it could have been heated by a nearby supernova, a star exploding, perhaps around six thousand years ago.

> i: pillars of creation | v: pillars of creation
If that were true, the shock wave from the explosion could have reached the pillars and destroyed them a thousand or so years ago. Because the Eagle Nebula is about six and a half thousand light-years away, we would still see them standing today. [pause] The pillars might already be gone, and we just have not seen the news yet.

> i: eagle nebula | v: eagle nebula
Later studies, however, found little evidence for that supernova blast. Most astronomers now think the pillars are still there, still being eroded slowly by starlight. [pause] But the idea is a good reminder. Everything we see in space, we see as it was, not as it is.

## A Cosmic Sculpture
mood: wonder
> i: pillars of creation | v: pillars of creation
Astronomers have even mapped the pillars in three dimensions, using the motions of their gas. They found that the pillars are not side by side, as they appear. Some point towards us and others away, at different distances from the stars that light them.

> i: nebula | v: nebula
And there is nothing unique about the Eagle Nebula. Pillars like these form wherever massive young stars sit near clouds of gas. The Carina Nebula, the Cone Nebula and many others have their own towers of dust being sculpted by starlight. [pause] It is how much of the galaxy's star-making works.

> i: sun | v: sun
Our own Sun was probably born in a crowded nursery like this, around four and a half billion years ago, perhaps inside a dusty pillar near a cluster of bright stars. The material that became the Earth may once have been sculpted the same way. [pause] When we look at the Pillars of Creation, we may be looking at our own origins. Light, carving darkness into stars.
