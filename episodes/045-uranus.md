## The Planet Nobody Expected
mood: wonder
> i: william herschel telescope | v: telescope night
On the thirteenth of March 1781, an amateur astronomer named {{William Herschel|WILLIAM HERSCHEL}} was scanning the sky from his garden in Bath, England, with a telescope he had built himself. He noticed a small, faint disc that did not look like a star.

> i: uranus | v: night sky
At first he thought it was a comet. But as the months passed, its orbit turned out to be almost circular, far beyond Saturn. It was a new planet. The first discovered since ancient times, and the first ever found with a telescope. [pause] Overnight, the solar system doubled in size.

> i: uranus | v: uranus rotation
Herschel wanted to name it after his patron, King George the Third. For a while, British astronomers called it the Georgian Star. The rest of the world disagreed. Eventually, it was named after Uranus, the ancient Greek god of the sky. [pause] The father of Saturn, the grandfather of Jupiter.

> i: solar system | v: solar system
Uranus is about four times wider than the Earth, and roughly three billion kilometres from the Sun. Sunlight there is about four hundred times fainter than here. One Uranus year lasts {{84 Earth years|84 YEARS}}. If you were born on Uranus, you would have your first birthday as an old person on Earth.

## Forty Two Years Of Night
mood: mystery
> i: uranus tilt | v: uranus rotation
Uranus is tilted by about ninety eight degrees. That means that for part of its orbit, its north pole points almost directly at the Sun. Half the planet sits in continuous daylight. The other half, in continuous night.

> i: uranus | v: uranus
Twenty one years later, the planet has moved a quarter of the way round. Now the Sun shines on its equator, and day and night come and go every seventeen hours or so. Another twenty one years after that, it is the south pole's turn to face the Sun. [pause] Each pole gets about forty two years of sunlight, and then {{forty two years of darkness|42 YEARS OF NIGHT}}.

> i: uranus webb | v: uranus
Imagine standing near one of the poles. The Sun rises, and does not set for decades. It circles slowly in the sky. Then it sinks, and night falls for the rest of your childhood, and well into adulthood. [pause] The strangest seasons in the solar system.

> i: planet collision | v: planet collision
What knocked it over? The leading idea is a giant collision, early in the history of the solar system, with an object perhaps a few times the size of the Earth. Some scientists think it may have taken more than one hit. Others suggest it was tipped gradually by the gravity of a lost moon. [pause] Nobody knows for sure. But something enormous happened here.

## The Coldest Planet
mood: dread
> i: uranus | v: uranus
Uranus should not be the coldest planet. Neptune is about one and a half times further from the Sun. And yet, the coldest temperature ever measured in a planetary atmosphere belongs to Uranus. About {{minus 224 degrees|−224°C}}.

> i: neptune | v: neptune
The difference is heat from inside. Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune all give off more energy than they receive from the Sun. They are still warm deep down, still releasing heat left over from their formation. Uranus, by comparison, seems to give off very little. It is as if its inner fire has almost gone out.

> i: uranus interior | v: uranus interior
Why? Perhaps the same collision that knocked it over also let much of its heat escape. Perhaps its insides are layered in a way that traps the heat deep down. Some recent studies suggest it does release a little extra warmth after all, just much less than its neighbours. [pause] It is one of the biggest puzzles in planetary science.

> i: uranus clouds | v: uranus
And its clouds hold another surprise. In 2018, astronomers found that the cloud tops of Uranus contain hydrogen sulphide. [pause] The gas that gives rotten eggs their smell. If you could somehow survive in its freezing air, the first thing you would notice is the stench.

## The Day We Visited
mood: mystery
> i: voyager 2 | v: voyager 2
On the twenty fourth of January 1986, Voyager 2 flew past Uranus. It is still the only spacecraft that has ever been there. The pictures it sent back showed an almost featureless, pale blue-green ball. Calm. Bland.

> i: uranus magnetic field | v: magnetic field
But its instruments found strange things. The magnetic field was tilted by about sixty degrees from the planet's spin axis, and its centre was shifted far away from the middle of the planet. The radiation belts were unexpectedly intense, yet the space around the planet was almost empty of the charged gas scientists expected.

> i: uranus | v: solar wind
For almost forty years, those measurements shaped everything we believed about Uranus. Then, in 2024, scientists went back to the solar wind data from the days before the flyby. They found that Voyager had arrived just after a powerful gust of {{solar wind|SOLAR WIND}} had slammed into the planet, squeezing its magnetic bubble to a fraction of its normal size.

> i: uranus webb | v: uranus
Conditions like that may occur only a few percent of the time. Voyager had visited on an extremely unusual day. [pause] It was as if aliens had studied the Earth for a few hours during a hurricane, and concluded that our planet is always stormy.

## Rings, Moons And A Return
mood: wonder
> i: uranus rings | v: uranus rings
Uranus has rings too. They were discovered in 1977, before Voyager arrived, when astronomers watched a star flicker on and off as it passed behind the thin, dark rings. Today we know of thirteen. In Webb's infrared images, they glow like a halo around the tilted planet.

> i: miranda | v: miranda
And it has nearly thirty moons, many named after characters from Shakespeare. The strangest is {{Miranda|MIRANDA}}. Its surface is a patchwork of grooved, scarred terrain, with cliffs up to about twenty kilometres high, among the tallest in the solar system. It looks as if it was shattered and reassembled.

> i: titania | v: uranus moons
Some of the bigger moons, like Titania and Oberon, might even hide oceans of salty water beneath their ice. If so, Uranus could be home to several worlds where life is at least worth looking for.

> i: uranus | v: spacecraft
That is why a mission to Uranus has been ranked as a top priority for planetary science in the United States. A spacecraft that would orbit the planet for years and drop a probe into its atmosphere. If it launches in the 2030s, it could finally reveal what Voyager missed.

> i: uranus webb | v: uranus rotation
A planet knocked onto its side. Seasons that last for decades. A heart that has gone cold. A single visit, on the wrong day. [pause] Uranus is not a boring planet. It is the solar system's most beautiful unanswered question. And it is still rolling around the Sun, waiting for us to come back.
