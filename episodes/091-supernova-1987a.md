On the night of the twenty third of February 1987, deep underground in a mine in Japan, a giant tank of ultra-pure water recorded something it had never seen before. In thirteen seconds, eleven ghostly particles called neutrinos passed through it.

A few hours later, its light arrived. In Chile, an astronomer named Ian Shelton developed a photograph of a neighbouring galaxy and saw a bright new star that had not been there the night before. He walked outside and looked up. He could see it with his own eyes.

It was the closest exploding star seen since 1604, before the invention of the telescope. Astronomers named it {{SN 1987A|SN 1987A}}. [pause] And for thirty seven years, it hid one of its biggest secrets.

## A Star In A Neighbouring Galaxy
mood: wonder
> i: large magellanic cloud | v: large magellanic cloud
The explosion happened in the {{Large Magellanic Cloud|LARGE MAGELLANIC CLOUD}}, a small galaxy orbiting our own Milky Way, about one hundred and sixty eight thousand light-years away. It is visible from the southern hemisphere as a faint smudge in the sky.

> i: supernova 1987a | v: supernova
So the star actually exploded about one hundred and sixty eight thousand years ago, when our ancestors were living in Africa, before any human had set foot in Europe. Its light had been crossing the dark between galaxies ever since, arriving in February 1987.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
Ian Shelton, working at Las Campanas Observatory, was the first to report it. A telescope operator there, Oscar Duhalde, had also noticed the new star by eye that same night. Within hours, astronomers all over the world were turning their telescopes south.

> i: supernova 1987a | v: supernova explosion
For the first time in the modern era, scientists could study a supernova close up, with every instrument available, from the moment it appeared. [pause] And almost immediately, it broke the rules.

> i: night sky | v: night sky
Over the next three months, the supernova kept brightening, peaking in May at roughly the brightness of the stars of the Plough. Then it slowly faded. Observers across the southern hemisphere could step outside and watch a star from another galaxy blazing in their sky. [pause] Something no telescope-age astronomer had ever seen before.

## The Wrong Kind Of Star
mood: mystery
> i: blue supergiant | v: star
Astronomers could look back at old photographs and identify the star that had exploded. It was called Sanduleak minus sixty nine two hundred and two, a star about twenty times the mass of the Sun. And it was a {{blue supergiant|BLUE SUPERGIANT}}.

> i: red supergiant | v: red supergiant
That was a shock. Theory said that massive stars should explode when they are red supergiants, bloated and cool, like Betelgeuse. Blue supergiants were not supposed to explode at all. Yet here was one, gone.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Scientists have since proposed explanations. Perhaps the star had swallowed a companion, or had a lower content of heavy elements that kept it compact and blue. [pause] Either way, SN 1987A taught astronomers that stars can die in more ways than the textbooks said.

> i: supernova 1987a rings | v: supernova remnant
The strange rings around it, which we will come to, also hint at a dramatic past. Some astronomers think Sanduleak was once two stars that spiralled together and merged, tens of thousands of years before the end. [pause] The merger could explain both its blue colour and the hourglass of gas it left behind.

## Ghosts From A Dying Star
mood: wonder
> i: neutrino detector | v: neutrino detector
The neutrinos were the real prize. When a massive star's core collapses, it releases almost all of its energy as neutrinos, particles so light and so shy that they pass through planets as if they were not there. Around one hundred billion billion of them flowed through every square centimetre of the Earth. The Japanese detector, called Kamiokande, had actually been built to search for something else entirely, the possible decay of protons. It was not designed to catch a supernova. It simply happened to be listening.

> i: neutrino detector | v: neutrino detector
Out of all those trillions, detectors in Japan, the United States and the Soviet Union caught about {{two dozen|~24 NEUTRINOS}}. It was enough. For the first time, scientists had directly detected the death of a star from outside our solar system, by particles that came from its collapsing core.

> i: neutrino detector | v: physics laboratory
The neutrinos arrived around three hours before the light, because they escaped the star immediately, while the light had to wait for the shock wave to reach the surface. They confirmed the theory of how massive stars collapse. In 2002, the Japanese physicist Masatoshi Koshiba shared the Nobel Prize, partly for this detection. [pause] A handful of particles, from a star that died before humans left Africa.

## Rings Of Light
mood: wonder
> i: supernova 1987a rings | v: supernova remnant
A few years later, Hubble revealed something beautiful around the explosion. Three glowing rings of gas. One tight ring around the star's equator, and two larger rings above and below it, like an hourglass.

> i: supernova 1987a rings | v: supernova 1987a
The rings were made of gas the star had shed about twenty thousand years before it exploded. The flash of the supernova lit them up. Then, starting around 2001, the blast wave itself began slamming into the inner ring, making it glow in a string of bright spots astronomers called the {{string of pearls|STRING OF PEARLS}}.

> i: sn 1987a webb | v: supernova remnant
For decades, telescopes have watched the pearls brighten and fade as the debris collides with the ring. It is one of the only places in the universe where we can watch, year by year, how an exploding star spreads into its surroundings. [pause] A star's death, playing out in real time.

> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
SN 1987A also confirmed what powers a supernova's long glow. As it faded, its light dropped at exactly the rate expected from radioactive nickel decaying into cobalt, and cobalt into iron. Telescopes even detected gamma rays from the cobalt. [pause] The iron in your blood was made the same way, in explosions like this one.

## The Missing Neutron Star
mood: mystery
> i: neutron star | v: neutron star
The neutrinos proved that the star's core had collapsed. That should have left behind a {{neutron star|NEUTRON STAR}}, a city-sized ball of neutrons heavier than the Sun. But when astronomers looked for it, they could not find it.

> i: supernova 1987a | v: supernova remnant
For decades, the centre of the explosion stayed dark, hidden behind thick clouds of dust thrown out by the blast. Some scientists wondered whether the core had collapsed further into a black hole. In 2019, radio telescopes spotted a warm blob of dust near the centre, a hint that something was heating it.

> i: webb telescope | v: webb telescope
Then, in 2024, the James Webb Space Telescope looked through the dust in infrared. It found light from argon and other elements, glowing near the centre in a way that requires a powerful source of ultraviolet and X-ray radiation. The best explanation was a {{hidden neutron star|NEUTRON STAR FOUND}}, or the cloud of energetic particles around one.

> i: sn 1987a webb | v: supernova remnant
After thirty seven years of searching, the ghost at the heart of SN 1987A had finally been glimpsed. [pause] And astronomers will keep watching as the dust clears, perhaps one day seeing the neutron star itself, born on a February night that humanity happened to witness.
