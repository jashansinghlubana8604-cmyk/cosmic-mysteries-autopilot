Space is cold. Very cold. Far from any star, the temperature of the universe is just under three degrees above {{absolute zero|ABSOLUTE ZERO}}, the coldest temperature anything can ever reach.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
That chill is set by the faint glow left over from the Big Bang, which fills all of space. Everything in the universe sits in that glow, and nothing in nature is expected to be colder.

> i: boomerang nebula | v: nebula
And yet, about five thousand light-years away, in the constellation Centaurus, there is a cloud of gas that is colder than the empty space around it. [pause] Just {{one degree|1 DEGREE}} above absolute zero.

> i: boomerang nebula hubble | v: nebula zoom
It is called the Boomerang Nebula. It is the coldest natural place ever found in the universe. [pause] And it is cold because a star is dying in a very strange way.

## How Cold Can It Get?
mood: wonder
> i: ice | v: ice crystals
Temperature is really a measure of motion. In anything warm, atoms and molecules jiggle and fly about. The colder something gets, the slower they move.

> i: ice crystals | v: frost
At {{minus two hundred and seventy three point one five degrees Celsius|-273.15°C}}, that motion would reach its absolute minimum. That is absolute zero. Nothing can be colder, and in fact, the laws of physics say it can never quite be reached.

> i: thermometer | v: ice
Scientists measure these temperatures in kelvin, a scale that starts at absolute zero. Water freezes at about two hundred and seventy three kelvin. A warm summer day is around three hundred.

> i: ice | v: ice crystals
The scale is named after the Scottish physicist William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, who in 1848 worked out that there must be a lowest possible temperature. [pause] Long before anyone could get close to it, he calculated where it lay.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: cosmic microwave background
The afterglow of the Big Bang, the cosmic microwave background, has a temperature of about {{2.7 kelvin|2.7 K}}. It reaches every corner of the universe. [pause] Any object left alone in deep space, far from stars, will slowly cool until it matches that temperature, and then stop.

## The Cold Places We Know
mood: mystery
> i: antarctica | v: antarctica
On Earth, the coldest temperature ever measured by a thermometer was recorded at the Russian Vostok Station in Antarctica in 1983: minus eighty nine point two degrees Celsius. Satellites have since detected pockets of snow on the high East Antarctic plateau that reach close to minus one hundred.

> i: moon crater | v: moon surface
Our Moon is far colder. At its poles, some craters never see sunlight. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter measured temperatures there of around {{minus two hundred and forty|-240°C}}, among the coldest places ever measured in the solar system.

> i: pluto | v: pluto
Further out, Pluto and Neptune's moon Triton are frozen at around minus two hundred and thirty degrees. [pause] Cold enough to freeze nitrogen, the gas that makes up most of the air you breathe, into solid ice.

> i: star field | v: star field
But all these places are warmed, at least a little, by the Sun or by starlight. Nothing in nature should fall below the temperature of the Big Bang's afterglow. [pause] So when astronomers found something colder, they were baffled.

## The Bow Tie In The Sky
mood: wonder
> i: boomerang nebula | v: nebula
In 1980, astronomers using a telescope in Australia spotted a small, lopsided cloud around a star. Its two lobes looked slightly curved, so they named it the {{Boomerang Nebula|BOOMERANG NEBULA}}.

> i: boomerang nebula hubble | v: nebula zoom
Later images from the Hubble Space Telescope showed its real shape: more like a bow tie or an hourglass, two lobes of gas and dust lit by a central star, with a dark band across the middle.

> i: radio telescope | v: radio telescope
In 1995, two astronomers, Raghvendra Sahai and Lars-Åke Nyman, measured radio waves from carbon monoxide molecules in the nebula. They expected the gas to absorb nothing from the cosmic background glow behind it. Instead, it absorbed it. [pause] That could only happen if the gas was colder than the glow itself.

> i: boomerang nebula | v: nebula
Their measurements put the temperature at around {{one kelvin|1 KELVIN}}. Colder than outer space, colder than any natural object ever measured. [pause] Somehow, this cloud was cooling itself below the temperature of the universe.

> i: boomerang nebula hubble | v: nebula
The nebula is so cold that its outer gas does not glow the way most clouds do. Instead, it shows up as a dark shadow against the faint background glow of the universe. [pause] It is visible because it is colder than everything behind it.

## A Cosmic Refrigerator
mood: mystery
> i: red giant star | v: red giant star
The answer lies in how fast the dying star is throwing off its gas. Near the end of their lives, stars like the Sun swell into red giants and blow away their outer layers.

> i: nebula expanding | v: star shedding layers
But the star at the centre of the Boomerang is doing it astonishingly fast. Gas is rushing away at about one hundred and sixty four kilometres per second, and the star is losing mass far more quickly than a normal dying star.

> i: aerosol spray | v: gas expanding
When gas expands quickly, it cools. You can feel it when you use a spray can: the can gets cold as the gas inside rushes out. Refrigerators work in a similar way. [pause] In the Boomerang, the gas is expanding so fast and so freely that it cools faster than the faint Big Bang glow can warm it back up.

> i: boomerang nebula hubble | v: nebula zoom
In 2013, astronomers used the ALMA telescope array in Chile to study the Boomerang in more detail. They found that its cold outflow is far bigger than the bright hourglass seen by Hubble, stretching out in a roughly round shape. [pause] And inside it, a dense band of dust is shaping the central lobes.

> i: binary star | v: binary star
Why is the star losing mass so quickly? One leading idea is that it once had a companion star that spiralled into it and merged with it. Such a merger could release enormous amounts of energy and drive the gas out at tremendous speed. [pause] The coldest place in the universe may be the wreckage of two stars becoming one.

## Colder Than Nature
mood: epic
> i: boomerang nebula | v: nebula
This freezing phase will not last. In a few thousand years, the central star will heat up and its light will begin to warm the gas. The Boomerang will slowly become a normal planetary nebula. [pause] We happen to be seeing it during a brief, extraordinary moment.

> i: james webb telescope | v: james webb telescope
Humans have also learned to make things very cold. The James Webb Space Telescope keeps one of its instruments below seven kelvin, colder than Pluto, so that its own heat does not blind it to faint infrared light.

> i: international space station | v: international space station
But our laboratories go much further. On the International Space Station, NASA's Cold Atom Lab has cooled clouds of atoms to less than a ten millionth of a degree above absolute zero. On Earth, physicists have briefly reached a few trillionths of a degree. [pause] At those temperatures, atoms stop behaving like separate particles and merge into a single strange quantum state.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotating
So the coldest place in the known universe is not in a nebula at all. It is in human laboratories, on a warm blue planet. [pause] The Boomerang remains the coldest place that nature has made on its own.

> i: boomerang nebula hubble | v: nebula zoom
A dying star, cooling its own breath below the temperature of the universe. It reminds us that even the background glow of creation is not the coldest thing there is. [pause] Space can still surprise us with how cold it can be.
