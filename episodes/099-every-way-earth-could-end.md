## Rocks From The Sky
mood: dread
> i: asteroid | v: asteroid
The most likely cosmic catastrophe is an impact. The solar system is full of asteroids and comets, and the Earth has been hit many times. Sixty six million years ago, a rock about ten kilometres wide ended the age of the dinosaurs.

> i: asteroid impact | v: asteroid impact
Impacts that big are rare, perhaps once every hundred million years or so. Smaller ones, enough to destroy a city or a region, happen more often. In 1908, an object perhaps fifty metres across exploded over Tunguska in Siberia, flattening around two thousand square kilometres of forest.

> i: telescope night | v: telescope dome
The good news is that this is the one cosmic threat we can do something about. Astronomers have now found the vast majority of asteroids big enough to end civilisation, and none of them is on a collision course for the foreseeable future. And in 2022, NASA's DART mission proved we can push an asteroid off course by crashing a spacecraft into it. [pause] For the first time in Earth's history, a species is learning to defend its planet.

> i: comet | v: comet
Comets are harder. A long-period comet, falling in from the far edge of the solar system, might be spotted only a year or two before it arrived. That would not leave much time. [pause] Which is why astronomers keep watching the sky every night.

## Death From The Stars
mood: dread
> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Further out, there are the stars. When a massive star explodes as a supernova, it showers its surroundings with radiation. If one went off within about fifty light-years of us, it could damage the ozone layer that protects life from the Sun's ultraviolet light.

> i: star field | v: star field
The good news is that there are no stars that close that are about to explode. Betelgeuse, the famous red supergiant in Orion, will explode one day, but it is more than ten times too far away to hurt us. [pause] We would get a spectacular show, nothing more.

> i: gamma ray burst | v: gamma ray burst
Gamma-ray bursts are more dangerous, because they focus their energy into narrow beams. A burst pointed at the Earth from within a few thousand light-years could strip much of the ozone layer. Some scientists have suggested one may have contributed to a mass extinction hundreds of millions of years ago, though that is unproven. [pause] Thankfully, these bursts are rare in galaxies like ours.

> i: rogue star | v: star field
And then there are wandering stars. Stars pass near the solar system from time to time. In about one point three million years, a star called {{Gliese 710|GLIESE 710}} is expected to pass through the outer edges of the solar system, disturbing the distant comets and possibly sending a shower of them inward. [pause] That would raise the risk of impacts for a while, far in the future.

## Our Own Star
mood: dread
> i: solar flare | v: solar flare
Closer to home, the Sun itself is a danger, but mostly to our technology. A giant solar storm, like the Carrington Event of 1859, could cripple power grids and satellites. That would be a disaster for civilisation. But it would not end life on Earth.

> i: sun | v: sun
The real threat from the Sun is slow. As it ages, it is getting brighter, by about one percent every hundred million years. In roughly a billion years, it will be bright enough to start boiling the oceans away. [pause] That is the most likely end of life on Earth. Not a bang. A slow heating.

> i: sun red giant earth | v: red giant
And in about five billion years, the Sun will swell into a {{red giant|RED GIANT}}, hundreds of times its current size. Mercury and Venus will be swallowed. The Earth may be swallowed too, or left as a scorched rock skimming the Sun's edge. [pause] Either way, that is the end of the planet as we know it.

## The Galaxy
mood: mystery
> i: rogue black hole | v: black hole
Our galaxy may contain around a hundred million black holes, most of them invisible. If one passed through the solar system, it would not need to swallow the Earth to destroy it. Its gravity alone could fling our planet out of its orbit, sending it into the freezing dark.

> i: rogue planet | v: rogue planet
The same could happen with a rogue planet, a world wandering between the stars without a sun. A close pass could disturb the Earth's orbit. The chances of either are tiny, but over billions of years, the galaxy is a busy place.

> i: andromeda milky way collision | v: galaxy collision
And what about the Andromeda galaxy, heading towards us? Even if the two galaxies collide in billions of years, the stars are so far apart that the Earth would almost certainly not be hit by anything. [pause] The night sky would change. The Earth itself would probably carry on.

> i: volcano | v: volcano
One honourable mention does not come from space at all. The Earth has its own weapons. Around seventy four thousand years ago, the Toba supervolcano in Indonesia erupted, one of the largest eruptions of the last few million years. Supervolcanoes could cool the climate for years and devastate harvests. [pause] They would not destroy the planet, but they remind us the threats are not only above our heads.

## The Laws Of Physics
mood: dread
> i: vacuum decay | v: expanding universe
Finally, there are endings built into the universe itself. Some physicists think the universe may be in a slightly unstable state, a false vacuum. If a bubble of a more stable state appeared somewhere, it could spread at the speed of light, rewriting the laws of physics and destroying everything in its path. [pause] We would never see it coming. But scientists think it is extremely unlikely to happen for an unimaginably long time, if ever.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: expanding universe
And in the far, far future, the expansion of the universe, driven by dark energy, will slowly isolate galaxies, burn out the last stars, and leave a cold, dark cosmos. Long before then, the Earth will be gone.

> i: particle collision | v: particle collision
Some theories even suggest that matter itself is not permanent. Protons, the particles at the heart of every atom, might decay over timescales far longer than the current age of the universe, by trillions upon trillions of times. Experiments have searched for it and never seen it happen. [pause] If it does, then in the unimaginably distant future, even the rock of the Earth would slowly dissolve away.

> i: earth from space | v: earth rotation
So here is the honest summary. The most likely threats on human timescales are the ones we can see coming and, increasingly, the ones we can stop. The ones we cannot stop are millions or billions of years away. [pause] The Earth has a long future. The real question is whether we will be wise enough to be here for it.
