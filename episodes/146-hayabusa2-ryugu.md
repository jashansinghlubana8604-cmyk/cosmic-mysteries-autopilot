## A Mission That Nearly Died
mood: dread
> i: itokawa asteroid | v: spacecraft asteroid
The story starts with an earlier Japanese mission. In 2003, the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, {{JAXA|JAXA}}, launched a spacecraft called {{Hayabusa|HAYABUSA}}, meaning peregrine falcon. Its target was a small asteroid called Itokawa, shaped like a peanut.

> i: itokawa asteroid | v: asteroid
Almost everything went wrong. A small lander it released missed the asteroid and drifted away into space. Its sampling device did not fire as planned. Fuel leaked, contact was lost for weeks, and three of its four ion engines failed.

> i: ion engine | v: spacecraft
Engineers refused to give up. They combined the working parts of two broken engines into one, and nursed the crippled spacecraft home three years late. [pause] In June 2010, its capsule landed in Australia.

> i: sample return capsule | v: capsule reentry
Inside, scientists found around fifteen hundred tiny grains of asteroid dust, kicked up when the spacecraft touched the surface. [pause] It was the first time anyone had brought material back from an asteroid. And it proved it could be done.

## Hayabusa2 At Ryugu
mood: wonder
> i: ryugu asteroid | v: asteroid rotating
Japan's second mission, {{Hayabusa2|HAYABUSA2}}, launched in December 2014 and reached its asteroid in June 2018. Ryugu, named after an underwater palace in a Japanese folk tale, is about nine hundred metres wide.

> i: ryugu surface | v: asteroid surface
Close up, it was a shock. Scientists had hoped for smooth, sandy patches to land on. Instead, Ryugu was covered in boulders, some as big as buildings. [pause] It is a rubble pile, a loose heap of rocks held together by weak gravity, with very little solid ground.

> i: minerva rover | v: asteroid surface
In September 2018, Hayabusa2 dropped two tiny rovers onto the surface. With so little gravity, wheels would be useless, so they moved by hopping, using spinning motors to bounce slowly across the rocks. [pause] They became the first rovers ever to move on an asteroid, and sent back photos from the surface.

> i: asteroid surface | v: asteroid surface
A small European lander called {{MASCOT|MASCOT}} followed, studying the surface for about seventeen hours before its battery ran out. Then the mission team spent months searching for a safe place to touch down, between boulders taller than the spacecraft itself.

## The Bullet And The Grab
mood: epic
> i: ryugu touchdown | v: spacecraft landing
In February 2019, Hayabusa2 descended and touched Ryugu for just a moment. As it did, it fired a small metal bullet into the ground, kicking up material into a collecting horn. Then it climbed away. The touchdown was so precise that it landed within about a metre of its target.

> i: asteroid impact | v: asteroid impact
In April came the impactor. The explosion carved a crater roughly {{fourteen metres|14 METRES}} across, far bigger than expected, because the asteroid's surface had almost no strength. [pause] It showed that Ryugu's surface could be much younger than scientists thought.

> i: asteroid impact | v: asteroid impact
To watch it happen, Hayabusa2 had released a small camera before escaping. Floating alone above the asteroid, the camera filmed a fan of debris spraying upwards from the surface, like a shower of dark sand, as the copper plate struck. [pause] It was the first time anyone had watched an impact experiment on another world from up close.

> i: ryugu asteroid | v: asteroid
In July 2019, Hayabusa2 touched down a second time, right beside the crater, to collect material thrown out from beneath the surface. [pause] It was the first time any mission had collected rock from inside an asteroid.

> i: sample return capsule | v: capsule reentry
On the sixth of December 2020, the capsule streaked through the night sky over Woomera in Australia and landed by parachute. Inside were about {{five point four grams|5.4 GRAMS}} of asteroid, more than fifty times the mission's minimum goal. [pause] The spacecraft itself never landed. It fired its engines and set off on an extended mission to visit two more asteroids in the 2020s and 2030s.

## OSIRIS-REx At Bennu
mood: mystery
> i: bennu asteroid | v: bennu asteroid
Meanwhile, NASA had sent its own mission. {{OSIRIS-REx|OSIRIS-REx}} launched in 2016 and arrived at the asteroid Bennu in December 2018. Bennu is a little smaller than Ryugu, but looks remarkably similar: a dark spinning top, covered in boulders.

> i: bennu surface | v: asteroid surface
Bennu had another surprise. Shortly after arrival, the spacecraft saw small pebbles being flung off the surface into space, then falling back. [pause] The asteroid was active, shedding bits of itself.

> i: bennu touchdown | v: spacecraft asteroid
In October 2020, OSIRIS-REx reached down with a robotic arm to touch the surface for a few seconds. Instead of meeting solid ground, the sampler sank straight in, as if into a children's pit of plastic balls. Had the spacecraft not fired its thrusters to back away, it might have kept sinking. [pause] Bennu's surface was barely held together at all.

> i: bennu touchdown | v: spacecraft asteroid
The sampler worked like a reverse vacuum cleaner. A burst of nitrogen gas blew loose rock and dust up into a collecting chamber at the end of the arm. The site chosen, a crater named Nightingale, was one of the few places on Bennu clear enough of large boulders, and the clear patch the spacecraft aimed for was only about eight metres wide. [pause] The spacecraft had to navigate down to it on its own, using maps it had made of the surface.

> i: bennu asteroid | v: asteroid
The collector was so full that rocks jammed its lid open, and particles began drifting away into space. Engineers hurried to stow it. In September 2023, the capsule landed in the Utah desert with about {{one hundred and twenty grams|121 GRAMS}} of asteroid, the largest sample ever brought back. Even then, two stubborn fasteners kept the container partly closed for months, until engineers designed new tools to open it.

## Why It Matters
mood: wonder
> i: asteroid sample | v: laboratory
These are some of the purest samples of the early solar system ever studied. Meteorites that fall to Earth are scorched as they enter the atmosphere and contaminated once they land. These grains were sealed away from the Earth's air and handled in clean rooms.

> i: asteroid sample | v: microscope
Inside them, scientists found clays and minerals formed in water, organic molecules, and many of the chemical building blocks used by life. [pause] Rocks that had waited four and a half billion years were finally telling their story.

> i: bennu asteroid | v: asteroid
There is a practical side too. Bennu is one of the most potentially hazardous asteroids known, with about a one in two thousand seven hundred chance of hitting Earth in the year 2182. Knowing how loose its surface is helps us plan how we might one day push such an object away.

> i: apophis asteroid | v: asteroid flyby
And the journeys continue. The OSIRIS-REx spacecraft has been renamed OSIRIS-APEX and is on its way to meet the asteroid Apophis after its close pass by Earth in 2029. Hayabusa2 is heading for a small, fast-spinning asteroid in 2031. [pause] We have learned to visit asteroids, touch them, shoot them and bring them home. And every one we visit surprises us.
