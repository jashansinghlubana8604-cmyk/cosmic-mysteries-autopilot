## Monsters With Jets
mood: epic
> i: active galactic nucleus | v: black hole accretion disk
At the centre of most large galaxies sits a supermassive black hole, millions or billions of times the mass of the Sun. Most of the time, they are quiet. But when gas falls in, it forms a swirling disc that heats up and blazes with light.

> i: black hole accretion disk | v: black hole accretion disk
Some of these feeding black holes do something remarkable. Powerful magnetic fields, twisted by the black hole's spin, fling a small fraction of the matter outwards in two narrow beams, shooting from the poles in opposite directions. [pause] These are {{relativistic jets|JETS}}, moving at more than ninety nine percent of the speed of light.

> i: m87 jet | v: black hole jet
The jets can stretch for thousands, even millions of light-years, far beyond the galaxy that launched them. The galaxy Messier 87, whose black hole was the first ever photographed, has a jet about five thousand light-years long. [pause] But its jet points off to the side, so we see it in profile.

> i: radio galaxy | v: radio galaxy
When we see jets from the side, the galaxy is called a radio galaxy, with huge glowing lobes where the jets plough into the gas around them. When we see the black hole's glowing disc more directly, we call it a quasar. [pause] But if a jet happens to point almost straight at us, everything changes.

## Looking Down The Barrel
mood: dread
> i: blazar | v: blazar
A jet moving at nearly the speed of light, aimed towards us, is boosted enormously. Its light is concentrated in our direction and squeezed to higher energies. [pause] The result can appear hundreds or even thousands of times brighter than the same jet seen from the side.

> i: blazar | v: black hole jet
That is why blazars outshine the entire galaxy around them. They emit across the whole spectrum, from radio waves to the most powerful gamma rays. Most of the sources of high-energy gamma rays that NASA's {{Fermi|FERMI}} telescope has found beyond our galaxy are blazars.

> i: blazar | v: blazar
They also flicker. A blazar can brighten or fade dramatically within days, hours or even minutes. Because light can only cross a region so quickly, that tells astronomers the flaring source must be incredibly compact, sometimes not much bigger than our solar system. [pause] A region that small, outshining a galaxy of hundreds of billions of stars.

> i: star field | v: star twinkling
Their wild variability fooled astronomers for decades. In 1929, a German astronomer recorded what he thought was a strange variable star in the constellation Lacerta and named it {{BL Lacertae|BL LACERTAE}}. [pause] Only in the late 1960s did astronomers realise it was not a star at all, but the blazing core of a distant galaxy, millions of times farther away.

## Faster Than Light?
mood: mystery
> i: radio telescope array | v: radio telescope
In the early 1970s, radio astronomers linking telescopes across the world noticed something even stranger. Inside some of these jets, bright blobs seemed to be racing outwards at several times the {{speed of light|FASTER THAN LIGHT?}}.

> i: black hole jet | v: black hole jet
It is an illusion. The blobs are moving at almost the speed of light, nearly towards us. As they travel, they almost catch up with the light they emitted earlier. [pause] So the light from later positions arrives soon after the light from earlier positions, making the motion across the sky look impossibly fast.

> i: blazar | v: blazar
This effect, called {{superluminal motion|SUPERLUMINAL}}, became powerful evidence that the jets really do move at close to the speed of light. [pause] Nature was creating optical illusions on the scale of light-years.

> i: x-ray telescope | v: x-ray telescope
Even today, exactly how the jets accelerate particles to such energies is still being studied. In 2022, an X-ray observatory called IXPE measured the polarisation of light from a nearby blazar called Markarian 501. It found evidence that particles are boosted by shock waves travelling along the jet. [pause] But the details are still a puzzle.

## The Ghost Particle
mood: mystery
> i: icecube neutrino | v: antarctica ice
Now back to the South Pole. The particle that arrived in September 2017 was a {{neutrino|NEUTRINO}}, a ghostly particle that barely interacts with anything. Trillions pass through your body every second without touching a single atom.

> i: icecube observatory | v: antarctica
To catch the rare ones that do interact, scientists built {{IceCube|ICECUBE}}, a detector made from a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice, threaded with more than five thousand light sensors buried up to two and a half kilometres deep. When a neutrino strikes an atom in the ice, it makes a faint blue flash.

> i: blazar | v: blazar
The neutrino of 2017 pointed back to a blazar called TXS 0506+056. Gamma-ray telescopes found it in a bright flare. When IceCube searched its older data, it found an earlier burst of neutrinos from the same direction, in 2014 and 2015. [pause] It was the first strong evidence for a source of the highest-energy neutrinos in the universe.

> i: cosmic rays | v: particle collision
That matters because neutrinos are made alongside {{cosmic rays|COSMIC RAYS}}, charged particles from space that have puzzled scientists for more than a century. For decades, nobody knew where the most energetic ones came from. [pause] Blazars are now strong suspects for at least some of them.

> i: icecube neutrino | v: particle collision
The energy of that single neutrino was around three hundred trillion electron volts, about twenty times more than the collisions in the Large Hadron Collider, the most powerful machine humans have built. [pause] One invisible particle, fired from a black hole four billion years ago, carried that energy all the way to Antarctica.

## Are We In Danger?
mood: epic
> i: galaxy | v: galaxy
It may sound alarming to be in the firing line of a black hole's jet. But even the nearest blazars, such as Markarian 421 and Markarian 501, are hundreds of millions of light-years away. [pause] Their beams are spread across such enormous distances that they pose no threat to life on Earth.

> i: deep field | v: hubble deep field
Instead, they are tools. Because they are so bright, blazars can be seen across most of the universe. Their light, crossing billions of light-years, is slightly absorbed by the faint glow of all the starlight ever emitted, helping astronomers measure the history of star formation.

> i: blazar | v: black hole jet
In 2024, astronomers announced the most distant blazar yet found. Its light left it when the universe was less than a billion years old. [pause] Even then, black holes were already launching jets aimed across the cosmos.

> i: active galactic nucleus | v: black hole accretion disk
Somewhere in that faraway galaxy, a black hole is turning matter into a beam that crosses the universe. By pure chance, the Earth is sitting in its path. [pause] And when a single ghostly particle from it struck the ice at the bottom of our world, it opened a new window, letting us study the universe not just with light, but with the particles it fires at us.
