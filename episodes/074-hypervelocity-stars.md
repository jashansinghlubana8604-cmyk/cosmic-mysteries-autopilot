Most stars in our galaxy move together, in a slow, graceful orbit around the centre of the Milky Way. Our Sun takes about two hundred and thirty million years to go around once. Calm. Predictable.

> i: hypervelocity star | v: star
But some stars are not orbiting at all. They are fleeing. Racing outward at millions of kilometres an hour, so fast that the gravity of the entire galaxy cannot hold them back.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
One of them is moving at about {{six million kilometres an hour|6 MILLION KM/H}}. At that speed, you could travel from the Earth to the Moon in about four minutes. [pause] And it is never coming back.

> i: milky way center | v: milky way center
Where do these runaway stars come from? Something must have thrown them. And when astronomers traced their paths backwards, many of them pointed to the same place. [pause] The supermassive black hole at the centre of our galaxy.

## The Slingshot
mood: mystery
> i: binary star | v: binary star
In 1988, an astronomer named {{Jack Hills|JACK HILLS}} worked out what would happen if a pair of stars, orbiting each other, wandered too close to a supermassive black hole.

> i: black hole | v: black hole
The black hole's gravity would tear the pair apart. One star would be captured into a tight orbit around the black hole. And the other would be flung away at tremendous speed, carrying off the energy of the broken partnership.

> i: black hole artist impression | v: stars orbiting black hole
Hills calculated that the ejected star could leave at more than a thousand kilometres per second. Fast enough to escape the whole Milky Way. [pause] He predicted that, somewhere in our galaxy, there should be stars racing outward from the centre, on paths leading straight back to the black hole.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
For seventeen years, nobody found one. Spotting a single fast-moving star among hundreds of billions is extraordinarily difficult. [pause] Then, in 2005, astronomers got lucky.

## The First Escapee
mood: wonder
> i: telescope dome | v: telescope night
In 2005, a team at the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, led by Warren Brown, was studying faint blue stars in the outer halo of the Milky Way. One of them was moving away from us at more than seven hundred kilometres per second.

> i: hypervelocity star | v: star
That was far too fast for a normal star. Too fast for the galaxy to hold onto. Its path pointed back towards the galactic centre. It was the first {{hypervelocity star|HYPERVELOCITY STAR}} ever found, exactly as Hills had predicted.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
To put that in perspective, our Sun is carried around the galaxy at about two hundred and thirty kilometres per second. Near our part of the Milky Way, a star needs roughly five hundred to six hundred kilometres per second to escape the galaxy's pull completely. [pause] Anything faster is on a one-way trip.

> i: gaia spacecraft | v: gaia
Since then, dozens more have been discovered. The European Space Agency's Gaia mission, which has measured the motions of more than a billion stars with exquisite precision, has made it possible to trace their paths across the sky and work out where they came from. [pause] One star at a time, astronomers are mapping the galaxy's escapees.

## The Fastest Of All
mood: wonder
> i: grus constellation | v: night sky
In 2019, a team led by Sergey Koposov announced the most dramatic one yet. A star called {{S5-HVS1|S5-HVS1}}, in the southern constellation Grus, the crane. It was racing through space at about one thousand seven hundred and fifty kilometres per second.

> i: milky way center | v: milky way center
That is around six million kilometres per hour. Tracing its path backwards, the team found it had come from the centre of the Milky Way. It was ejected by Sagittarius A*, our supermassive black hole, about five million years ago.

> i: early humans | v: earth
Five million years ago, on Earth, the ancestors of humans were just beginning to walk upright in Africa. At that moment, at the heart of the galaxy, a black hole broke apart a pair of stars, captured one and threw the other out. [pause] It has been racing towards the edge of the galaxy ever since.

> i: sagittarius a star orbits | v: stars orbiting black hole
And its partner? It may still be there. Around Sagittarius A*, astronomers have tracked a swarm of young stars on tight, fast orbits, the so-called S-stars. Some scientists think many of them are the captured halves of broken pairs, held close to the black hole while their companions were thrown away. [pause] Each runaway star may have a twin, still circling the monster that separated them.

> i: hypervelocity star | v: deep space flight
S5-HVS1 is now about twenty nine thousand light-years away. In about a hundred million years, it will have left the Milky Way altogether, heading out into intergalactic space. [pause] A star that will spend the rest of its life in the dark between the galaxies.

## Other Ways To Run
mood: mystery
> i: supernova | v: supernova explosion
Black holes are not the only slingshots. When a star in a close pair explodes as a supernova, its partner can suddenly be released, flying off at the speed it was orbiting. These are called runaway stars.

> i: white dwarf | v: white dwarf
And some of the fastest objects of all are the remains of exploding stars themselves. In 2018, using Gaia data, astronomers found white dwarfs moving at more than a thousand kilometres per second. They are thought to be survivors of a particular kind of supernova, where one white dwarf blew up and flung its companion away like a stone from a sling.

> i: white dwarf | v: star field
Some of these zombie stars, partly burned and scorched, are hurtling through the galaxy at speeds of two thousand kilometres per second or more. [pause] Shrapnel from a cosmic explosion, still flying.

> i: hypervelocity star | v: star
One of the most famous is a small, hot star called US 708, moving at around twelve hundred kilometres per second. It seems to have been the companion of a white dwarf that exploded, and the blast flung it out at a speed no black hole was needed to explain. [pause] The galaxy is full of things that were in the wrong place when something blew up.

## A Hidden Black Hole Next Door
mood: mystery
> i: large magellanic cloud | v: large magellanic cloud
In 2025, astronomers made a surprising discovery about some of the hypervelocity stars. When they traced their paths carefully, not all of them led back to our galaxy's centre. Some seemed to come from the {{Large Magellanic Cloud|LARGE MAGELLANIC CLOUD}}, a small satellite galaxy orbiting the Milky Way.

> i: large magellanic cloud | v: galaxy
That would mean the Large Magellanic Cloud has its own big black hole, capable of slinging stars out. The team estimated it at around six hundred thousand times the mass of the Sun. [pause] A black hole nobody had ever seen, revealed only by the stars it threw away.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
Hypervelocity stars are not just curiosities. Because their paths pass through the whole galaxy, they are bent slightly by everything along the way, including the invisible dark matter halo surrounding the Milky Way. By measuring their paths, astronomers can weigh our galaxy, and map the shape of that unseen halo.

> i: star field | v: deep space flight
Imagine living on a planet orbiting one of these stars, if any have planets at all. Year by year, the night sky would change. The bright band of the Milky Way would shrink and fade behind you, until one day, your sky would hold only a few distant smudges of other galaxies. [pause] A world thrown out of its home, by a black hole, at six million kilometres an hour.
