A black hole is the simplest object in the universe. Just a region of space where gravity has become so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.

> v: black hole simulation | i: black hole
And yet they come in an astonishing range of sizes. Some could be smaller than an atom. Others are larger than our entire solar system.

> i: black holes merging | v: black hole merger
Some sit quietly for billions of years, completely invisible. Others blaze brighter than whole galaxies. Some spin at nearly the speed of light. Some collide, and shake the very fabric of space.

## The Ones That May Not Exist
mood: mystery
> i: early universe | v: early universe
We start with the smallest, and the most speculative. In the first fraction of a second after the Big Bang, the universe was unimaginably dense. Some physicists think that in places, matter was packed so tightly that it collapsed directly into black holes.

> v: particle physics | i: big bang
These would be {{primordial black holes|PRIMORDIAL BLACK HOLES}}. Unlike every other kind, they would not need a star to die. Some could have the mass of a mountain, packed into a space smaller than an atom. Others could be far larger.

> i: hawking radiation | v: black hole evaporating
In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that black holes slowly leak energy and shrink. The smaller a black hole, the faster it evaporates. The tiniest primordial black holes would have already vanished, ending in a final flash of energy. Astronomers have searched for those flashes, so far without success.

> v: dark matter simulation | i: dark matter
Some scientists have even suggested that primordial black holes could make up part of the mysterious dark matter that holds galaxies together. It is an idea that is still being tested, and one that has not yet been proven or ruled out.

## The Dead Stars
mood: wonder
> v: supernova explosion | i: supernova
The most common black holes are born when giant stars die. When a star many times heavier than the Sun runs out of fuel, its core collapses in less than a second. Its outer layers may explode as a supernova. What is left behind can be a {{stellar black hole|STELLAR BLACK HOLE}}, a few to a few dozen times the mass of the Sun.

> i: cygnus x-1 | v: x-ray binary
The first one ever identified was Cygnus X-1, about seven thousand light years away. In the 1960s and 70s, astronomers noticed it blasting out X-rays, and found a giant blue star orbiting an invisible partner far too heavy to be anything else.

> v: x-ray binary black hole | i: black hole stealing gas
Many stellar black holes are found this way, in pairs with living stars. They pull gas from their companion, which swirls into a hot disc and glows brightly in X-rays.

> i: gaia bh3 | v: black hole binary
But most of them are probably completely dark, alone or with a partner they are not feeding on. In 2024, the Gaia spacecraft found one simply by noticing a star wobbling around nothing. Called {{Gaia BH3|GAIA BH3}}, it is about thirty three times the mass of the Sun and less than two thousand light years away. There may be a hundred million black holes like this, scattered through our galaxy, completely unseen.

> v: neutron star | i: neutron star
Not every dying star makes a black hole. If the collapsing core is lighter than roughly two to three times the mass of the Sun, it stops just short, as a neutron star. Heavier than that, and nothing in physics can hold it up. Exactly where that line falls is still uncertain, and astronomers have found a few mysterious objects sitting right in that gap, too heavy to be neutron stars, perhaps too light to be ordinary black holes.

## Collisions In The Dark
mood: wonder
> v: black hole merger | i: black holes merging
Sometimes, two black holes orbit each other. Over millions of years they spiral closer and closer, until in the final second they collide at a large fraction of the speed of light.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational waves
On the 14th of September 2015, the LIGO detectors in the United States felt the result. A ripple in spacetime itself, a {{gravitational wave|GRAVITATIONAL WAVE}}, had passed through the Earth, stretching and squeezing it by less than the width of a proton.

> v: black hole merger simulation | i: black hole merger
It came from two black holes, about thirty six and twenty nine times the mass of the Sun, merging one point three billion years ago. In a fraction of a second, they released energy equal to about three Suns, more power than all the stars in the observable universe combined, for that brief moment.

> i: gravitational waves | v: gravitational wave simulation
Since then, detectors have heard hundreds of these collisions. A whole new way of observing the universe was born, not with light, but with the trembling of space itself.

> i: black hole | v: black hole simulation
Whatever their size, black holes are strangely simple. According to general relativity, a black hole can be completely described by just three numbers: its mass, its spin and its electric charge. Everything else about whatever fell in, every star, every planet, every atom, is hidden from the outside universe forever. Physicists say that black holes have no hair.

## The Missing Middle
mood: mystery
> v: globular cluster | i: omega centauri
For decades, there was a strange gap. We found black holes a few to a hundred times the Sun's mass, and giants of millions or billions. But almost nothing in between. These missing {{intermediate mass black holes|THE MISSING MIDDLE}} became one of the great hunts in astronomy.

> i: omega centauri | v: omega centauri zoom
In 2024, astronomers studied twenty years of Hubble images of Omega Centauri, a dense ball of about ten million stars. In its core, they found seven stars moving too fast. Something invisible, at least eight thousand times the mass of the Sun, had to be holding them there.

> v: star cluster | i: globular cluster
It was some of the best evidence yet for an intermediate black hole. These middle sized black holes may be the missing link, the seeds from which the giants grew.

## The Giants
mood: wonder
> v: milky way centre zoom | i: sagittarius a star
At the centre of almost every large galaxy sits a {{supermassive black hole|SUPERMASSIVE BLACK HOLE}}. Ours, Sagittarius A star, weighs about four million Suns. In 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope revealed its shadow, a dark centre in a ring of glowing gas.

> i: m87 black hole | v: m87 jet
The black hole in the galaxy M87 is far bigger, six and a half billion Suns. It launches a jet of particles moving at nearly the speed of light, stretching thousands of light years into space.

> v: quasar | i: quasar artist impression
When giant black holes feed quickly, they become quasars, the brightest steady lights in the universe. And the very largest, like TON 618, may weigh tens of billions of Suns. These are sometimes called {{ultramassive black holes|ULTRAMASSIVE}}.

> i: spinning black hole | v: black hole spinning
Almost all black holes also spin, some at close to the maximum speed nature allows. A spinning black hole drags space itself around with it, like a whirlpool. Some physicists have even imagined using this to extract energy from a black hole, an idea first described by Roger Penrose.

> v: zoom out galaxy | i: galaxy
From invisible specks born in the Big Bang, to dead stars in silent pairs, to the monsters at the hearts of galaxies, black holes may be the most extreme things in existence. [pause] And they may also be the last things to remain, long after every star in the universe has gone dark.
