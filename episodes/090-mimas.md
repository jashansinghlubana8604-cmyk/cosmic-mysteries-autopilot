## The Death Star
mood: wonder
> i: mimas | v: mimas
Mimas was discovered in 1789 by William Herschel, the astronomer who had found Uranus a few years earlier. It is small, only about four hundred kilometres across, the smallest of Saturn's major moons. But it is just big enough for its own gravity to have pulled it into a round shape.

> i: saturn moons | v: saturn moons
It orbits close to Saturn, racing around the planet in less than a day, and like our own Moon, it always keeps the same face turned towards its planet. Its name comes from Greek mythology, where Mimas was one of the giants who fought against the gods. [pause] A fitting name for a small moon with a giant scar.

> i: mimas herschel crater | v: mimas
In 1980, Voyager 1 flew past and revealed its most famous feature. A crater about one hundred and thirty kilometres wide, almost a third of the moon's entire width. It was named {{Herschel|HERSCHEL CRATER}}, after the moon's discoverer. Its walls rise around five kilometres high, with a towering central peak.

> i: mimas | v: mimas
Whatever made that crater nearly destroyed Mimas. If the impact had been just a little stronger, the moon might have shattered completely. Fractures on the far side of Mimas may even have been caused by the shock of the impact travelling through it.

> i: death star | v: mimas
Three years before that first close-up, the film Star Wars had featured a planet-destroying space station with a giant dish on its side. The resemblance was so striking that Mimas has been called the Death Star moon ever since. [pause] Pure coincidence. But a perfect one.

## A Moon That Shaped The Rings
mood: wonder
> i: saturn rings cassini division | v: saturn rings
Mimas may be small, but it has a big influence. The largest gap in Saturn's rings, the dark band called the {{Cassini Division|CASSINI DIVISION}}, exists largely because of Mimas.

> i: saturn rings | v: saturn rings
Ring particles at the inner edge of the gap orbit Saturn exactly twice for every one orbit of Mimas. Each time they line up, Mimas gives them a tiny gravitational nudge, again and again, in the same place. Over time, those nudges clear the particles out. [pause] A moon four hundred kilometres wide, carving a gap four and a half thousand kilometres across.

> i: mimas pac-man | v: mimas
Cassini found another oddity. When it measured the temperature across Mimas's surface, the warmer and cooler regions formed a pattern that looked exactly like the video game character Pac-Man, with a wedge-shaped mouth. Scientists think energetic electrons trapped in Saturn's magnetic field bombard one part of the moon, hardening the ice so it holds heat differently. [pause] The Death Star, wearing a Pac-Man disguise.

## The Wobble
mood: mystery
> i: cassini spacecraft | v: cassini saturn
In 2014, scientists studying Cassini images noticed that Mimas wobbles slightly as it orbits Saturn, more than a solid ball of ice and rock should. There were two possible explanations. Either its rocky core was oddly elongated, like a rugby ball. Or there was liquid water inside, letting the outer shell slide.

> i: mimas | v: mimas
Most scientists favoured the first idea. An ocean inside Mimas seemed impossible. Its surface is ancient and covered in craters, showing no signs of the cracking and resurfacing seen on ocean worlds like Enceladus or Europa. [pause] Surely an ocean would leave some trace?

> i: mimas | v: saturn moons
Then, in 2024, a team led by astronomer {{Valéry Lainey|VALÉRY LAINEY}} analysed how Mimas's orbit itself slowly shifts over time, using years of precise Cassini measurements. A moon with a stretched-out core and a moon with a hidden ocean would change their orbits in different ways.

> i: mimas interior | v: mimas
Their result pointed clearly to an ocean. A global body of liquid water, beginning about twenty to thirty kilometres beneath the frozen surface. [pause] The Death Star had a heart of water.

## A Brand New Ocean
mood: wonder
> i: mimas interior | v: mimas
But how could such an obvious ocean leave no mark on the surface? The answer was the most surprising part of the discovery. The ocean is {{young|YOUNG}}. Their models suggest it formed only around five to fifteen million years ago.

> i: earth from space | v: earth
On the scale of the solar system, that is incredibly recent. When the ocean of Mimas began to form, the Earth already had grasslands, whales, and the ancestors of apes. [pause] Mimas's ocean is younger than many mountain ranges on our planet.

> i: saturn | v: saturn rings
The likely cause is a change in its orbit. Mimas's path around Saturn is slightly stretched, and the varying tides from the giant planet flex the moon and heat it. If that orbit became more stretched in the recent past, the extra heating could have started to melt the interior. [pause] The ocean may still be growing, and the ice above it thinning.

> i: mimas | v: mimas
That explains why the surface looks untouched. The melting has not had time to crack the crust or reshape the landscape. The evidence has simply not reached the surface yet. Scientists called it a stealth ocean world.

> i: spacecraft | v: spacecraft flight
Confirming it directly will take a new visit. A future spacecraft carrying ice-penetrating radar could look for the boundary between ice and water, and measure precisely how Mimas flexes as it orbits. [pause] Until then, the ocean is known only through the subtle way it moves a moon.

## What It Means
mood: wonder
> i: enceladus plumes | v: enceladus plumes
This discovery changes how we search for ocean worlds. Until now, scientists looked for clues on the surface. Geysers, cracks, fresh ice. Mimas shows that an ocean can exist with no visible sign at all.

> i: saturn moons | v: saturn moons
That means many more moons in the solar system, and across the galaxy, could be hiding oceans we have never suspected. Small, dead-looking icy moons might be quietly melting inside. [pause] The universe may have far more water than we thought.
