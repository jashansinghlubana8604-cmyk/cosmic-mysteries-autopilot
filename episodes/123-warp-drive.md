The universe has a speed limit. Nothing can travel through space faster than light. That is not an engineering problem. It is built into the structure of reality, confirmed by more than a century of experiments.

> i: milky way | v: milky way galaxy
And at that speed, the galaxy is enormous. The nearest star is more than four years away, even for light. The centre of the Milky Way, twenty six thousand years. If we are ever going to explore the stars in person, the speed of light looks like a wall.

> i: warp drive | v: warp drive
But there may be a loophole. Nothing can move through space faster than light. But what if space itself moved? [pause] What if you could shrink the space in front of you and stretch the space behind you, and ride a bubble of space across the galaxy?

> i: warp bubble | v: spacetime curvature
In 1994, a young physicist showed that Einstein's equations allow exactly that. It became known as the {{Alcubierre drive|ALCUBIERRE DRIVE}}. A real, mathematical warp drive. [pause] The question is whether anything in our universe could ever build one.

## Einstein's Speed Limit
mood: mystery
> i: albert einstein | v: spacetime curvature
Einstein's special relativity tells us that as an object speeds up, it takes more and more energy to accelerate it further. Reaching the speed of light would take infinite energy. So no spaceship, no particle with mass, can ever reach it.

> i: particle accelerator | v: particle accelerator
This has been tested countless times. At particle accelerators, protons are pushed to more than ninety nine point nine nine nine percent of light speed. They never reach it, no matter how much energy is used. [pause] The wall is real.

> i: voyager spacecraft | v: deep space flight
And our real spacecraft are far, far slower. Voyager 1, travelling at around sixty thousand kilometres per hour, would take roughly seventy five thousand years to cross the distance to the nearest star. [pause] At the speeds we can actually reach, the galaxy is not just big. It is unreachable.

> i: expanding universe | v: expanding universe
But general relativity, Einstein's theory of gravity, contains a surprise. It describes space and time as a flexible fabric that can stretch, bend and expand. And the speed limit applies to things moving through space, not to space itself. [pause] Distant galaxies are being carried away from us faster than light right now, by the expansion of the universe, without breaking any law.

## The Bubble
mood: wonder
> i: warp bubble | v: warp drive
In 1994, the Mexican physicist {{Miguel Alcubierre|MIGUEL ALCUBIERRE}}, then a student, was inspired by the warp drive in Star Trek. He asked whether Einstein's equations could allow something similar. And he found a solution. He later recalled that he was partly motivated by the simple wish to know whether the science fiction he loved could ever be real.

> i: warp bubble | v: spacetime curvature
Imagine a ship sitting inside a bubble of normal, flat space. In front of the bubble, space is squeezed together. Behind it, space is stretched out. The bubble surfs along on this wave of distorted space, carrying the ship with it. [pause] The ship itself never moves through the space around it. It is the space that moves.

> i: star field warp | v: star field
Inside the bubble, the crew would feel no acceleration. Clocks would tick normally, with no strange time dilation. And the bubble could, in principle, travel faster than light relative to the rest of the universe, without breaking the rule that nothing moves through space faster than light. [pause] On paper, it is a warp drive.

> i: spacetime curvature | v: spacetime curvature
The wall of the bubble, where space switches from squeezed to stretched, would be the hardest part to build. The distortion there would need to be extreme, concentrated in a thin shell around the ship. [pause] Everything about the drive depends on controlling the shape of space with incredible precision.

## The Catch
mood: dread
> i: wormhole | v: spacetime curvature
There is a problem, and it is a big one. To bend space in this way, Alcubierre's solution requires something called {{negative energy|NEGATIVE ENERGY}}. A kind of matter or energy that has less than nothing. It would push space apart rather than pull it together.

> i: casimir effect | v: physics laboratory
Negative energy densities do appear in small amounts in quantum physics, in effects like the Casimir effect, where two metal plates very close together feel a tiny push. But nobody knows how to gather large amounts of it, or whether that is even possible.

> i: galaxy cluster | v: galaxy cluster
And the amounts needed were staggering. The first estimates suggested that a warp bubble big enough for a ship would require negative energy equivalent to more than the mass of the entire observable universe. [pause] Later researchers found ways to reshape the bubble that greatly reduce the requirement, but it remains far beyond anything imaginable.

> i: sun | v: sun
To put it another way, even the reduced estimates call for amounts of exotic energy comparable to converting a large spacecraft, or more, entirely into negative energy, something no known physics can do. [pause] The universe gives us plenty of positive energy. Negative energy is a different story.

## More Problems
mood: dread
> i: warp bubble | v: warp drive
Even if you had the energy, other problems appear. A ship inside a faster-than-light bubble would be cut off from the front edge of its own bubble. Signals from the ship could not reach the region in front, because that would require sending them faster than light. [pause] You could not steer it, or switch it off, from inside.

> i: star field warp | v: deep space flight
Some calculations suggest that particles from space could pile up at the front of the bubble as it travels. When the ship stopped, that accumulated energy might be released in a powerful burst, potentially devastating whatever lay ahead. [pause] Arriving at another star might destroy the very place you were trying to visit.

> i: clock | v: time lapse
And faster-than-light travel creates paradoxes. In relativity, if you can travel faster than light, you can, in some situations, travel back in time. Many physicists suspect that the laws of nature have some hidden rule that prevents warp drives from ever working, just to avoid that kind of contradiction.

## Is There Any Hope?
mood: wonder
> i: physics laboratory | v: physics laboratory
In the years since Alcubierre's paper, physicists have kept exploring the idea. In 2021, a few studies proposed new kinds of warp bubbles that might not need negative energy at all, though they may only work at speeds slower than light. Others have questioned those results.

> i: laser | v: laser
A small team at NASA once ran experiments looking for tiny warping of space with sensitive laser instruments. They did not find convincing evidence of a warp effect. [pause] For now, warp drives remain on the blackboard, not in the laboratory.

> i: light sail | v: spacecraft flight
Meanwhile, more realistic ideas for reaching the stars are being studied. Tiny probes pushed by powerful lasers to a fifth of the speed of light. Ships carrying generations of people over centuries. [pause] Slow, but possible.

> i: milky way | v: milky way time-lapse
Is faster-than-light travel possible? Based on everything we know, almost certainly not in any practical way. But the Alcubierre drive shows that Einstein's universe is stranger and more flexible than it first appears. [pause] Space can stretch. Space can shrink. And for a moment, in the mathematics, the stars came within reach.
