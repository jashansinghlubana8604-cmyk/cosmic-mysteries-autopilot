## The Map With A Hole In It
mood: mystery
> i: galaxy survey map | v: galaxy distribution simulation
In 1981, a team of astronomers led by Robert Kirshner was doing something that sounds simple. They were measuring how far away galaxies are, in a few narrow strips of sky, to build a three dimensional map of the universe.

> v: redshift galaxies | i: spectrum galaxy
They did it with light. The further away a galaxy is, the more its light is stretched toward red by the expansion of space. Measure that stretch, and you know the distance.

> i: galaxy field | v: zoom galaxy cluster
Plot enough galaxies, and a pattern appears. Galaxies are not scattered evenly, like sand. They gather in clusters and long filaments, like a vast spider web, with emptier regions in between.

> v: cosmic web simulation | i: dark matter simulation
Kirshner's team expected to see some of that structure. What they found in the constellation Boötes was something else entirely. In every strip they measured, the galaxies stopped. [pause] Then, a very long way further out, they started again.

> i: empty space stars | v: star field slow
Between them was a gap so large that the team checked their data, and then checked it again. It was not a glitch. It was a hole, roughly spherical, and it was the largest empty region anyone had ever found.

> v: telescope observatory night | i: observatory dome night
Some astronomers suspected it was an illusion, a trick of looking through only a few thin slices of sky. So over the following years, they went back and surveyed the whole region, galaxy by galaxy. If the hole was a mistake, a full survey would fill it in. [pause] It did not. The more carefully they looked, the more certain it became that the emptiness was real.

## How Empty Is Empty?
mood: wonder
> v: milky way galaxy | i: milky way artist impression
To understand how strange this is, picture our own neighbourhood. The Milky Way sits in a small group of galaxies. Andromeda is two and a half million light years away. In every direction, there are galaxies, thousands of them, close enough to see with a modest telescope.

> i: spiral galaxy | v: spiral galaxy zoom
In a region the size of the Boötes Void, you would normally expect to find around {{two thousand galaxies|2,000 GALAXIES}}. Astronomers have spent decades searching it carefully.

> i: isolated galaxy | v: lonely galaxy
They have found about {{sixty|60}}. A thin tube of them runs through the middle, like a few lights scattered along a dark motorway. The rest is darkness.

> v: zoom isolated galaxy | i: dwarf galaxy
One astronomer, Greg Aldering, put it in a way that is hard to forget. If the Milky Way had been born in the middle of the Boötes Void, we would not have known there were other galaxies at all until the 1960s.

> i: night sky | v: night sky time-lapse
Imagine looking up from a planet like that. Your night sky would be full of stars from your own galaxy, but beyond them, nothing. No fuzzy patches, no Andromeda. Just your galaxy, alone, in an apparently empty universe. [pause] Your entire civilisation might grow up believing it was the only island in an endless sea.

## Too Big To Exist
mood: mystery
> v: early universe simulation | i: cosmic microwave background
The real problem is not that voids exist. Voids are expected. The problem is time.

> i: cosmic microwave background | v: big bang
When the universe was young, it was astonishingly smooth. The light left over from that era, the cosmic microwave background, shows a universe where density varied by only about one part in a hundred thousand.

> v: dark matter simulation | i: dark matter map
Over billions of years, gravity amplified those tiny differences. Slightly denser regions pulled in more matter and became clusters. Slightly emptier regions lost matter and became voids. It is a slow process, and our computer simulations show how far it should have gone by today.

> v: cosmic web simulation | i: galaxy filament
Those simulations produce plenty of voids. But a void as large and as empty as Boötes, in the time the universe has had, is very hard to make. It is like finding a desert in the middle of a rainforest that is older than the rainforest itself.

> v: dark matter simulation | i: galaxy cluster
And voids do not just sit there quietly. Because they contain so little matter, the space inside them expands slightly faster than average, and the galaxies around their edges are pulled outward, toward the denser walls. A void, left alone, keeps growing. Every year, the Great Nothing gets a little bigger.

> i: galaxy collision | v: galaxy merger
The most popular explanation is that Boötes is not one void, but several smaller ones that merged. As neighbouring empty regions grew, the thin walls of galaxies between them were pulled apart and drained away, leaving one enormous bubble. That tube of galaxies through the middle might be the last remains of those walls.

> v: zoom galaxy | i: deep field
It is an elegant idea, and it may well be right. But it still has to happen fast enough, and the Boötes Void sits right at the edge of what our models can comfortably explain.

## The Wilder Ideas
mood: dread
> i: dyson sphere | v: alien civilisation artist
Whenever something in space looks impossible, wilder ideas follow. And one has followed Boötes for years.

> v: galaxy fading | i: dark galaxy
In 1964, the astronomer Nikolai Kardashev suggested ranking civilisations by how much energy they use. The most advanced kind, a Type Three civilisation, would harness the power of an entire galaxy, perhaps by surrounding its stars with vast energy collectors.

> i: dyson sphere | v: star dimming
From far away, a galaxy like that would go dark in visible light. So some have asked, half seriously: what if the galaxies in Boötes are not missing at all? What if they are simply hidden?

> v: infrared galaxy | i: infrared galaxy
It is a thrilling thought. But astronomers can test it. Anything that captures a star's energy must release waste heat, and that heat glows in infrared light. Surveys have looked for galaxies glowing in the infrared like this, and have found no sign of galaxy-wide civilisations. [pause] The darkness of Boötes appears to be real darkness.

## We May Live In One
mood: wonder
> v: milky way galaxy | i: local group galaxies
And here is the twist. The Boötes Void is not the only great emptiness. In recent years, some astronomers have argued that we ourselves might be living inside a gigantic underdense region, sometimes called the {{KBC Void|THE LOCAL HOLE}}.

> i: galaxy survey | v: galaxy distribution
If that is true, it is far larger than Boötes, perhaps two billion light years across, with the Milky Way somewhere inside it. The idea is still debated. Other surveys do not find such a large hole, and many cosmologists are sceptical.

> v: expanding universe | i: hubble constant
But if it exists, it might explain one of the biggest arguments in modern cosmology. Measurements of how fast the universe is expanding do not agree with each other. Living inside a vast void could make the nearby universe seem to expand slightly faster than it really does.

> v: light travel galaxy | i: gravitational lens
Voids even change the light that crosses them. A photon that passes through a growing void comes out with slightly less energy than it went in with, because the void has spread out while it was travelling. Astronomers have seen hints of this effect in the cosmic microwave background, as faint cold patches lined up with some of the largest voids in the sky.

> i: deep field | v: zoom deep field
So these empty places are not just curiosities. They might be distorting our view of the entire cosmos.

> v: cosmic web simulation | i: cosmic web
The Boötes Void is still out there, seven hundred million light years away, a sphere of almost nothing in a universe built of something. Maybe it formed exactly the way our models say. Or maybe, in that great silence, the universe is trying to tell us that we have not understood how it was built. [pause] Not yet.
