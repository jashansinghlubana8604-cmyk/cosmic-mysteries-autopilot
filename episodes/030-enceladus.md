## A Ball Of Ice
mood: wonder
> i: saturn moons | v: saturn moons
William Herschel discovered Enceladus in 1789, through a telescope he had built himself. To him it was a pinprick of light next to Saturn. For almost two centuries, nobody could see anything more.

> i: enceladus voyager | v: voyager saturn
In 1981, Voyager 2 flew past and sent back the first real pictures. They were strange. Enceladus is only about {{five hundred kilometres|504 KM}} across, yet parts of its surface had almost no craters at all. Something had wiped them away, recently.

> i: enceladus surface | v: enceladus surface
And it was bright. Not just bright. Enceladus is one of the most reflective objects in the solar system, whiter than fresh snow. It bounces back almost all the sunlight that hits it.

> i: enceladus | v: ice moon
That should not happen to an old surface. Over billions of years, dust and radiation darken everything in space. [pause] Something was constantly repainting this moon with fresh white ice. But what?

## The Tiger Stripes
mood: mystery
> v: cassini saturn | i: cassini spacecraft
The answer came with Cassini, a spacecraft the size of a school bus that arrived at Saturn in 2004. Its mission planners had not expected much from Enceladus. Then its magnetometer noticed that Saturn's magnetic field was being bent around the little moon, as if something was puffing out from it.

> i: enceladus tiger stripes | v: enceladus south pole
So Cassini went closer. At the south pole, it found four enormous parallel cracks, each about one hundred and thirty kilometres long. They were warmer than anything around them. Scientists named them {{the tiger stripes|TIGER STRIPES}}.

> i: enceladus plumes | v: enceladus plumes
And from those stripes, jets of ice and vapour were blasting into space. Over a hundred of them. Every second, the moon was losing hundreds of kilograms of water. [pause] Not in a single eruption. Constantly.

> i: saturn e ring | v: saturn rings
Some of that ice falls back as snow, which is why the surface is so white. The rest escapes and spreads out into a huge, faint ring around Saturn, the {{E ring|THE E RING}}. A whole planetary ring, made by one tiny moon breathing out.

> i: enceladus south pole | v: enceladus south pole
Cassini's heat sensors measured how much warmth was leaking out of the stripes. Scientists estimated it at around fifteen billion watts. That is roughly the output of a dozen large power stations, pouring out of a moon you could drive across in an afternoon.

> i: enceladus plumes backlit | v: enceladus

## An Ocean Beneath The Ice
mood: mystery
> i: enceladus interior | v: enceladus interior
At first, scientists thought the water might come from small pockets under the south pole. Then Cassini measured how Enceladus wobbles as it orbits Saturn. The wobble was too big for a moon that was solid all the way through.

> i: enceladus ocean | v: ocean moon
There was only one explanation. The ice shell is not attached to the rock below. It floats. Beneath it lies a {{global ocean|A GLOBAL OCEAN}}, wrapping the entire moon, tens of kilometres deep.

> i: saturn | v: saturn
But how can an ocean stay liquid so far from the Sun, where the surface sits at about minus two hundred degrees? The answer is Saturn. As Enceladus swings around the planet, it is squeezed and stretched, again and again. That flexing turns into heat.

> i: dione | v: saturn moons
And another moon, Dione, keeps the orbit from settling down. For every one lap Dione makes, Enceladus makes exactly two. It is pushed in the same rhythm, forever, like a child on a swing. [pause] The squeezing never stops. And the ocean never freezes.

> i: enceladus interior | v: enceladus interior
The ice is not the same thickness everywhere. Across most of the moon it may be twenty kilometres or more. But under the south pole, models suggest it thins to just a few kilometres. That is where the cracks open, and where the ocean comes closest to space.

## Is Anything Swimming There
mood: mystery
> i: enceladus | v: enceladus
Let's be honest about what this means. Nobody has found life on Enceladus. Every chemical Cassini saw can be made without life. What we have found is a place where life could exist, and an ocean that is throwing samples of itself into space for free.

> v: deep ocean | i: deep sea
Imagine standing on the floor of that ocean. No sunlight has ever reached it. Above you, tens of kilometres of black water. Above that, an ice ceiling.
